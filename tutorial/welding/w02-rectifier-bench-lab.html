Welding Repair Track · W02

Rectifier testing: the first repair skill

Build a tiny battery-powered bridge, predict eight diode-test readings and learn what those measurements can—and cannot—prove about a welding rectifier.

Study + exercises 2–3 hoursLevel After P03; battery labContext India · family repair shop

Parts and purpose

You need four 1N4007 diodes (or a small documented bridge), a breadboard, a 9 V battery, a 1 kΩ, ¼ W resistor and a multimeter. For this lab there is no mains input and no smoothing capacitor. The resistor is the load and limits battery current to about 8 mA. Disconnect the battery before using diode or resistance mode.

A silicon diode’s band marks its cathode. In diode mode a conventional digital meter sources a small test current from its red lead to its black lead. Verify your meter’s manual. A forward reading is a voltage at that test current, not the diode’s resistance or its rated operating drop.

Build a bridge with four visible diodes

Label four nodes A (~1), B (~2), + and −. Wire these four branches; the arrow below indicates forward conventional current, not the band:

D1A+
D2B+
D3−A

  1. Check the unpowered wiring. Find the cathode band on all four diodes. Verify that only the nodes listed above are joined. Put the 1 kΩ resistor between + and −.

  2. First input polarity. Battery positive to A, negative to B. Set the meter to DC volts; red to +, black to −. Expect about 7–8 V for a fresh 9 V battery, depending on the battery and diode drops. The conducting path is A → D1 → load → D4 → B.

  3. Reverse the battery at A/B. Expect a similar positive load voltage. Now current follows B → D2 → load → D3 → A. The output polarity did not reverse.

  4. Disconnect the battery and remove the load. Take the eight diode readings in the next table. Only use diode mode on this disconnected circuit.

  5. Make one safe fault. Remove D1. Predict which battery polarity stops delivering load current, then reconnect the resistor and battery to check. Remove the battery again before repeating diode tests.

Why it works

Each input polarity selects two forward-biased junctions. The other two are reverse-biased. The diode’s depletion barrier and injected carriers explain the polarity preference; there is no fixed universal “0.7 V turn-on switch”. Read T11 or your earlier diode deep dive.

A stable near-zero result in both directions across one isolated junction strongly indicates a short. OL in both directions can mean an open junction, poor probe contact or incorrect pin identification. Two-diodes-in-series measurements, such as − to +, depend on the meter’s available test voltage and are less useful than testing the four individual junctions.

The output rectifier is a different part

Many welders use three-lead dual diodes, sometimes with the tab electrically connected to the common terminal. They may be common-cathode, common-anode or another arrangement. Read the exact part-number suffix and package drawing. Do not copy the pin order of a visually similar device.

For a common-cathode dual package, red to each anode and black to the common cathode gives two forward readings. Reverse each pair for two blocking readings. For a common-anode package, reverse that connection logic. In-circuit transformer windings and parallel rectifiers can disguise either failure.

An example to practise reading is ST’s STTH6003 datasheet: a dual high-frequency rectifier with specified recovery behaviour. This is an identification example, not a recommendation to fit it in your machine.

What this test does not prove

  • A normal forward/reverse test does not verify high-voltage blocking, hot leakage, full-current conduction or reverse recovery at switching speed.
  • A low in-circuit reading may belong to a different component. Isolate as the schematic and safe procedure require before condemning the rectifier.
  • A replacement needs voltage, current, surge, switching, thermal and mechanical compatibility. A bigger printed amp number is not enough.
You get 0.01 V in both directions on A to + while the bridge is still on a board. Is the bridge certainly shorted?

No. A parallel path can produce the reading. On a verified de-energised machine, a technician isolates the bridge and repeats it. A stable near-zero reading both ways on that isolated junction is strong evidence of a short.

Why can a 1,000 V general-purpose rectifier still be wrong at the welding output?

Voltage rating says nothing about how quickly it clears stored charge during commutation. A high-frequency output requires the specified diode technology, recovery, current and thermal characteristics.

Skills checklist

  • I have built the battery bridge and explained both current paths.
  • I have recorded all eight isolated-junction readings.
  • I can test a dual-diode package using its actual datasheet.
  • I can explain why a meter pass is only a screening result.
