Telecom cabling in Australia: ACMA rules and structured cabling

Why you need to be a registered cabler to run data cable in Australia, what the registration types and endorsements are, and how a building's structured cabling is laid out, labelled, protected and tested. This is the licence most security, BMS and IT installer jobs ask for first.

Read 55 minLevel Job-ready basicsPractice P19, P20Theory T09, T10

Why cabling is regulated

Telephone and data cabling in a building connects, directly or through a router, to the public telecommunications network. Badly installed cabling can harm the network, give an electric shock if it touches mains wiring, or carry a fault from one building into the carrier's equipment. So in Australia, cabling work on customer cabling is regulated under the Telecommunications Act 1997 by the ACMA (Australian Communications and Media Authority).

  • Customer cabling is the cabling on the customer's side of the network boundary: the point where the carrier's network ends. For example, for an NBN fibre service the boundary is at the user ports of the NBN connection box (NTD). For copper services in larger buildings it is often at the building's main distribution frame (MDF).
  • Everything on the customer side that is connected, or will be connected, to a telecommunications network counts. That includes phone cabling, and the Cat6 cabling for computers, Wi-Fi access points, IP cameras and BMS controllers on a network that connects to the internet.
  • Under the ACMA's Telecommunications Cabling Provider Rules (the CPRs), this work must be done by a registered cabler, or by someone working under the direct supervision of one.
  • The work must follow the Wiring Rules: the standard AS/CA S009 (Installation requirements for customer cabling), published by Communications Alliance.
In Australia

The ACMA rules apply nationally, unlike security licences, which are set by each state. Doing cabling work without being registered (or properly supervised) is an offence, and the ACMA can take enforcement action. The rules, the registration types and the current edition of AS/CA S009 change from time to time. Check the ACMA website and talk to an accredited registrar for the current requirements before you pay for a course.

Registration types and endorsements

You do not register directly with the ACMA. You register through an ACMA-accredited registrar (for example ACRS or BICSI; the ACMA lists the current registrars). You show evidence that you have completed the required training units, and you pay a fee. The registrar gives you a registration card with your number. You must renew it, and some registrars require ongoing training.

The TCA1 form

When a registered cabler finishes a job, they must give the customer a Telecommunications Cabling Advice form, called the TCA1 (or an equivalent document with the same information). It records who did the work, their registration number, the address, the type of work, and a statement that it complies with the Wiring Rules. Keep a copy. Many companies fill in a TCA1 electronically for every job.

On the job

"ACMA Open Registration" (often written as "ACMA open licence" or "cabling licence", although it is a registration, not a licence) appears in a very large share of Australian ads for data cablers, CCTV and security installers, AV installers and BMS technicians. Structured cabling endorsement is usually expected; fibre is a strong bonus. A White Card and a driver's licence are near-universal requirements too.

Structured cabling: how a building is wired

Structured cabling is a planned, standard layout. Every outlet is wired back to a patch panel in a comms room, in a star. Then any outlet can be connected to any service (data, phone, camera, BMS) just by moving a short patch cord. The Australian generic cabling standard is AS/NZS 3080, based on the international ISO/IEC 11801. Manufacturers' documents often use the American TIA-568 series too. The ideas are the same.

Doing it properly: labelling, separation, fire-stopping and earthing

Labelling

Every cable gets the same unique ID at both ends: on the outlet faceplate, on the cable near each termination, and on the patch panel port. A common scheme is level – panel – port, for example 2-B-17 for Level 2, patch panel B, port 17. Labels must be printed (not handwritten), durable and readable without removing anything. The Australian administration standard is AS/NZS 3085.1, and many sites also use the American TIA-606 scheme. Follow the client's or builder's scheme if they have one. The as-built records (floor plans with outlet IDs and the test results) are part of the handover.

Worked example. 30 runs with an average route of 60 m, 3 m of slack and a 10% allowance: each run is (60 + 3) × 1.1 = 69.3 m. The total is 30 × 69.3 ≈ 2079 m, which looks like 2079 / 305 = 6.8, so 7 boxes. But each 305 m box only gives 4 whole runs of 69.3 m (277 m), with 28 m left over. You cannot join horizontal cable, so you need 30 / 4 = 7.5, rounded up to 8 boxes. Ordering 7 would leave you two runs short on the last day.


    
Separation from power

    
Telecommunications cabling must be kept apart from electrical (mains) cabling. This is for safety: if mains insulation fails, it must not energise data cable that people touch at a desk. It also reduces interference. AS/CA S009 sets minimum separation distances, or requires a suitable barrier, for cables in walls, ceilings, conduits, trays and underground. A figure you will often hear for building interiors is 50 mm from low-voltage power cables unless there is a barrier, but the requirement depends on the situation, so learn the table in the current edition. Never share a conduit, pit or enclosure compartment with mains cables, and never use power cable fixings or penetrations for data.

    
Safety
In ceilings and risers you work close to live 230 V cabling and equipment you did not install. Treat all electrical cabling as live. Do not move, cut or handle mains cables to make room for yours: ask the site electrician. Check the asbestos register before drilling in older buildings, use the right ladder or EWP, and watch for ceiling insulation and heat in summer roof spaces.

Fire-stopping penetrations

    
Many walls and floors in commercial buildings are fire-rated: they are built to stop fire and smoke spreading for a set time. Every hole you drill through one for cables breaks that rating until it is sealed with a tested fire-stopping system (approved collars, sleeves, pillows, mastics or boards, installed as the system's test report says). The National Construction Code requires the rating to be kept. On most commercial sites the builder or fire engineer specifies the system, and every penetration is labelled and photographed for the records. Leave room in sleeves for future cables, and reseal after each addition.


    
Earthing of racks: the telecommunications earth

    
Metal racks, cable trays and screened cabling are bonded to an earth bar in the comms room. That bar connects back to the building's earthing system through a dedicated earth conductor. This protects people if a mains fault makes a rack live, gives surge currents a path to earth, and makes screened (F/UTP) cabling work properly. AS/CA S009 sets out how the telecommunications earth is designed and connected. The final connection to the electrical installation's main earth is electrician's work.

    
Common misconception
"The rack is plugged into a power outlet, so it is earthed." The equipment in the rack is earthed through its power cords, but the rack frame, doors and trays are not, unless they are bonded. Paint and powder coat insulate the panels from each other. Use the proper bonding kit (toothed washers or bonding straps) so every part of the rack is connected to the earth bar.


    
Testing and certification

    
A cheap continuity tester only checks the wiremap: that each of the 8 wires goes to the right pin. That is the minimum. A commercial job is certified with a certification tester (Fluke Networks testers are the brand most often named in Australian ads). The tester measures the permanent link at frequencies up to 250 MHz for Cat6 (500 MHz for Cat6A) and compares the results with the limits in the standard:

Save every result with the cable ID. The results go to the client and, for a manufacturer's system warranty (often 20–25 years), to the manufacturer. Those warranties usually require the installer to be trained and certified by the manufacturer and to use only that manufacturer's matched components. Fibre backbones are tested with a light source and power meter (loss) and often an OTDR. See C09.

    
Bench tip
Most failed links are made at the termination or during the pull. Keep the jacket on to within a few millimetres of the jack, keep the twists to the termination, do not exceed the cable's bend radius (about 4 × the cable diameter for UTP once installed) or pulling tension (about 110 N for 4-pair UTP), and never crush bundles with tight cable ties. Hook-and-loop (Velcro) straps are standard in racks and trays.

Brands you will see

    
Cabling systems: CommScope, Panduit, Molex, Clipsal (Schneider Electric) and others. Testers: Fluke Networks certifiers. Racks, cable managers, fibre trays and fire-stopping products come from many makers. You do not need to know them all; most employers standardise on one or two cabling systems.


    
On the job
A typical data-cabling day: read the outlet plan, set up cable boxes, pull a bundle through the ceiling or cable tray (one person feeding, one pulling), leave slack, dress the cables into the rack, terminate patch panels and outlets, label everything, then certify-test at the end of the day and fix the fails. Neat, labelled work with clean test reports is what gets you kept on.

Check yourself

    
Is installing Cat6 for IP cameras on an office network "customer cabling" under the ACMA rules?
Yes, if the network is connected (or will be connected) to a telecommunications network, which almost every office network is through its internet connection. So the work must be done by a registered cabler or under direct supervision of one, following AS/CA S009.

    
What is the difference between open and restricted registration?
Open registration covers all types of cabling work in any premises (within your endorsements). Restricted covers only limited simple work, mainly in residential and small business premises. Commercial employers almost always need open registration.

    
Why must the same label appear on the outlet, the cable and the patch panel port?
So anyone can trace a fault or move a service without opening ceilings or toning cables. A user says "outlet 2-B-17 is dead"; the tech goes straight to panel B, port 17 on Level 2, checks the patch cord and the switch port, and tests the link.

    
40 runs with an average route of 45 m, 2 m slack and 10% allowance. How many 305 m boxes?
Each run = (45 + 2) × 1.1 = 51.7 m. Whole runs per box = floor(305 / 51.7) = 5. Boxes = 40 / 5 = 8. (Total metres would suggest 40 × 51.7 = 2068 m / 305 = 6.8, so 7. That is one box short.)

    
Why does a hole through a fire-rated wall need a tested fire-stopping system, not just any sealant?
The wall's fire rating was proven by a test. A penetration must be sealed with a system that has itself been tested in that type of wall with those cables, so the wall still resists fire and smoke for the rated time. Ordinary sealant or foam may burn or shrink and let fire and smoke through.

    
What does a certification tester check that a cheap continuity tester does not?
Performance at high frequency: insertion loss, NEXT, return loss, delay and skew, and length, against the Cat6 limits. A cheap tester checks only the wiremap. A link can pass wiremap but fail NEXT because of too much untwisting, and then it may not run gigabit reliably.

    
Why bond a rack to the telecommunications earth if the equipment already has earthed power cords?
The power cords earth the equipment, not the rack frame, doors, side panels or cable trays, which are insulated from each other by paint. Bonding makes every metal part safe to touch if a mains fault occurs, gives surges a low-impedance path, and is needed for screened cabling to work as designed.
