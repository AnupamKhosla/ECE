Circuit laws: Kirchhoff, Thevenin and superposition

Two bookkeeping rules, one for charge and one for energy, are enough to solve any DC circuit. Add Thevenin's idea and you can replace a whole circuit with one source and one resistor, which explains sensor loading, battery sag and why industry sends signals as current.

How we found out

Ohm's law (T02) handles one loop. Real circuits, even the telegraph networks of the 1840s, had branches and several batteries. In 1845 Gustav Kirchhoff, then a 21-year-old student at the University of Königsberg in Prussia, published two simple rules that solve any such network. He later became famous for something else entirely (discovering that each element has its own spectral lines), but every electronics student still learns his two circuit laws first.

In 1853 Hermann von Helmholtz, while studying currents in animal tissue, noticed that any network of batteries and resistors behaves, at two chosen terminals, exactly like a single battery with a single resistor. The French telegraph engineer Léon Charles Thévenin rediscovered and published the same idea in 1883, and his name stuck. In 1926 Edward Norton at Bell Labs in America and Hans Ferdinand Mayer at Siemens in Germany independently described the "current source" version. Underneath all of these is linearity: in circuits made of resistors and ideal sources, doubling every source doubles every current. That property also gives us the superposition principle.

Words first: nodes, branches, loops

  • A node is a point where two or more parts connect. All the wire joined together without any part in between counts as one node, even if it is drawn spread out.
  • A branch is a single path between two nodes, containing one or more parts in series. The same current flows everywhere along a branch.
  • A loop is any closed path through the circuit that returns to its starting node. A mesh is a loop with no other loop inside it, like a window pane.
  • A reference node (ground, 0 V, common) is the node we choose to call 0 V. Every other node voltage is measured from it.

Kirchhoff's current law: charge is conserved

Σ Iin = Σ Iout (at every node) Or: the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero, counting currents in as positive and currents out as negative.

Why it is true: charge cannot be created or destroyed, and in a steady circuit it cannot pile up at a node either. A node is a bit of metal; if extra charge collected there, its potential would shoot up within nanoseconds and push the charge away. (T01 showed how strongly matter resists becoming charged.) So whatever flows in each second must flow out each second.

Common misconception

"Current takes the path of least resistance." Current takes every available path, each in inverse proportion to its resistance. Put 1 kΩ and 9 kΩ in parallel across 9 V: 9 mA goes through the 1 kΩ and 1 mA goes through the 9 kΩ. The low-resistance path takes most of the current, but not all. This matters for safety: a body in parallel with a "better" path still carries some current.

Kirchhoff's voltage law: energy is conserved

Σ V = 0 (around any closed loop) Add the voltage rises and drops around any loop, taking care with signs, and the total is zero.

Why it is true: voltage is the energy per coulomb gained or lost between two points (T01). If you carry a test charge round a loop and back to where you started, it must end with the same energy, or you could circle forever and create energy from nothing. In physics language, the electrostatic field is conservative: the work it does round any closed path is zero. It is like walking around a hill: however you go, when you return to the car park you have climbed exactly as much as you descended.

Bench tip

KVL is how you find faults with a meter. In a working series circuit, the voltages across the parts add up to the supply. If one part has almost the whole supply voltage across it and the others have none, that part is open-circuit (a broken wire, blown fuse, open switch or failed part). The full voltage always appears across the break.

Go deeper (optional): when KVL needs care

KVL comes from the electrostatic field being conservative. If a changing magnetic field passes through the loop, Faraday's law adds an induced voltage, and the "sum of voltages" round the loop is no longer zero in the simple sense. Circuit theory handles this by putting all the changing magnetic flux inside the inductors and transformers and treating their induced voltage as an element voltage (T06). At very high frequencies, or with long cables, the wires themselves have significant inductance and capacitance, and you must include those too. For DC and ordinary low-frequency circuits, KVL and KCL as stated are exact enough.

Sign conventions that stop mistakes

Most errors in circuit sums are sign errors. Use one fixed method:

  1. Guess a direction for each unknown current and draw an arrow. The guess does not need to be right. If the answer comes out negative, the real current flows the other way, with that size.
  2. Mark the voltage across each resistor with + where the current arrow enters. Current flows "downhill" through a resistor, so the end it enters is the higher-potential end. This is the passive sign convention.
  3. Power. With the passive sign convention, P = VI is the power absorbed by a part. If it comes out negative, the part is delivering power (it is acting as a source). For a battery: current coming out of its + terminal means it is delivering energy; current being pushed into its + terminal means it is being charged.
  4. Walking a loop. Going from − to + across any part is a rise (+); going from + to − is a drop (−).

Series and parallel, derived

Series: same current

Parts in one branch carry the same current I (KCL: nothing can leave along the way). KVL round the loop: V = IR₁ + IR₂ + … = I(R₁ + R₂ + …). So the series combination behaves as one resistor:

Rseries = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …

The voltage divider follows straight away. With Vin across R₁ and R₂ in series, I = Vin/(R₁ + R₂), so the voltage across R₂ is

Vout = Vin × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) Example: 12 V with R₁ = 10 kΩ and R₂ = 4.7 kΩ gives 12 × 4.7/14.7 = 3.84 V. Dividers are how sensors such as thermistors and light-dependent resistors turn a resistance change into a voltage (P12).

Parallel: same voltage

Parts connected between the same two nodes have the same voltage V across them (KVL: there is only one potential difference between two nodes). Each carries Ik = V/Rk, and KCL says the total is the sum:

I = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + … ⟹ 1/Rparallel = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
For two resistors: Rp = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) It is often easier to think in conductance G = 1/R (in siemens, S): conductances in parallel simply add. The parallel result is always smaller than the smallest resistor.

The current divider: two resistors in parallel share a total current I in inverse proportion to their resistance. I₁ = I × R₂/(R₁ + R₂). Note R₂ on top: the other resistor.

Common misconception

"Adding a load in parallel doesn't affect the others." True only for an ideal source with zero internal resistance. A real supply sags when you add load (V = ℰ − Ir), so every load on it sees a little less voltage. Turn on a big motor in a car and the headlights dim. Thevenin's theorem below makes this exact.

Worked example: nodal analysis, step by step

Here is a circuit that series and parallel rules cannot solve directly, because it has two sources pushing against each other.

  1. Choose the reference node. The bottom wire is 0 V. Then the + terminal of V₁ is at 12 V and the + terminal of V₂ is at 5 V. The only unknown is the voltage at node A, call it VA.

  2. Write each branch current in terms of VA, using Ohm's law. Current flows from high to low potential through a resistor:
    I₁ = (12 − VA) / 1 kΩ (into A)
    I₂ = (5 − VA) / 2 kΩ (into A)
    I₃ = VA / 3 kΩ (out of A)

  3. Apply KCL at A: in = out, so I₁ + I₂ = I₃:
    (12 − VA)/1 + (5 − VA)/2 = VA/3 (currents in mA, resistances in kΩ)

  4. Clear the fractions. Multiply every term by 6:
    6(12 − VA) + 3(5 − VA) = 2VA
    72 − 6VA + 15 − 3VA = 2VA
    87 = 11VA ⟹ VA = 7.909 V

  5. Find the currents.
    I₁ = (12 − 7.909)/1 = 4.091 mA
    I₂ = (5 − 7.909)/2 = −1.455 mA
    I₃ = 7.909/3 = 2.636 mA
    I₂ is negative, so the real current flows the other way: 1.455 mA flows from A into the + terminal of the 5 V source. The 12 V source is charging the 5 V one.

  6. Check with KCL: in = 4.091, out = 2.636 + 1.455 = 4.091 mA. ✓

  7. Check with energy. V₁ delivers 12 × 4.091 = 49.09 mW. V₂ absorbs 5 × 1.455 = 7.27 mW. The resistors absorb 4.091² × 1 + 1.455² × 2 + 2.636² × 3 = 16.74 + 4.23 + 20.85 = 41.82 mW. Total absorbed = 7.27 + 41.82 = 49.09 mW = total delivered. ✓

That is nodal analysis: pick a reference, name the unknown node voltages, write KCL at each unknown node using Ohm's law, and solve. A circuit with N unknown nodes gives N simultaneous equations. Circuit simulators such as SPICE do exactly this, with matrices, for thousands of nodes.

Go deeper (optional): the same circuit by mesh analysis

Mesh analysis uses KVL instead. Let Ia circulate clockwise in the left window and Ib clockwise in the right window. R₃ is shared, so it carries Ia − Ib downwards.

Left mesh (up through V₁, right through R₁, down through R₃): 12 − 1·Ia − 3(Ia − Ib) = 0, so 4Ia − 3Ib = 12.

Right mesh (right through R₂, down through V₂ from + to −, back, up through R₃): −2Ib − 5 − 3(Ib − Ia) = 0, so −3Ia + 5Ib = −5.

Solving: Ib = 16/11 = 1.455 mA and Ia = 4.091 mA. Then I₃ = Ia − Ib = 2.636 mA. Same answers. Use whichever method gives fewer equations: nodal analysis is usually easier when sources connect to ground.

Superposition

In a linear circuit (resistors and ideal sources only), each source's effect simply adds to the others. To use this:

  1. Keep one source and turn all the others off. A voltage source turned off becomes a short circuit (0 V across it). A current source turned off becomes an open circuit (0 A through it).
  2. Solve the simple circuit.
  3. Repeat for each source, then add the results with their signs.

On our example: with only V₁ (V₂ shorted), R₂ ∥ R₃ = 2 × 3/5 = 1.2 kΩ, so VA′ = 12 × 1.2/(1 + 1.2) = 6.545 V. With only V₂ (V₁ shorted), R₁ ∥ R₃ = 0.75 kΩ, so VA″ = 5 × 0.75/(2 + 0.75) = 1.364 V. Total: 6.545 + 1.364 = 7.909 V, the same as before.

Superposition is more than a trick for sums. It is how engineers think about a signal riding on a DC level: work out the DC bias with the signal turned off, then the signal with the DC turned off, and add. It is why an audio amplifier, a sensor input and a radio receiver can be analysed in parts.

Common misconception

"Superposition works for power too." It does not. Power goes as I² or V², and (a + b)² ≠ a² + b². Find the total current or voltage by superposition first, then calculate power from the total. Superposition also fails for non-linear parts such as diodes and LEDs.

Thevenin and Norton equivalents

Thevenin's theorem: any linear circuit, seen from two terminals, behaves exactly like one voltage source Vth in series with one resistor Rth. Norton's theorem: it also behaves like one current source IN in parallel with one resistor RN. The two are linked by RN = Rth and IN = Vth/Rth.

How to find Vth and Rth

  • Vth is the open-circuit voltage between the terminals, with nothing connected.
  • Rth is the resistance between the terminals with every source turned off (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened). Or equivalently Rth = Vth / Isc, where Isc is the short-circuit current.

Worked example: our circuit, seen by R₃

Remove R₃ and look into the terminals at node A and ground.

  1. Open-circuit voltage. With R₃ gone, a single current flows from V₁ through R₁ and R₂ into V₂: I = (12 − 5)/(1 + 2) = 2.333 mA. Then VA = 12 − 2.333 × 1 = 9.667 V = Vth.

  2. Resistance. Short both sources. Looking in from A, R₁ and R₂ are in parallel to ground: Rth = 1 × 2/3 = 0.667 kΩ.

  3. Reconnect the load. R₃ = 3 kΩ on a 9.667 V source with 0.667 kΩ in series: VA = 9.667 × 3/(3 + 0.667) = 7.909 V. Same answer a third time.

  4. Norton. IN = 9.667/0.667 = 14.5 mA. Check by shorting A to ground: 12/1 + 5/2 = 12 + 2.5 = 14.5 mA. ✓

The power of this is that once you have Vth and Rth, you can try any load instantly. With R₃ = 0.667 kΩ (matched), the load gets maximum power (T03). With R₃ = 10 kΩ, VA = 9.667 × 10/10.667 = 9.06 V.

Why Thevenin matters on the job: loading

Every sensor output, battery, divider and signal source has a Thevenin resistance, usually called its output impedance or source impedance. Every meter, input pin and cable that you connect has an input impedance that acts as a load. The voltage you actually measure is

Vmeasured = Vth × Rin / (Rin + Rth) If Rin is much larger than Rth (say 100 times or more), the error is small. If they are similar, the measurement is badly wrong.

Example: a 10 kΩ NTC thermistor and a 10 kΩ fixed resistor form a divider on 5 V. At 25 °C the output is 2.5 V and its Thevenin resistance is 10 ∥ 10 = 5 kΩ.

That is why some microcontroller datasheets (the classic Arduino Uno chip among them) ask for a source impedance of about 10 kΩ or less (P12).

On the job

Industrial and BMS transmitters often send their reading as a current between 4 and 20 mA instead of a voltage. A 4–20 mA transmitter behaves like a Norton source with a very large parallel resistance: it forces the chosen current through the loop whatever the wire resistance, as long as the loop supply has enough voltage left over. So 200 m of thin cable, or a few ohms of corroded terminal, does not change the reading, where a 0–10 V signal would sag. See T16 and C10.

Try this

Measure a Thevenin equivalent without opening the box. Measure the open-circuit voltage Voc with a meter (that is Vth). Then connect a known load resistor RL and measure VL. Then Rth = RL(Voc/VL − 1). With our example, Voc = 9.667 V and VL = 7.909 V across 3 kΩ give Rth = 3 × (1.2223 − 1) = 0.667 kΩ. Do not measure Rth by shorting a battery or power supply: the short-circuit current can be dangerous.

Check yourself

Three wires meet at a node. 250 mA flows in on one and 100 mA flows out on another. What is the third current?

KCL: in = out. 250 = 100 + I₃, so I₃ = 150 mA flowing out.

A 24 V supply feeds three resistors in series. Two drop 8 V and 10 V. What does the third drop, and what law did you use?

KVL: 24 − 8 − 10 − V₃ = 0, so V₃ = 6 V. Energy per coulomb gained in the supply equals energy lost in the parts.

What is 4.7 kΩ in parallel with 4.7 kΩ? And 1 kΩ in parallel with 100 kΩ?

Two equal resistors in parallel: half, 2.35 kΩ. 1 kΩ ∥ 100 kΩ = 100/101 kΩ ≈ 0.990 kΩ: the parallel combination is always slightly less than the smallest, and a much bigger resistor in parallel hardly changes it.

In the worked example, I₂ came out as −1.455 mA. What does the minus sign mean physically?

The guessed direction (from V₂ into node A) was wrong. 1.455 mA actually flows from A into the + terminal of the 5 V source. That source is absorbing 7.27 mW: it is being charged by the 12 V source.

When using superposition, what do you replace a turned-off voltage source with, and why?

A short circuit (a plain wire). An ideal voltage source set to 0 V keeps 0 V across itself whatever current flows, which is exactly what a wire does. A turned-off current source becomes an open circuit, because 0 A must flow through it.

A sensor has an open-circuit output of 3.0 V and a Thevenin resistance of 47 kΩ. What will a 100 kΩ input read?

3.0 × 100/(100 + 47) = 2.04 V, a 32% error. The input needs to be at least 100 × 47 kΩ ≈ 4.7 MΩ for an error under 1%, or the sensor needs a buffer (an op-amp follower, T14).

A battery reads 12.6 V open circuit and 12.0 V with a 2 Ω load. Find its Thevenin equivalent.

Vth = 12.6 V. Rth = RL(Voc/VL − 1) = 2 × (12.6/12.0 − 1) = 2 × 0.05 = 0.1 Ω. This is the same as the internal resistance from T01: a battery's model is a Thevenin equivalent.

Why can a 4–20 mA signal travel hundreds of metres without error while a 0–10 V signal over the same cable might read low?

The 4–20 mA transmitter acts as a current source with a very high Norton resistance, so the series wire resistance does not change the current (KCL: the same current flows everywhere in the loop). A 0–10 V signal is read across an input resistance; any current through the wire's resistance drops some voltage before the input, and leakage or ground differences add errors.
