Robot trajectories, acceleration and synchronized axes

A path tells you which poses to pass through. A trajectory adds the time at each pose, which sets speed, acceleration and load. Calculate a move duration and check what happens when two joints follow the same timing.

Find out whether an axis can reach top speed

For a rest-to-rest move of length D, use equal acceleration and braking magnitude a and speed limit V. Each ramp covers V²/(2a) if the axis reaches V. For D ≥ V²/a, accelerate for V/a, cruise for (D − V²/a)/V and brake for V/a. For a shorter move the two ramps meet at a lower peak speed √(Da). The time is 2√(D/a). A negative move uses the same duration with the direction reversed.

For D = 240 mm, V = 120 mm/s and a = 240 mm/s², the ramps use 60 mm in total. The axis accelerates for 0.50 s, cruises for 1.50 s and brakes for 0.50 s. At D = 30 mm it peaks at 84.9 mm/s and takes 0.707 s. A short move can take longer than distance divided by the speed limit because the ramps dominate. Northwestern · Timing profiles video.

Make two joints follow the same progress

Let a shared progress variable s run from 0 to 1. For each joint, use q = qstart + Δq × s. If two joints travel +90° and −30°, at s = 0.5 they have moved +45° and −15°. Each nonzero travel imposes a limit on the common profile. Divide the joint’s positive speed limit by |Δq| to bound |ds/dt|. Divide its acceleration and jerk limits by |Δq| to bound the corresponding derivatives. Use the tightest limit from any moving joint for each derivative. A stationary joint stays at its initial position.

With joint 1 limited to 60°/s and 120°/s², and joint 2 to 90°/s and 180°/s², the shared limits for this pair are 0.667 s⁻¹ and 1.333 s⁻². The normalized move takes 2.00 s with trapezoidal timing. Joint 2 on its own could stop after 0.816 s. Running both independent moves from time zero reaches the same endpoint but follows a different path between endpoints. Check tool and link clearances along whichever path will actually run.

For a curved joint path q(s), acceleration also includes a term from the curvature of q with respect to s. A Cartesian straight line can require very different joint speeds along its length, especially near a kinematic singularity. Northwestern · Separating path and time scaling.

Calculate a smooth profile within the real limits

Jerk is the time derivative of acceleration. Constant-acceleration ramps have step changes in acceleration at their boundaries. A ramp with bounded jerk spreads the load change across time. One simple smooth progress profile uses normalized time u = t/T and s = 10u³ − 15u⁴ + 6u⁵. It starts and ends at rest with zero acceleration. Its largest normalized speed, acceleration magnitude and jerk magnitude are 1.875, 10√3/3 and 60. Differentiating with respect to real time divides the three values by T, T² and T³ respectively.

Use the largest of the three resulting duration bounds. For a 90° joint limited to 60°/s, 120°/s² and 480°/s³, the bounds are about 2.81, 2.08 and 2.24 s. Speed determines the duration for this quintic profile. The profile may help reduce vibration, and a measured settling test establishes the effect on cycle time. Other smooth profiles such as segmented S-curves can use the limits differently. Northwestern · Polynomial and jerk-limited timing.

Check what the controller really interpolates

Sending waypoints with only positions can produce different derivatives from the plan you calculated. In the Jazzy ROS 2 joint trajectory controller’s spline mode, positions use linear interpolation, adding consistent waypoint velocities allows cubic interpolation, and including accelerations allows quintic interpolation. Check time-from-start, units, joint order, start state, limits and controller configuration. With inconsistent waypoint derivatives, a spline can overshoot. Compare commanded and measured traces through the transition, then record arrival, settling and the separate proof that tooling is ready. ROS 2 Control · Trajectory representation and interpolation.
