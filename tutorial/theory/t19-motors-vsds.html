Motors and variable speed drives

Fans, pumps, compressors, lift doors and camera heads all move because a magnetic field pushes on a current. This chapter builds the motor from that one force, explains why the three-phase induction motor runs just below a speed set by the supply frequency, shows what a variable speed drive (VSD) does to change that speed, proves with the fan affinity laws why slowing a fan by 20 % cuts its power by half, and ends with how a BMS controller starts, speeds up and watches a VSD through its low-voltage control terminals.

Safety first

Motor terminals and VSD power terminals carry mains or three-phase voltage (230 V single-phase, 400 V three-phase). A VSD also holds a dangerous charge on its DC bus (about 540–565 V DC) for minutes after it is switched off and isolated. In Australia only a licensed electrician may open, wire, test or repair the power side of a motor or drive. A BMS technician works only on the control-terminal side, which is extra-low voltage (ELV). Everything you build in this course stays at ELV: batteries, 5 V, 12 V and 24 V DC.

Force on a current in a magnetic field

Chapter T06 showed that a current makes a magnetic field and that a changing field makes a voltage. The motor uses the other half of the story: a magnetic field pushes sideways on a wire that carries current. This is the same push that deflected the charges in the Hall sensor (T18); here we add up the push on all the moving charges in a length of wire.

F = B · I · L F = force in newtons (N). B = magnetic flux density in tesla (T). I = current in amperes. L = length of wire inside the field, in metres. This holds when the wire is at right angles to the field. Worked: a magnet gives B = 0.5 T across a 0.1 m wire carrying 10 A: F = 0.5 × 10 × 0.1 = 0.5 N, about the weight of a 50 g mass.

The direction of the force is at right angles to both the field and the current. A quick memory aid is Fleming's left-hand rule (for motors): point the first finger along the field (N to S), the second finger along the current, and the thumb shows the force and motion. Reverse the current and the force reverses. Reverse the field and the force reverses too.

For a coil of width w and length L, the two sides each feel F = BIL at a lever arm of w/2 from the axle, so the torque (turning force) is 2 × (w/2) × BIL = B·I·(wL) = B·I·A, where A is the coil area. With N turns it is N·B·I·A. Torque is proportional to current. Remember this: it explains most of what follows.

Brushed DC motors: back-EMF and why stall current is huge

A brushed DC motor has a rotating coil (the armature) in a fixed magnetic field made by permanent magnets. Two carbon brushes press against a split ring (the commutator) and feed current to the coil. As the coil passes the vertical, the commutator swaps the connections, so the current in each side reverses just in time. The torque always pushes the same way.

Here is the key idea. A coil moving through a magnetic field is also what a generator is (T07). Spinning the armature makes a voltage in it, and by Lenz's law (T06) that voltage opposes the supply. It is called the back-EMF, E, and it is proportional to speed:

E = k · ω I = (V − E) / R torque = k · I V = supply voltage, R = armature winding resistance, ω = angular speed in rad/s (rpm × 2π/60), and k = the motor constant in V·s/rad, which is also N·m/A. The same k links back-EMF to speed and torque to current.

Worked example: a 12 V motor with R = 1.2 Ω and k = 0.036

  • Why stall current is high. At standstill there is no back-EMF to fight the supply, so the only limit is the winding resistance: 12 V / 1.2 Ω = 10 A. As the motor speeds up, E rises and the current falls. When it is stalled, all that power turns into heat: I²R = 10² × 1.2 = 120 W in a small motor, compared with 0.4 W when running free. A jammed motor burns out in seconds to minutes. This is the same reason a mains fan motor that cannot turn gets very hot, and why starting currents are large.
  • Speed is proportional to voltage. Rearrange: ω = (V − I·R)/k. With little load the I·R term is small, so speed ≈ V/k. Half the voltage gives roughly half the speed. That is how PWM (P09, P13) controls small DC motors: the average voltage sets the speed.
  • Speed falls as load rises. More load needs more torque, so more current, so a bigger I·R drop and lower speed (table above: 3000 rpm to 2230 rpm).
  • Reversing and flyback. Reverse the supply and the motor reverses. Because the coil is an inductor, switching it off makes a voltage spike, so drivers have flyback diodes (T06, P08, P13).
Bench tip

Spin a small DC motor by hand with its leads across a multimeter set to millivolts or volts: it makes a voltage that rises with speed. That is the back-EMF, and it shows a motor is also a generator.

On the job

You will meet small DC motors inside door closers, damper and valve actuators (with gearboxes), and in security gear such as PTZ camera heads and turnstiles. When an actuator "stalls" at the end of its travel, the controller or limit switch must cut the current; a good actuator has built-in limit or torque switches.

Stepper motors

A stepper motor does not spin freely. It moves in small fixed angles, one per pulse. It has coils on the stator and a toothed or magnetised rotor. A controller energises the coils in a sequence, and the rotor snaps to line up with each new magnetic field direction, one step at a time.

  • Steps per revolution = 360° / step angle. The common 1.8° motor has 360 / 1.8 = 200 steps per revolution. A 0.9° motor has 400. With microstepping the driver splits each step into smaller current steps, for example 1/16, giving 3200 steps per revolution for a 1.8° motor.
  • Speed is set by the step rate: rpm = (steps per second ÷ steps per revolution) × 60. At 1000 steps/s on a 200-step motor: 1000 / 200 = 5 rev/s = 300 rpm.
  • Holding torque is the torque the motor can resist when its coils are energised and it is standing still. It is the headline figure on a datasheet (in N·m). Torque falls as speed rises, so a stepper that holds well can stall at high speed.
  • Open loop. A stepper counts pulses and assumes the rotor followed. If the load is too much, it silently loses steps and the controller never knows. That is why steppers are used where the load is predictable (3D printers, small actuators, the 28BYJ-48 in lab P13 with 2048 steps per output turn).
  • It draws current even when standing still to keep holding, so it gets warm.

BLDC and EC fans

A brushless DC (BLDC) motor turns the brushed motor inside out. The permanent magnets sit on the rotor, and the coils are fixed in the stator, so there is no commutator or brush. But then something else must switch the current in the coils at the right moment: an electronic circuit. It needs to know where the rotor is, usually from three Hall sensors (T18) that sense the rotor magnet. The circuit energises the three coils in a rotating sequence that drags the rotor around. Some designs skip the Hall sensors and sense the back-EMF instead.

In building services a BLDC fan with its electronics built in is called an EC fan (electronically commutated). It is increasingly common in new AHUs, fan coil units, EC condenser fans and computer-room cooling. It connects to mains, rectifies it, and drives the motor with its own inverter, so it behaves like a motor and a VSD in one housing. Benefits over an older AC induction fan: no rotor current losses (permanent magnets), good efficiency across the whole speed range, no belt, and easy speed control.

The three-phase induction motor

Most of the motor power in a commercial building is in three-phase induction motors: chiller compressors, pumps, older AHU fans, cooling-tower fans. They are simple, rugged and cheap. They have no brushes and no magnets. The trick is that the stator makes a magnetic field that rotates.

The rotating field

Three-phase supply (T09) gives three AC currents of the same frequency, 120° apart in time. Place three coils in the stator 120° apart in space and feed one phase to each. The field of each coil points along its own axis and pulses in size with its current. At any instant the three fields add up (as vectors) to one field of constant size, whose direction moves round the circle once per cycle. At 50 Hz it makes 50 turns per second. No part moves; the pattern of current does.

Why the rotor follows, and why it must lag

Inside the stator sits the rotor, usually a "squirrel cage": aluminium or copper bars joined at both ends by rings. It has no wires to the outside. The rotating field sweeps past the bars. By Faraday's law (T06), a changing flux through a loop induces a voltage, and the bars are part of shorted loops, so current flows. The field then pushes on those currents with F = BIL, and the force drags the rotor in the direction of the field. By Lenz's law, the rotor tries to reduce the relative motion between itself and the field, so it follows it.

But if the rotor ever reached the field's speed, the bars would see no change in flux, there would be no induced voltage, no current and no force. So the rotor always runs a little slower than the field. The field's speed is the synchronous speed, ns, and the shortfall is the slip.

ns = 120 · f / p slip s = (ns − n) / ns rotor frequency fr = s · f f = supply frequency in Hz. p = number of poles (always even: 2, 4, 6 …). n = actual rotor speed in rpm. Why 120? One pole pair makes one field turn per cycle, so ns = 60 f / (p/2) = 120 f / p. Worked: 4-pole at 50 Hz: ns = 120 × 50 / 4 = 1500 rpm. Actual speed on the nameplate is 1440 rpm, so slip = (1500 − 1440)/1500 = 4 %, and rotor frequency = 0.04 × 50 = 2 Hz.

Torque against speed

The torque the motor makes depends on slip. At very small slip, torque grows roughly in proportion to slip. It reaches a peak (the breakdown torque) and then falls toward standstill, though the starting torque is still strong.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A 4 kW motor draws 4 kW." Right: 4 kW is the mechanical power at the shaft. The electrical input is higher, about 4.6 kW here, because of losses, and the current is set by apparent power: 8.1 A at 400 V, not 4000/400/√3 = 5.8 A. And a motor that is lightly loaded draws much less than its nameplate. The plate shows the rating, not the actual load.

Starting a motor: DOL, star-delta, soft starter and VSD

When a stationary induction motor is switched on, the rotor sees the field sweeping past at full speed (slip = 100 %), so it induces large rotor currents, and the stator draws a large starting current. Connecting the motor straight to the supply, called direct-on-line (DOL), typically draws about 6–8 times the full-load current for a few seconds (indicative; check the motor data). For our 4 kW motor with FLC 8.1 A, that is about 57 A. Big motors on weak supplies can cause lights to dip, and the sudden torque shocks belts and couplings.

A soft starter is a good answer when you only need a gentle start and stop (a pump that suffers water hammer, a conveyor). A VSD is the answer when you need to vary the speed during normal running, which is what saves energy on fans and pumps.

Why VSDs exist, and what is inside one

Speed is set by ns = 120 f / p. You cannot change the number of poles easily, so the way to change the speed is to change the frequency. The mains supply is fixed at 50 Hz, so a VSD (also called a variable frequency drive, VFD, or inverter) makes its own AC supply at any frequency from near 0 to 50 Hz or more.

  1. Rectifier. Six diodes (T11, P06) turn the three-phase AC into DC. The DC voltage is about 1.35 × the line voltage: 1.35 × 400 = 540 V, and up to √2 × 400 = 566 V when lightly loaded.
  2. DC bus. Large capacitors (T05) smooth the DC and store energy. They stay charged after the power is switched off. Many drives bleed this off slowly through resistors, so the wait time on the warning label is commonly several minutes (read the label on the actual drive). Only an electrician, using a proper procedure and instrument, may test that the bus is safe.
  3. Inverter. Six IGBTs (insulated-gate bipolar transistors, a power switch that combines a MOSFET gate (T13) with a bipolar output (T12)) switch very fast, typically a few thousand times a second. By changing the width of the pulses, the drive controls both the frequency and the effective (average) voltage of the output.
  4. Control. A small microprocessor board (T15) takes the speed reference, calculates the pulses, watches current and temperature, and trips on a fault. This low-voltage board and its terminals are the part a BMS connects to.

V/f control

If you simply lower the frequency and leave the voltage alone, the magnetic flux in the motor rises (flux is proportional to V/f), the iron saturates, and the current shoots up. So a drive keeps the ratio V/f constant. For a 400 V, 50 Hz motor that is 400/50 = 8 V per Hz:

V = 8 V/Hz × f 30 Hz → 240 V 25 Hz → 200 V 10 Hz → 80 V (plus a small boost) At low frequency the winding's resistive drop is a larger part of the voltage, so drives add a "boost" there. Above 50 Hz the output voltage cannot rise beyond the supply, so V/f falls: the motor enters the field-weakening region, where it can run faster but with less torque.

A VSD also gives other benefits: it starts the motor gently (like a soft starter), it can stop it smoothly, it protects against overload and phase loss, and it reports current, power and faults. The costs are the purchase price, the heat it makes (typically a few percent of the power; indicative), and electrical noise.

Noise and cables

The fast edges of the PWM pulses make radio-frequency noise. Motor cables from a VSD are therefore usually screened, with the screen bonded to earth at both ends, and kept apart from control and signal cables (cross them at 90° if you must cross). This is the opposite of the single-end earthing used for your 4–20 mA instrument cables (T16). The goal is different: instrument cables fight low-frequency hum and ground loops; motor cables contain high-frequency radiated noise. Follow the drive maker's installation guide and the electrician's design.

The fan affinity laws

A fan or centrifugal pump moves a fluid. If you change its speed, three things scale in a predictable way. Imagine the blades slowing to 80 % of the speed.

  • Flow ∝ speed. The tips of the blades sweep out 80 % as much air per second. Q₂/Q₁ = n₂/n₁.
  • Pressure ∝ speed². The pressure a fan makes is the energy it gives each kilogram of air. Kinetic energy goes as velocity squared, so ΔP₂/ΔP₁ = (n₂/n₁)².
  • Power ∝ speed³. Power is flow × pressure. Flow goes as n and pressure as n², so P₂/P₁ = (n₂/n₁)³.
Q ∝ n ΔP ∝ n² P ∝ n³ Worked: 80 % speed → flow 80 %, pressure 0.8² = 64 %, power 0.8³ = 51.2 %. At 90 % speed: power 72.9 % (a 27 % saving for a 10 % slow-down). At 60 %: flow 60 %, pressure 36 %, power 21.6 %. At 50 %: power 12.5 %. A small drop in flow saves a large amount of energy.

These laws hold when the system resistance is all friction (ducts, coils, filters), so the system pressure also scales with flow squared and the fan's operating point just slides along the system curve. They are only approximate where there is a fixed static head (a pump lifting water up a building), where the saving is smaller, and at very low speed where fan and motor efficiency fall. Drive and motor losses also eat a few percent of the saving.

Why a damper wastes energy

The old way to reduce airflow was to throttle it with a damper while the fan ran at full speed. The fan still makes full pressure, and the damper simply burns off the excess pressure as turbulence. Because the fan motor keeps pushing against a higher system resistance, the power only falls a little (for many fan types, to roughly 85–95 % of full power at 80 % flow; indicative and fan-dependent). Slowing the fan down to 80 % speed reaches the same 80 % flow at about 51 % of full power.

Worked example: annual energy

A 15 kW supply fan runs 6000 hours a year. At A$0.25 per kWh (an assumed tariff; use your own), full speed uses 15 × 6000 = 90,000 kWh = A$22,500 a year. At 80 % speed all year it would use 0.512 × 90,000 = 46,080 kWh = A$11,520, saving 43,920 kWh or about A$10,980. A real fan runs at varying speeds, so the actual saving depends on the load profile over the year, but this shows why VSDs on fans and pumps pay back so quickly.

How a BMS controls a VSD

A BMS does not touch the motor power. It talks to the drive's control terminals, which are low-voltage. A typical hardwired connection uses five signals, or a single serial link replaces them all.

Worked scaling

The BMS commands 80 % speed. For a 0–10 V reference, the output is 0.80 × 10 = 8.0 V. The drive maps 0–10 V to 0–50 Hz, so the frequency is 40 Hz. A 4-pole motor has ns = 120 × 40 / 4 = 1200 rpm (a little less with slip), and by the affinity laws the fan moves about 80 % of its full-speed air using about 51 % of its full-speed power. If the drive has a minimum speed of 20 Hz (40 %), a command of 20 % (2.0 V) will still run it at 20 Hz.

What the BMS technician sets up

  • Check the input type. The drive's analogue input has a switch or parameter for 0–10 V versus 4–20 mA. The wrong choice is the most common reason a drive "ignores" the BMS (T16).
  • Check Hand-Off-Auto. Most motor control panels have a selector. The BMS controls the drive only in Auto. If someone leaves it in Hand, the BMS cannot start or stop the fan, and it may show "running" while the BMS command is ignored.
  • Minimum and maximum speed. Set in the drive by the person commissioning it (not the BMS). A minimum speed (often 20–30 Hz for fans; indicative) prevents hunting and stalling.
  • Prove airflow or flow. Use a DP sensor or switch across the fan (T18), not just the run contact, to detect a broken belt or a slipped coupling.
  • Fire mode. In many buildings smoke-control fans must keep running in a fire, so the drive has a fire-mode input from the fire panel that overrides the BMS and some faults. Never remove or bypass these (C04).
  • Test the whole loop. Command 0 %, 50 % and 100 % and watch the feedback; trip the fault and check the alarm point.
Safety: even the "control" side can surprise you

The control terminals are normally 24 V DC (ELV), but the drive's relay outputs can switch up to 230 V, and an electrician may have used one to switch a mains circuit (a damper actuator, a signal lamp, a contactor coil). Never assume a terminal is ELV because it sits on the control board. If you are not sure, do not touch or measure it: ask the electrician to confirm what it carries, or to isolate it. Do not remove covers over power terminals. After isolation, the DC bus can still be charged: it is not yours to test.

In Australia

The split of work is clear in practice. An electrician installs the drive, its power cabling and protection, and sets its motor parameters. The BMS technician (or controls contractor) lands the ELV control wires, configures the BMS points, and commissions the control loop. Licensing categories differ between states, so confirm what a BMS technician may do on a drive with the state electrical regulator and your employer.

On the job

Typical service calls: "fan runs at full speed all the time" (the 0–10 V is missing, shorted or the input is set to the wrong mode), "fan will not start from the BMS" (Hand-Off-Auto in Hand, run enable open, drive faulted), "VSD trips on overvoltage when stopping" (deceleration too fast; the electrician or drive specialist adjusts it), "BMS shows the fan running but there is no air" (belt or coupling failure, caught only by a DP proof). The BMS technician reads the fault code and signals; the drive specialist or electrician fixes the power side.

Check yourself

A 0.2 m length of conductor in a 0.4 T field carries 15 A at right angles to the field. What is the force?

F = B I L = 0.4 × 15 × 0.2 = 1.2 N. It acts at right angles to both the field and the current. Doubling the current or the length doubles the force, which is why motor torque is proportional to current.

A 24 V brushed DC motor has an armature resistance of 2 Ω. What current flows at stall? What if it is spinning freely with a back-EMF of 21 V?

At stall there is no back-EMF: I = 24 / 2 = 12 A, and the heat is I²R = 288 W, so a jammed motor cooks itself. When spinning with E = 21 V: I = (24 − 21)/2 = 1.5 A. The motor's own generator action cuts the current by a factor of eight.

A 1.8° stepper is driven at 2000 steps per second with no microstepping. How fast does it turn?

A 1.8° motor has 360 / 1.8 = 200 steps per revolution. 2000 / 200 = 10 revolutions per second = 600 rpm. At that speed the torque will have fallen well below the holding torque, so check the speed-torque curve for the load.

A 6-pole motor on 50 Hz has 960 rpm on its plate. Find the synchronous speed, slip and rotor frequency.

ns = 120 × 50 / 6 = 1000 rpm. Slip = (1000 − 960) / 1000 = 4 %. Rotor frequency = 0.04 × 50 = 2 Hz. A plate speed just under a "round" synchronous speed is the clue to the pole count.

A VSD runs a 400 V / 50 Hz, 4-pole motor at 30 Hz. What output voltage does V/f give, and what is the synchronous speed?

V/f = 400 / 50 = 8 V/Hz, so at 30 Hz the voltage is 240 V. ns = 120 × 30 / 4 = 900 rpm, and the real speed is a little under that because of slip. Keeping V/f constant holds the motor flux constant so the motor does not saturate.

A 22 kW fan is slowed to 60 % of full speed. What are the flow, pressure and power, and the power in kW?

Flow = 60 %. Pressure = 0.6² = 36 %. Power = 0.6³ = 21.6 %, which is 0.216 × 22 = 4.75 kW. So a 40 % reduction in flow cuts power by nearly 80 % (before allowing for drive and motor losses at low load).

The BMS sends a 6.0 V reference to a drive scaled 0–10 V = 0–50 Hz, but the fan runs flat out. List the first four checks.

1) Measure the voltage at the drive's ELV analogue input terminals to see whether the 6.0 V actually arrives. 2) Check that the input type is set to voltage and not current. 3) Check the Hand-Off-Auto selector and whether the drive is using its local keypad reference instead of the terminal. 4) Check the reference-source parameter and scaling in the drive (set by an authorised person). Also check the shield and common wiring (T16).

A colleague has just isolated a VSD and wants to open its cover to look at the power terminals "right now, it is switched off". What do you say?

Stop. The DC bus capacitors can hold a dangerous charge (roughly 540–565 V DC) for several minutes after isolation, and the terminals carry mains or three-phase supply. Only a licensed electrician may open the power side, follow the maker's wait time on the label, and prove the bus is safe with a suitable instrument. A BMS technician works only on the control terminals.

Go deeper (optional): why the torque falls at breakdown

At small slip the rotor frequency is low, so the rotor's inductive reactance (2πfrL) is small and the rotor current is mostly in phase with the induced voltage. Current grows in proportion to slip, and so does torque. As slip increases, the rotor frequency rises, the reactance grows, and the rotor current lags more and more behind the voltage that drives it. A lagging current makes less torque per amp. The result is a peak (breakdown torque) and then a fall. A simple model is T = 2·Tmax / (s/sb + sb/s), where sb is the slip at breakdown. Skills checklist

  • I can use F = BIL and explain why a coil in a field turns.
  • I can explain back-EMF, why a stalled DC motor draws a huge current, and why speed is proportional to voltage.
  • I can calculate steps per revolution and rpm for a stepper, and explain holding torque and lost steps.
  • I know what a BLDC or EC fan is, and which of its terminals are ELV control and which are mains.
  • I can calculate synchronous speed (120f/p) and slip, and read a three-phase motor nameplate.
  • I can describe the blocks inside a VSD, explain V/f control, and say why the DC bus is dangerous after isolation.
  • I can use the affinity laws to show that 80 % speed gives about 51 % power, and estimate an annual saving.
  • I can compare DOL, star-delta, soft starter and VSD.
  • I can list the signals between a BMS and a VSD, scale a 0–10 V reference, and diagnose a drive that ignores the BMS.
  • I know that only a licensed electrician opens or wires the power side, and that I stay on the ELV control side.
