Magnetism and inductors

A current makes a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field makes a voltage. These two facts explain relays, motors, transformers, buck converters and the voltage spike that kills transistors. By the end you will be able to calculate that spike and explain exactly why one cheap diode stops it.

A short history: from a twitching compass to the henry

In April 1820, in Copenhagen, Hans Christian Ørsted (often written Oersted) was giving a lecture with a battery and a wire. A compass needle lying near the wire swung when the current flowed. Until then, electricity and magnetism were thought to be separate subjects. One twitching needle showed that a current makes a magnetic field. Within weeks, André-Marie Ampère in Paris showed that two parallel wires carrying current attract or repel each other.

If electricity makes magnetism, can magnetism make electricity? Many people tried by putting a strong magnet next to a coil and waiting. Nothing happened. In August 1831, Michael Faraday in London wound two separate coils on one iron ring. He connected a battery to one coil and a galvanometer (a sensitive current meter) to the other. The needle kicked only at the moment he connected or disconnected the battery. While the current was steady, the needle sat at zero. This was the key discovery: a changing magnetic field makes a voltage; a steady one does not.

In the USA, Joseph Henry found the same effect at about the same time. He also noticed that a single coil gives a large spark when its own circuit is broken: the coil induces a voltage in itself. This is self-induction, and the SI unit of inductance, the henry (H), is named after him. In 1834 Heinrich Lenz stated the rule for the direction of the induced current. Faraday pictured the space around magnets filled with "lines of force". Thirty years later James Clerk Maxwell turned that picture into equations, and those equations predicted radio waves and explained light. That story continues in T10.

The magnetic field around a current

A magnetic field is a condition of space around moving charges. We describe it with the magnetic flux density B, measured in tesla (T). You can feel it as a force on another moving charge or on a compass needle. One tesla is large. Useful reference numbers:

Right-hand grip rule

Field lines around a straight wire are closed circles centred on the wire. To get the direction, grip the wire with your right hand with your thumb pointing along the conventional current. Your fingers curl the way the field points. For a coil, use the same hand the other way round: curl your fingers along the current in the turns, and your thumb points to the coil's north end (where field lines come out).

How strong? Two formulas you will use

Straight wire: B = μ₀ I / (2π r) Long coil: B = μ₀ n I μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m ≈ 1.26 × 10⁻⁶ H/m is the permeability of free space, a constant that sets how much field a current makes in vacuum or air. I is current in amperes, r the distance from the wire in metres, n the number of turns per metre of coil.

Worked example, wire. A 10 A cable, measured 1 cm from its centre: B = (1.26 × 10⁻⁶ × 10) / (2π × 0.01) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ T = 200 µT. That is about four times Earth's field, which is why a compass near a car's battery cable gives nonsense. Why mains cables in a wall do not disturb compasses much: active and neutral sit side by side with equal and opposite currents, so their fields almost cancel a few centimetres away. (That cancellation is also exactly how an RCD works; see T09.)

Worked example, coil. A coil wound with 1000 turns per metre carries 0.5 A. B = 1.26 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000 × 0.5 = 0.63 mT. Small. Put a steel or ferrite core inside and the field becomes hundreds or thousands of times bigger, because the core's atoms line up their own tiny magnets with the field. That multiplication factor is the relative permeability μr. More on cores later.

Flux: how much field passes through a loop

Induction depends on how much field threads through a loop of wire. We call this the magnetic flux Φ (Greek "phi"). For a uniform field straight through a flat loop of area A:

Φ = B × A (unit: weber, Wb = T·m²) If the field is tilted at angle θ to the loop's axis, only the perpendicular part counts: Φ = B A cos θ. For a coil of N turns, the same flux threads every turn, so the flux linkage is NΦ.

A useful way to remember the weber: 1 Wb = 1 volt × 1 second. You will see why in the next section.

Faraday's law and Lenz's law

Faraday's discovery, in one sentence: when the flux through a loop changes, a voltage (EMF) appears around the loop, equal to the rate of change of flux. For a coil of N turns:

ε = − N dΦ/dt ε (epsilon) is the induced EMF in volts. dΦ/dt is how fast the flux changes, in webers per second. The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced voltage pushes current in the direction that fights the change.

Now the unit makes sense: if flux changes by 1 Wb in 1 s through one turn, you get 1 V. A weber is a volt-second.

It does not matter how the flux changes. You can move a magnet, move the coil, change the current in a nearby coil (a transformer), change the current in the same coil (self-inductance), or rotate the coil in a field (a generator, T07). Only the rate of change matters.

Worked example. A pickup coil has 200 turns and an area of 4 cm² (4 × 10⁻⁴ m²). A magnet brings the field through it from 0 to 0.5 T in 10 ms. Change of flux per turn: ΔΦ = 0.5 × 4 × 10⁻⁴ = 2 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. EMF ≈ N ΔΦ / Δt = 200 × 2 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.01 = 4 V while the magnet moves. Move it ten times faster and you get 40 V. Hold it still and you get 0 V. This is how a bicycle dynamo, a guitar pickup and a car's magnetic wheel-speed sensor all work.

Lenz's law: nature opposes the change

The direction of the induced current is always such that its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that caused it. Push a magnet's north pole into a coil and the coil's near end becomes a north pole, pushing back. Pull the magnet out and the near end becomes a south pole, pulling it back.

Why must it be this way? Energy conservation. If the induced current helped the change, pushing a magnet in slightly would induce a current that pulls it in harder, which induces more current, and so on: free energy forever. That does not happen. Because the induced current opposes you, you must do work to move the magnet, and that work is exactly the electrical energy delivered to the circuit. A bike dynamo feels harder to pedal when the light is on for this reason.

Common misconception

"Lenz's law says the induced field opposes the magnet's field." Not quite. It opposes the change in flux. When you pull the magnet out, the flux is falling, so the induced field points the same way as the magnet's field, trying to keep the flux up. The coil is not anti-magnet; it is anti-change. Keep that phrase: anti-change. It is the whole personality of an inductor.

Try this

Drop a strong neodymium magnet down a copper or aluminium pipe (a 30 cm length of 15 mm copper plumbing pipe from Bunnings works). It falls slowly, as if through honey, even though copper is not magnetic. The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, rings of current (eddy currents) flow, and by Lenz's law they brake the magnet. The magnet's lost potential energy becomes a tiny amount of heat in the pipe.

Self-inductance: a coil that fights its own changes

A coil carrying current makes flux through its own turns. If you change the current, you change that flux, and by Faraday's law a voltage appears across the coil. By Lenz's law that voltage pushes against the change. This is self-inductance. The amount of flux linkage per ampere is the coil's inductance L:

L = NΦ / I → VL = L · di/dt L is in henries (H). 1 H means a current changing at 1 A per second produces 1 V across the coil. di/dt is the rate of change of current in amperes per second. The voltage across an ideal inductor depends only on how fast the current is changing, not on how big the current is.

For a coil, the flux per turn is proportional to N (more turns, more field), and the flux linkage is N times that, so L grows as N². For a long solenoid:

L ≈ μ₀ μr N² A / ℓ A is the cross-section area of the coil (m²), ℓ its length (m), μr the relative permeability of the core (1 for air).

Worked example. 100 turns of wire on a 1 cm diameter former, 2 cm long, air core. A = π × (0.005)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m². L = 1.26 × 10⁻⁶ × 100² × 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ / 0.02 ≈ 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ H ≈ 49 µH. Double the turns to 200 and L becomes about 200 µH (four times). Put the same winding on a closed ferrite core with an effective μr of a few hundred and you reach several millihenries. That is why real inductors are wound on cores: you get the inductance you need with fewer turns, so less copper resistance.

The best mental model: an inductor is inertia for current

Compare V = L di/dt with Newton's second law, F = m dv/dt. Voltage plays the role of force, current the role of velocity, and inductance the role of mass. A heavy flywheel does not resist spinning; it resists changes in spin. Once it is spinning steadily, it needs no force to keep going (apart from friction). Try to stop it suddenly and it pushes back with an enormous force.

An inductor is the same. Once current is flowing steadily, the inductor has no voltage across it (apart from its wire resistance). Try to stop the current suddenly and it produces whatever voltage it needs to keep the current flowing. That is the relay kick you will meet below.

Capacitor (T05)

I = C dV/dt. Resists sudden changes of voltage. Stores energy in the electric field: ½CV². At DC steady state it is an open circuit.

Inductor

V = L di/dt. Resists sudden changes of current. Stores energy in the magnetic field: ½LI². At DC steady state it is a short circuit (just its wire resistance).

Common misconception

"An inductor resists current, like a resistor." No. An inductor resists the change of current. A 10 mH coil with 2 Ω of copper resistance on 12 V DC settles at 12 / 2 = 6 A, exactly as a 2 Ω resistor would. The inductance only decides how long it takes to get there. At AC, where the current is always changing, the inductor does limit current, and we call that opposition reactance (T07). But it comes from the change, not from the current.

Energy stored in the magnetic field

To build up current in an inductor you must push against its back-EMF. The power you put in is p = v·i = L i di/dt. Add it up (integrate) from zero current to a final current I:

W = ∫ L i di = ½ L I² The energy does not go into heat (for an ideal coil). It sits in the magnetic field, with energy density B² / 2μ₀ joules per cubic metre. When the current falls, the field gives the energy back.

Worked example, relay coil. A 12 V relay coil of 0.1 H draws 50 mA. W = ½ × 0.1 × 0.05² = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ J = 125 µJ. That sounds tiny, and it is: a 5 mm LED uses more energy than that in a few milliseconds. But if that energy is released in 1 µs, the power is 125 µJ / 1 µs = 125 W, concentrated in one small transistor junction. Energy is not the problem; how fast it is forced out is the problem.

Worked example, buck converter inductor. A 22 µH inductor carrying 3 A stores ½ × 22 × 10⁻⁶ × 9 ≈ 99 µJ. In a 500 kHz converter, the inductor takes in and hands back a fraction of this energy half a million times a second. A small part refilled very often is how a pea-sized inductor can move tens of watts.

Where the energy really sits

In a ferrite inductor with a small air gap in its core, most of the stored energy is in the gap, not in the ferrite. The ferrite has high permeability, so it needs only a small H to carry the flux, and energy density is ½BH. The gap has the same B but needs a big H. Designers add a gap on purpose: it stores the energy and stops the core saturating.

RL circuits and the time constant

Connect a coil with inductance L and total resistance R to a DC supply V through a switch. At the moment you close the switch, the current is zero. The inductor will not let it jump, so the whole supply voltage appears across the inductor: di/dt = V / L. As current builds, more voltage is dropped across R, less is left for L, and the current rises more slowly. The result is the same shape as a charging capacitor:

i(t) = (V/R) · (1 − e−t/τ) τ = L / R τ (tau) is the time constant in seconds. After 1 τ the current is 63 % of its final value V/R; after 3 τ, 95 %; after 5 τ, more than 99 %: call it finished. When the supply is removed and the current has a path, it decays as i = I₀ e−t/τ.

Worked example. A 12 V relay coil has R = 240 Ω (so it draws 12 / 240 = 50 mA) and L = 0.1 H. τ = 0.1 / 240 ≈ 0.42 ms. The current reaches 63 % (32 mA) after 0.42 ms and is essentially full after about 2 ms. Add the mechanical time for the armature to move and a small relay pulls in after roughly 5–15 ms. Notice that L/R being small is why relays still feel "instant".

Note the duality with T05: for RC, τ = RC (more R makes it slower). For RL, τ = L/R (more R makes it faster), because more resistance means a smaller final current, which the inductor reaches sooner.

The relay-coil kick and the flyback diode

Here is the situation you will build in P08: a microcontroller pin drives an NPN transistor, and the transistor switches a 12 V relay coil to ground. When the transistor turns on, current builds up in the coil gently, with τ = 0.42 ms, and nothing dramatic happens. The trouble is when it turns off.

Worked example: how big is the spike?

The coil carries 50 mA. A transistor turns off in about 1 µs. If the current really fell from 50 mA to zero in 1 µs:

V = L · di/dt = 0.1 H × (0.05 A / 1 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 5000 V The coil produces this voltage in the direction that tries to keep the current flowing, so the transistor's collector is driven far above the 12 V supply.

In real life the voltage does not reach 5000 V, because something gives way first. The collector voltage rises until something conducts: the transistor's collector–base junction breaks down (avalanche), or a spark jumps across a mechanical switch's contacts, or the coil's own stray capacitance absorbs the energy and rings. We can estimate the last case. If all ½LI² goes into a stray capacitance C of about 100 pF, then ½CV² = ½LI², so

Vpeak = I √(L/C) = 0.05 × √(0.1 / 100 × 10⁻¹²) ≈ 1600 V

A small-signal transistor such as a BC337 or 2N2222 is rated for only about 40–45 V between collector and emitter. Each switch-off pushes it into breakdown and dumps the coil's energy into a tiny spot of silicon. It may survive tens or thousands of times, then fail short. On top of that, a spike of hundreds of volts couples into nearby wiring and resets microcontrollers. This is the most common "my circuit works for a while and then dies" fault in hobby and field electronics.

Common misconception

"The spike can't be bigger than the supply, because the supply is only 12 V." The spike does not come from the supply. It comes from the energy stored in the coil's field, and the inductor will produce any voltage needed to keep its current flowing. A 5 V relay can produce hundreds of volts. An inductor is a current source for a short moment, not a voltage source.

The fix: give the current somewhere to go

The inductor's demand is simple: "my current must keep flowing for a moment". So give it a path. Put a diode across the coil, with its cathode (band) to the +12 V end and its anode to the transistor end. In normal operation the diode is reverse biased and does nothing. At switch-off the coil's voltage reverses, the diode becomes forward biased, and the coil's current circulates round the little loop of coil and diode. The collector only rises to 12 V + 0.7 V = 12.7 V. The stored 125 µJ is burned slowly as heat in the coil's own 240 Ω resistance and in the diode. This diode is called a flyback, freewheeling or snubber diode. A 1N4007 (~A$0.10) or 1N4148 is enough for small relays.

Deep dive: why a diode conducts one way only

The price of the simple diode: slow release

With only a diode, the voltage across the coil during freewheeling is small: the coil's own drop I·R plus 0.7 V. So the current falls slowly: di/dt = −(I·R + 0.7) / L = −(12 + 0.7) / 0.1 ≈ −127 A/s at the start, and it takes about a millisecond for the field to collapse. The relay contacts open later and more slowly, which can increase arcing on the contacts when switching heavy loads.

To release faster, let the coil voltage rise higher, but to a controlled level. Put a Zener diode (say 24 V) in series with the flyback diode. Now the freewheel voltage is 12 + 24.7 ≈ 37 V across the coil and di/dt is about three times larger, so the field collapses about three times faster. The collector now reaches 12 + 24.7 ≈ 37 V, so the transistor must be rated above that. A TVS (transient voltage suppressor) diode does the same job. This is a general rule: a higher clamp voltage gives a faster current decay, because the inductor's di/dt equals the voltage across it divided by L.

Safety

The kick is real even on ELV circuits. A 12 V coil interrupted by hand (pulling a wire off) can give you a sharp, harmless-but-painful tingle across your fingers and will pit the contacts. Large inductors (motor windings, big chokes) store far more energy. Always make sure an inductive load has a path for its current before you break it.

In Australia

The inductive loads you will meet on Australian building jobs mostly run on extra-low voltage: BMS relays and actuators on 24 V AC or 24 V DC, electric door strikes and magnetic locks on 12 V or 24 V DC, solenoid valves, and the coils of contactors in plant rooms. Relay modules sold by Jaycar, Altronics and Core Electronics normally already include the flyback diode; bare relays do not. The mains side of a contactor (the 230/400 V it switches) is electricians' work, but the 24 V coil circuit is often yours.

On the job

Access control installers fit a diode (or the suppressor supplied in the box) across every electric strike and maglock, as close to the lock as practical. Forget it, and the spike at each door release wears the controller's relay contacts, resets the controller, or corrupts reader data. For an AC coil (for example a 24 V AC contactor) a plain diode cannot be used, because it would short every other half-cycle; there you use an RC snubber or a varistor (MOV), which clamp in both directions.

Inductors in buck converters

Suppose you need 5 V at 3 A from a 24 V DC building supply. A linear regulator (like a 7805) would drop the extra 19 V as heat: 19 V × 3 A = 57 W of heat to make 15 W of output. A buck converter does it at about 90 % efficiency by using an inductor as a temporary energy store. This is why your USB car charger stays cool. You will build both in P10.

A transistor switch connects the inductor to 24 V for a short part of each cycle, then disconnects it. While connected, the inductor has (24 − 5) = 19 V across it and its current ramps up. When the switch opens, the inductor does exactly what the relay coil did: it insists on keeping its current flowing. This time we want that. A diode (or a second transistor, in a "synchronous" buck) gives it a path from ground, and the inductor keeps feeding the load while its current ramps down with −5 V across it. The output capacitor smooths the small ripple that remains.

The maths in two lines

In steady operation the inductor current must end each cycle where it started, so the "volt-seconds" up must equal the volt-seconds down. If the switch is on for a fraction D of the period (the duty cycle):

(Vin − Vout) · D = Vout · (1 − D) → Vout = D · Vin For 24 V → 5 V, D = 5/24 ≈ 0.21. The controller chip adjusts D continuously to hold 5 V as the load and input change.

Ripple. At 500 kHz the period is 2 µs, so the switch is on for 0.21 × 2 µs ≈ 0.42 µs. ΔI = VL × ton / L = 19 × 0.42 × 10⁻⁶ / 22 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 0.36 A peak-to-peak. A smaller inductor gives more ripple; a higher switching frequency gives less, which is why modern converters switch fast and use tiny inductors.

Bench tip

Buying a replacement inductor? Check three numbers on the datasheet: inductance, saturation current (Isat, where inductance has dropped by a set amount, often 20–30 %), and rated or RMS current (Irms, the current that warms it by about 40 °C). The peak current (average plus half the ripple) must stay below Isat. A same-value inductor with a lower Isat can make a converter fail.

Cores, ferrite and saturation

Iron, nickel, cobalt and certain ceramics are ferromagnetic. Inside them, groups of atoms called domains are already tiny magnets. Normally the domains point in random directions and cancel out. A small applied field (from the coil's current) swings them into line, and their fields add to the coil's. This is why a core multiplies inductance by μr.

Engineers describe this with two quantities. H, the "magnetising force", is what the coil's current provides: H = N·I / ℓ, in ampere-turns per metre. B is the resulting flux density. In air, B = μ₀H. In a core, B = μ₀μrH, but only up to a limit.

Saturation

Once nearly all domains are aligned, there is nothing left to line up. B stops rising steeply and only grows at the tiny air rate. The core is saturated. Its effective μr falls towards 1, so the inductance collapses. In a circuit this is dramatic: in the buck converter above, if the peak current pushes the core into saturation, L suddenly drops, di/dt = V/L shoots up, and the current spikes until the switch or fuse fails. Typical saturation flux densities: ferrite about 0.3–0.5 T, silicon steel about 1.5–2 T.

Losses in cores: hysteresis and eddy currents

Hysteresis loss. Flipping domains back and forth is not perfectly reversible; it costs a little energy each cycle, equal to the area of the loop in Figure 5. More cycles per second means more heat, so loss grows with frequency. "Soft" magnetic materials have thin loops.

Eddy currents. A changing flux inside a solid lump of iron induces circulating currents in the iron itself (the copper-pipe experiment, inside the core). They waste power as I²R heat. At 50 Hz the cure is to build the core from thin insulated sheets, called laminations, which break up the current loops (T08). At tens of kHz to MHz, even laminations are too thick, so we use ferrite: a ceramic of iron oxide mixed with manganese–zinc or nickel–zinc. It is magnetic but has very high electrical resistance, so eddy currents can hardly flow.

Common misconception

"More core material is always better." A high-μ core gives more inductance per turn, but it also saturates at a lower current for a given winding. For energy-storage inductors (bucks, flyback transformers), designers deliberately reduce the effective permeability with an air gap or use powdered-iron cores with a "distributed gap", so the inductor can carry more current without saturating.

Check yourself

A coil of 10 mH and 2 Ω copper resistance sits on a 12 V DC supply for a long time. What current flows, and what voltage is across the inductance?

6 A (12 / 2). In steady DC, di/dt = 0, so the voltage across the inductance part is L × 0 = 0 V; all 12 V is across the copper resistance. Inductance sets how fast you get to 6 A (τ = L/R = 5 ms), not how much flows at the end.

Why does breaking an inductive circuit cause a spark, but making it does not?

When you close the switch, the current starts at zero and rises at a rate set by V/L: the inductor's voltage can never exceed the supply. When you open it, you are trying to force the current from its full value to zero almost instantly. The inductor produces V = L di/dt, which is huge for a large di/dt, so the voltage across the opening gap rises until the air breaks down and an arc carries the current.

Someone fits the flyback diode the wrong way round (band to the transistor side). What happens?

When the transistor turns on, its collector drops to about 0 V. The reversed diode is now forward biased straight from +12 V to the collector, bypassing the coil, so it shorts the supply through the diode and transistor. Something overheats or the supply limits/fuses. Correct orientation: cathode (band) to the positive supply, so the diode is reverse biased in normal operation.

A relay with a flyback diode drops out too slowly. Why, and how could you speed it up?

During freewheeling, the voltage across the coil is only I·R + 0.7 V, so the current decays slowly (di/dt = V/L is small). To speed it up, allow a higher but controlled voltage: add a Zener (e.g. 24 V) in series with the diode, or use a TVS. The current then decays faster. The transistor must be rated above the supply plus the clamp voltage.

You double the number of turns on the same core. What happens to the inductance?

It becomes about four times bigger. Twice the turns make twice the flux for the same current, and that flux links twice as many turns: L ∝ N². (Copper resistance also roughly doubles, and saturation now happens at half the current, because H = N·I/ℓ doubles.)

A magnet falls slowly through a copper pipe. Copper is not magnetic. Where does the braking force come from, and where does the energy go?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, inducing eddy currents (Faraday). By Lenz's law, the eddy currents' fields oppose the change, which means they push up on the magnet as it approaches and pull up on it as it leaves. The magnet's lost gravitational energy becomes I²R heat in the copper.

Why can't you put a plain diode across a 24 V AC contactor coil?

With AC, the coil voltage reverses every half-cycle. A diode across it would be forward biased on every other half-cycle and short-circuit the supply. For AC coils use a component that only conducts above a voltage in both directions (a varistor/MOV or bidirectional TVS) or an RC snubber, which gives the current a path without shorting the normal AC.

A buck converter works at 1 A but fails at 3 A, even though the inductor's "rated current" is 4 A. What should you check?

The saturation current Isat. If the peak current (load plus half the ripple) exceeds Isat, the core saturates, L collapses, and current spikes. Rated (RMS) current is about heating, not saturation. Choose an inductor with Isat above the peak current with some margin.
