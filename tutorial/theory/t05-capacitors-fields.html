Electric fields and capacitors

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator, and its energy lives in the electric field between them. You will derive C = εA/d and the RC charging curve, see why a capacitor "blocks DC and passes AC", and learn what real capacitors do that the ideal one does not.

Reading 50 minLevel FoundationMaths Simple integration, one differential equationPractice P07, P09, P10, P15

How we found out

In 1745 and 1746, two experimenters independently tried to "store" electricity in a glass jar of water: Ewald Georg von Kleist in Germany and Pieter van Musschenbroek at the University of Leiden in the Netherlands. They charged the water through a wire from a friction machine while holding the jar in one hand. When they touched the wire with the other hand, they got a shock far stronger than anything the machine gave directly. Musschenbroek wrote that he would not repeat it for the whole kingdom of France. The "Leyden jar" (later made with metal foil inside and outside the glass) was the first capacitor.

Benjamin Franklin studied Leyden jars in the 1740s. He showed that the charge was held by the glass between the two conductors, not by the water, and he connected several jars together and called the group a "battery", the first use of that word in electricity. In 1837 Michael Faraday measured how much more charge a capacitor holds at the same voltage when you fill the gap with different insulators, which he called their "specific inductive capacity". We now call it the dielectric constant, and the unit of capacitance, the farad, is named after him.

In the 1860s James Clerk Maxwell asked a sharp question: current flows in the wire to one plate and out of the other plate, but no charge crosses the gap, so is the circuit broken? His answer was that a changing electric field in the gap acts as a current, which he called displacement current. That idea completed his equations and predicted electromagnetic waves (T10). Practical capacitors followed: the aluminium electrolytic capacitor was patented by Charles Pollak in 1896, and the first supercapacitor patent was filed at General Electric in 1957, with commercial products from the late 1970s.

The field between two plates

Take two flat metal plates, each of area A, parallel and a small distance d apart. Connect a battery: it pulls electrons off one plate and pushes them onto the other, until the plates carry +Q and −Q. The capacitor as a whole stays neutral. Between the plates there is now an electric field E, pointing from + to −.

Gauss's law (from Class 12) gives the field near a large charged sheet. For two closely spaced plates with charge density σ = Q/A, the fields from the two plates add between them and cancel outside:

E = σ / ε₀ = Q / (ε₀ A) V = E × d = Q d / (ε₀ A) ε₀ (epsilon-nought) = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space, a constant of nature. The field is uniform between the plates, so the voltage is simply field × distance.

The charge stored is proportional to the voltage. The ratio is the capacitance:

C = Q / V = ε₀ A / d (with an insulator: C = εr ε₀ A / d) C in farads (F): 1 F = 1 C/V. Bigger plates hold more charge at the same voltage; a smaller gap gives a stronger pull between the + and − charges, so again more charge for the same voltage. εr is the relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of the insulator.

Worked example: how big is a farad?

Two plates 10 cm × 10 cm (A = 0.01 m²), 1 mm apart in air: C = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 / 0.001 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹¹ F = 88.5 pF (picofarads). Tiny.

To make 1 µF with a 1 mm air gap you would need A = C d / ε₀ = 10⁻⁶ × 10⁻³ / 8.854 × 10⁻¹² ≈ 113 m² of plate: the floor of a small house. Yet a 1 µF capacitor is the size of a grain of rice. Engineers get there three ways, all visible in the formula: make d tiny (films and oxides a few micrometres down to nanometres thick), make εr large (special ceramics), and make A huge (long rolled foils, roughened surfaces, stacked layers).

Capacitors in parallel and in series

In parallel, capacitors share the same voltage and their charges add, like adding plate area: Ctotal = C₁ + C₂ + …. In series, each carries the same charge and their voltages add, like making the gap thicker: 1/Ctotal = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + …. These are the opposite of the resistor rules in T04.

Common misconception

"A capacitor stores charge." A charged capacitor has +Q on one plate and −Q on the other, so its total charge is zero, exactly as before. What it stores is energy, in the electric field between the plates. "Charging a capacitor" means separating charge, not adding it. The same was true of batteries in T01.

Dielectrics and polarisation

Put an insulator (a dielectric) between the plates and the capacitance goes up by the factor εr. Why? Inside an insulator the electrons cannot leave their atoms, but the field can stretch each atom or molecule slightly: the electron cloud shifts towards the + plate, and the nucleus towards the − plate. Some molecules (such as water) are already lopsided and they rotate to line up with the field. Either way, the material becomes full of tiny aligned dipoles. This is polarisation.

Every dielectric also has a breakdown field: above it, electrons are torn loose, a spark punches through, and the capacitor is destroyed (or, in air, a spark jumps). Air breaks down at about 3 kV per millimetre. Good plastic films withstand hundreds of volts per micrometre. This is the reason for every capacitor's voltage rating: the thinner the dielectric (for more capacitance), the lower the voltage it can survive.

Energy stored in the field

Charging a capacitor takes work, because each new bit of charge must be pushed onto a plate that already repels it. When the capacitor holds charge q, its voltage is v = q/C. Moving an extra small charge dq costs energy dW = v dq = (q/C) dq. Add up (integrate) from empty to Q:

W = ∫₀Q (q / C) dq = Q² / (2C) = ½ C V² The first coulombs go on easily (low voltage); the last ones need the full voltage. On average you pay half the final voltage per coulomb, hence the ½.

Where is the energy? In the field. Put C = εA/d and V = Ed into ½CV²: W = ½ (εA/d)(Ed)² = ½ εE² × (A d). A × d is the volume between the plates, so the field holds u = ½ εE² joules per cubic metre wherever it exists. This is the same "energy in the field" idea as in T01.

Worked numbers

  • A 1,000 µF, 25 V smoothing capacitor at 24 V: W = ½ × 10⁻³ × 24² = 0.29 J. Small, but released in microseconds through a screwdriver it makes a loud spark and pits the metal.
  • A 100 nF decoupling capacitor at 5 V: W = ½ × 10⁻⁷ × 25 = 1.25 µJ. Tiny, but it can deliver that in nanoseconds, which is exactly its job.
  • A 10 F, 2.7 V supercapacitor: W = ½ × 10 × 2.7² = 36 J. Enough to keep a clock chip running for a very long time.

Notice the V². Doubling the voltage stores four times the energy. That is why high-voltage capacitors are dangerous even when small.

Safety

Capacitors can stay charged after the power is off. Before touching a board, discharge large capacitors through a resistor (for example 100 Ω to 1 kΩ, 1–5 W, held with insulated leads), then confirm 0 V with a meter. Never short them with a screwdriver. Capacitors inside mains equipment, power supplies, microwave ovens and camera flash units can hold lethal energy at hundreds of volts: never open mains equipment. In Australia, work inside mains-powered equipment is for licensed electricians and qualified repairers; everything in this course stays at extra-low voltage.

Charging through a resistor: the RC curve

Intuition first

At the instant the switch closes, the capacitor is empty, so vC = 0. The whole supply voltage is across R, and the current is as large as it will ever be: i = V₀/R. An empty capacitor acts, for that instant, like a plain wire.

As charge builds up, vC rises. Now less voltage is left across R, so the current falls, so the capacitor fills more slowly. The closer it gets to V₀, the slower it goes. Anything that slows down in proportion to how far it still has to go follows an exponential curve. It approaches V₀ but, in theory, never quite gets there.

The maths

KVL round the loop: V₀ = iR + vC. The current is the rate at which charge arrives on the plate: i = dq/dt = C dvC/dt (since q = CvC). Substitute:

RC × dvC/dt = V₀ − vC

Separate the variables (all the v on one side, all the t on the other) and integrate from vC = 0 at t = 0:

∫ dvC / (V₀ − vC) = ∫ dt / RC
−ln(V₀ − vC) + ln V₀ = t / RC
(V₀ − vC) / V₀ = e−t/RC
vC(t) = V₀ (1 − e−t/RC) i(t) = (V₀/R) e−t/RC The product τ = RC is the time constant. Check the units: ohms × farads = (V/A) × (C/V) = C/A = seconds.

Discharging a capacitor from V₀ through R gives the mirror image: vC(t) = V₀ e−t/RC.

Worked examples

  • R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 µF: τ = 10⁴ × 10⁻⁴ = 1 s. On a 9 V supply the capacitor reaches 5.7 V after 1 s and is "full" (99%) after about 5 s. The first current is 9 V/10 kΩ = 0.9 mA.
  • A switch debounce filter, R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF: τ = 1 ms. Contact bounce lasting under a millisecond is smoothed out (P11).
  • A 555 timer measures time by charging a capacitor between 1/3 and 2/3 of the supply: t = RC × ln 2 ≈ 0.693 RC for that step (P09).
  • When does the voltage reach a given fraction? Solve v/V₀ = 1 − e−t/τ for t: t = −τ ln(1 − v/V₀). To 50%: t = τ ln 2 = 0.69τ. To 90%: t = τ ln 10 = 2.3τ.

A surprising energy fact

While charging from 0 to V₀, the supply pushes a total charge Q = CV₀ through its full voltage V₀, so it delivers energy QV₀ = CV₀². The capacitor ends up holding ½CV₀². The other half, ½CV₀², is turned into heat in the resistor. This is true whatever the value of R: a smaller R gives a bigger current for a shorter time, and the heat comes out the same. (You can check it: ∫ i²R dt with i = (V₀/R)e−t/RC gives exactly ½CV₀².) It is one reason switch-mode power supplies use inductors to move energy instead of charging capacitors straight from a fixed voltage.

Common misconception

"After one time constant the capacitor is full." After one τ it is only 63% charged. Engineers usually treat 5τ (over 99%) as "fully charged". And a capacitor charged through 1 MΩ is not "slower to hold charge" than one charged through 1 kΩ; it simply fills more slowly to the same final voltage and the same stored energy.

Why a capacitor "blocks DC and passes AC"

The key equation is the one we used above:

i = C × dv/dt Current flows into a capacitor only while its voltage is changing. The faster the change, the bigger the current.

DC: after about 5τ, the voltage stops changing, so dv/dt = 0 and the current is zero. In steady DC a capacitor is an open circuit. That is why a capacitor in series with a signal "blocks" a DC level, and why a charged smoothing capacitor across a supply draws no current once full (apart from leakage).

AC: if the voltage across a capacitor is a sine wave, v = Vp sin(2πft), it is always changing, so current always flows: i = C × dv/dt = 2πfC Vp cos(2πft). The current is a sine wave too, shifted a quarter cycle ahead of the voltage, and its peak is 2πfC times Vp. The ratio of voltage to current is the capacitor's reactance:

XC = 1 / (2π f C) (ohms) Large at low frequency, small at high frequency. At DC (f = 0), XC is infinite: an open circuit.

To the steady DC supply it is invisible. Reactance, phase and impedance are developed properly in T07.

Common misconception

"AC current flows through the capacitor." No electrons cross the insulator. During one half-cycle electrons pile onto one plate while the same number leave the other plate; next half-cycle they reverse. Outside the capacitor it looks exactly as if current passed through. Inside, the "current" across the gap is Maxwell's displacement current: the changing electric field. The opposite myth, "a capacitor blocks all current", is also wrong: it blocks only steady current.

What a real capacitor adds to the ideal one

  • ESR (equivalent series resistance). The foils, leads and electrolyte have resistance, modelled as a resistor in series. Ripple current through it makes heat: P = Iripple² × ESR. A general-purpose 1,000 µF electrolytic may have an ESR of the order of 0.1 Ω; "low-ESR" types made for switch-mode supplies have much less. As an electrolytic ages and its electrolyte dries, ESR rises, it heats more, and it ages faster still.
  • ESL (equivalent series inductance). Leads and internal structure add a few nanohenries. Above a certain frequency (the self-resonant frequency) the capacitor behaves like an inductor. That is why decoupling capacitors must be small, close to the pin, with short tracks, and why designers sometimes combine a 10 µF and a 100 nF.
  • Leakage current. No dielectric is perfect, so a small DC current leaks through. Ceramics and films leak almost nothing. Electrolytic datasheets often specify a maximum such as 0.01 × C × V µA (C in µF, V in volts) or 3 µA, whichever is larger, after a couple of minutes. For 1,000 µF at 25 V: up to 250 µA.
  • Voltage rating. Never exceed it. A common habit is to choose an electrolytic rated about 1.5 to 2 times the working voltage (a 25 V part on a 12 V rail, a 35 V or 50 V part on 24 V). Tantalums are usually derated further.
  • DC bias effect in class 2 ceramics. A 10 µF X5R ceramic in a small package may give only a fraction of its rated value with 5 V across it, sometimes less than half. Check the maker's DC-bias curve.
  • Temperature and life. Electrolytics are rated for a number of hours at their maximum temperature (for example 2,000 h at 105 °C). A widely used rule of thumb is that life roughly doubles for every 10 °C cooler they run. A capacitor next to a hot heatsink dies first.

Why electrolytics are polarised

The dielectric in an aluminium electrolytic is aluminium oxide that is grown on the anode foil by passing current in one direction through the electrolyte ("forming"). With the correct polarity, the same chemistry keeps the oxide healthy. With reverse voltage, the chemistry runs backwards: the oxide is attacked, leakage current rises sharply, the electrolyte heats and makes gas, the can bulges, and the scored vent on top splits open. A larger reverse voltage can make it burst. So the − stripe on the can and the shorter lead must go to the more negative point. (Bipolar or "non-polarised" electrolytics are two capacitors back-to-back inside one can.)

On the job

Swollen or leaking electrolytic capacitors are one of the most common reasons a 12 V CCTV power supply, a DVR or NVR, a network switch or a plug-pack stops working, especially in hot ceiling spaces and outdoor enclosures. Typical signs: the supply output has excess ripple or sags under load, devices reboot randomly, or the unit will not start. You will usually swap the whole power supply on site, but recognising a domed capacitor top tells you why it failed and that the location runs too hot.

Bench tip

When you replace an electrolytic, match or exceed the capacitance, voltage rating and temperature rating (105 °C rather than 85 °C), and use a low-ESR type in switch-mode supplies. Fit it with the stripe (−) the same way as the original. Mark the board orientation before you remove the old one.

Supercapacitors

A supercapacitor (electric double-layer capacitor, EDLC) pushes C = εA/d to the extreme. The electrodes are activated carbon, a sponge-like material with a surface area of the order of 1,000–2,000 square metres per gram. When a voltage is applied, ions in the electrolyte line up against the carbon surface, forming a "double layer" with a charge separation d of well under a nanometre. Enormous A and tiny d give capacitances of farads to thousands of farads in a small can.

The cost is voltage: the electrolyte starts to break down above about 2.5–3 V per cell. Higher-voltage modules put cells in series, with balancing circuits so each cell shares the voltage evenly.

Compare energy. A 100 F, 2.7 V cell stores ½ × 100 × 2.7² ≈ 365 J ≈ 0.1 Wh. An AA alkaline cell stores about 3 Wh (T03), about 30 times more. But the supercapacitor can be charged or discharged in seconds, at high current, for hundreds of thousands of cycles, which a battery cannot. Uses: keeping a real-time clock or memory alive during power cuts, buffering bursts of current for radios and motors, energy harvesting, and regenerative braking in some buses and trams.

Check yourself

Two plates of 20 cm × 20 cm are 0.5 mm apart in air. What is the capacitance? What if the gap is filled with polyester (εr = 3.3)?

A = 0.04 m². C = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.04 / 0.0005 ≈ 708 pF. With polyester: 3.3 × 708 ≈ 2.3 nF.

Why does inserting a dielectric increase capacitance, in terms of charges?

The field polarises the dielectric: its molecules become aligned dipoles. Bound charges appear on its surfaces, opposite in sign to the nearby plate charge, and partly cancel the field. For the same plate charge Q, the field and therefore the voltage are smaller, so C = Q/V is larger by the factor εr.

How much energy is stored in a 2,200 µF capacitor at 12 V? And at 24 V?

½ × 2.2 × 10⁻³ × 144 ≈ 0.16 J. At 24 V: ½ × 2.2 × 10⁻³ × 576 ≈ 0.63 J, four times as much, because energy goes as V².

R = 47 kΩ and C = 10 µF charge from a 5 V supply. What is τ, and what is the voltage after 1 s?

τ = 47,000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.47 s. After 1 s, t/τ = 2.13, so v = 5 × (1 − e−2.13) = 5 × (1 − 0.119) ≈ 4.4 V.

At the instant a switch connects an empty capacitor through 1 kΩ to 12 V, what is the current? Why?

12 mA. An empty capacitor has 0 V across it at that instant, so the full 12 V is across the resistor: I = 12/1,000. The current then falls exponentially as the capacitor voltage rises.

What is the reactance of a 100 nF capacitor at 50 Hz and at 100 kHz? What does that tell you about decoupling?

XC = 1/(2πfC): about 31.8 kΩ at 50 Hz and 15.9 Ω at 100 kHz. It is almost an open circuit to slow changes and a low impedance to fast ones, so it shunts fast current spikes to ground locally while leaving the DC supply alone. (At very high frequencies its ESL limits this.)

When you charge a capacitor from a fixed supply through a resistor, what fraction of the energy from the supply ends up stored? Does R matter?

Exactly half; the other half becomes heat in the resistor. R does not matter: a smaller R gives larger current for a shorter time, and the total heat is the same ½CV₀².

Why can reversing an aluminium electrolytic make it bulge or vent?

Its dielectric is an oxide layer formed electrochemically on the anode. Reverse voltage drives that chemistry backwards, damaging the oxide. Leakage current then rises sharply, heating the electrolyte and producing gas, which builds pressure until the can bulges or the vent opens.
