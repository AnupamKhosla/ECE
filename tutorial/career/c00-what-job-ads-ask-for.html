What Australian job ads actually ask for

Before learning BMS or CCTV in depth, look at the evidence. Your own job research captured 2,764 live SEEK adverts on 1–2 November 2026. This chapter pulls out the technician roles this course targets: how many ads there are, how crowded they are, which tickets and tools they mention, and what they pay.

How to read these numbers

The ads come from your seek_flutter research project ("Opportunity Radar"). Each ad has the applicant count SEEK showed on the day it was captured. That counts applications to that one ad, not unique people, and it says nothing about who was hired. Role families and requirements below were found by keyword matching on each ad's title and text, so an ad can appear in two families and some matches are noisy. Small groups (fewer than about 30 ads) can swing a lot. Treat this as a well-informed snapshot of one fortnight, not a law of the market.

How crowded is each role family?

The median ad across all 2,764 had 38 applicants. Lower numbers mean less competition per advert. Most of the families in this job track sit well below that, which is the main reason this course is built around them.

What this means

Less crowded does not mean easy; these ads often want specific tickets. But an ad with 15–25 applicants, where many applicants lack the licence it asks for, is a much better bet than a software role with 65 or more. That trade (tickets and hands-on skill in exchange for less competition) is the whole strategy of this job track.

Which tickets and qualifications do the ads mention?

Percent of ads in each family whose text mentions each requirement. A mention can be "required", "preferred" or just "an advantage"; the matching cannot tell these apart.

Patterns worth noticing:

  • Security ads almost never mention an electrical licence (1%), but often mention a security licence (29%), ACMA cabling registration (27%), a White Card (25%) and a driver's licence (30%). This is the clearest no-electrical-licence entry door.
  • Cabling ads are ticket-driven: ACMA registration in 60% and White Card in 44%. Cheap tickets, quick to get (see C11).
  • BMS and fire ads split: about a fifth to a quarter mention an electrical licence ("dry fire" technician roles are often written for electricians), the rest are open to ELV technicians. Read each ad: many say "licence preferred", not "required".
  • Instrumentation is the biggest family (218 ads) but leans toward qualifications: diploma or degree mentioned in 41%, electrical licence in 22%. Your old ECE diploma is most relevant here and in electronics technician roles (33%).
  • Work rights come up in roughly a fifth of ads in most families, and some government and defence-related sites ask for citizenship or a security clearance.

"Reads drawings" shows up in every family. It is the single most transferable skill on this list, which is why the Practice Book has a whole lab on it (P22). In security, cabling and BMS, "IP networking" is next: every camera, controller and reader is now a network device (P19).

Common misconception

"The median salary is what I'll get." These medians are pulled up by experienced technicians, leading hands and roles that need an electrical licence. A trainee or first-year installer usually starts lower, and pay rises quickly with tickets, platform certifications (for example a Genetec or Gallagher course) and a year of site experience. Many ads also add a vehicle, overtime or allowances, which the base figure leaves out.

Strategy, in one paragraph

The evidence points to a clear first move: electronic security, with data cabling as its partner skill. These ads are among the least crowded, almost never need an electrical licence, ask for tickets you can get in weeks (White Card, ACMA open registration, the state security licence), and value networking skills you may already have. BMS is the natural second step (also uncrowded, and it values the same networking plus BACnet/Modbus), and fire detection is worth a look in your state. Instrumentation has the most ads and the best pay, but it leans toward diplomas and electrical licences, so it is a medium-term target. The 12-week plan in C12 is ordered to match this.

Check yourself

Why does a low applicant count not mean an ad is easy to get?

Because the ad may require tickets (security licence, ACMA registration, driver's licence, work rights) that most applicants lack. Low competition helps only if you meet the hard requirements, which is why the course pairs skills with tickets.

In this sample, which family almost never mentioned an electrical licence, and what did it mention instead?

CCTV / electronic security: 1% mentioned an electrical licence, while about 29% mentioned a security licence, 27% ACMA cabling registration, 25% a White Card and 30% a driver's licence.

Why is the NBN/fibre median (88 applicants) not a fair picture of fibre splicing jobs?

The group has only 19 ads and includes labouring roles such as pit-and-pipe and cable hauling, which attract many applicants. The two skilled splicing ads in it had 1 and 2 applicants when captured.

Which skill appears across every family's tool list?

Reading drawings (plans, schematics, wiring diagrams). IP networking comes a close second in security, cabling and BMS.

A BMS ad says "electrical licence highly regarded". Should you apply without one?

Yes, if you meet the other requirements. "Highly regarded" or "preferred" is not "required". In the sample, about 20% of BMS ads mentioned an electrical licence at all, and many BMS teams split work so electricians do the mains parts and technicians do controls, networks and commissioning.

What does "eligible to obtain before licensed duties" mean in the Fortis ad?

The employer will consider someone who doesn't yet hold the security licence, as long as they can get it (meet the training and probity requirements) before doing work that legally requires it. It is an opening for trainees.
