HVAC for electronics people

A BMS technician does not repair chillers, but must understand what every valve, damper and fan in the plant is trying to do. This chapter explains heating, ventilation and air conditioning from the physics up: the refrigeration cycle, central plant, air handling units, VAV boxes, fan coils, the sensors, and the control loops you will tune. A simulator lets you tune a PID loop yourself.

Heat, temperature and latent heat

HVAC is the business of moving heat. Two kinds of heat matter.

Sensible heat changes temperature. You can "sense" it with a thermometer. The energy needed is:

Q = m · c · ΔT m is mass (kg), c is specific heat capacity (kJ/kg·K), ΔT is the temperature change (K, same size as °C). Water: c ≈ 4.19 kJ/kg·K. Air: c ≈ 1.005 kJ/kg·K, and 1 m³ of air weighs about 1.2 kg.

Latent heat changes state (liquid to gas) without changing temperature. When a liquid boils it soaks up a large amount of energy while staying at the same temperature. Evaporating 1 kg of water at room temperature takes about 2,450 kJ: almost 600 times more than warming that kilogram by 1 K. This is why sweating cools you, and why every air conditioner works by boiling a liquid.

The key fact that makes refrigeration possible: the boiling point depends on pressure. Water boils at 100 °C at sea level but at about 70 °C high on a mountain. A refrigerant can be made to boil at 5 °C by keeping it at low pressure, and to condense at 45 °C by squeezing it to high pressure. Control the pressure and you control where the heat is absorbed and where it is released.

Common misconception

Wrong: "An air conditioner makes cold."
Right: there is no such thing as "cold" to make. An air conditioner is a heat pump: it uses electrical work to move heat from a cool place (the room) to a warm place (outside). That is why the outdoor unit blows hot air, and why the same machine run backwards heats a house.

The refrigeration cycle

Every chiller, split system and fridge uses the same four-part loop. Follow one kilogram of refrigerant round it.

  1. Compressor. Squeezes cool, low-pressure gas into hot, high-pressure gas. This is where the electrical energy goes in.
  2. Condenser. The hot gas passes through a coil cooled by outside air (or by water from a cooling tower). At this high pressure it condenses at about 40–50 °C, releasing its latent heat outside.
  3. Expansion device. A small restriction (expansion valve) drops the pressure suddenly. Some of the liquid flashes to vapour and the mix becomes very cold.
  4. Evaporator. The cold mix passes through a coil over which room air (or chilled water) flows. At this low pressure it boils at about 5 °C, absorbing heat. The gas returns to the compressor.

Reverse the direction (with a reversing valve) and the indoor coil becomes the condenser: you have a heat pump heating the room. Heat pump water heaters and air-to-water heat pumps for building heating use the same idea.

Efficiency: the COP

COP = heat moved / electrical energy in Worked example: a chiller moves 500 kW of heat out of the building and its compressors draw 100 kW. COP = 500 / 100 = 5. It moves five units of heat for each unit of electricity. Indicative values for water-cooled chillers are around 5–7 at good conditions; they fall when the condenser side is hot. Raising the chilled water temperature or lowering the condenser water temperature improves COP, which is one reason BMS reset strategies save energy.
In Australia

Handling refrigerant (connecting gauges, recovering, charging, brazing refrigerant lines) requires a refrigerant handling licence issued by the Australian Refrigeration Council (ARC) under Commonwealth law, and businesses that buy and store refrigerant need a refrigerant trading authorisation. BMS technicians typically do not handle refrigerant: that is the refrigeration mechanic's job. Confirm the current requirements with ARC.

Central plant: chillers, towers, boilers and heat pumps

Large buildings do not put a refrigeration loop in every room. They make chilled water in a central plant and pipe it to coils around the building. Heating is often done the same way with heating hot water.

  • Chillers are either air-cooled (fans on the roof reject the heat) or water-cooled (a condenser water loop takes the heat to a cooling tower). Water-cooled plant is more efficient but needs towers and water treatment.
  • Cooling towers spray warm condenser water over fill while fans pull air through. A small fraction evaporates, and its latent heat cools the rest. The BMS controls tower fans (often with VSDs) to hold condenser water temperature.
  • Pumps circulate each loop, often in duty/standby pairs, increasingly with VSDs controlled from a differential pressure sensor.
  • Boilers (usually gas) or heat pumps make heating hot water. Many Australian buildings are now replacing gas boilers with heat pumps, so you will see both.
Q = ṁ · c · ΔT → ṁ = 60 kW / (4.19 kJ/kg·K × 6 K) ≈ 2.4 kg/s ≈ 2.4 L/s Worked example: an AHU coil removing 60 kW with chilled water going in at 6 °C and out at 12 °C needs about 2.4 litres per second of water. If the BMS sees only a 3 K difference across the coil, either the flow is twice what it needs (wasting pump energy) or the coil is not doing its job.
In Australia

Cooling towers can grow Legionella bacteria, which cause a dangerous pneumonia. State and territory health laws set rules for registering, maintaining, testing and risk-managing cooling towers, and the BMS often monitors tower water treatment and run hours. The details differ by state; confirm with the state health department.

Safety

AHU fans, pumps, chillers and compressors run on 400 V three-phase supplies, and fans can start automatically at any moment under BMS control. Never reach into an AHU or remove panels unless the unit is isolated and locked out under the site's procedure, done by an authorised person. The 400 V supplies are licensed electrician work. Your work is at the sensor and controller (ELV) end.

VAV boxes and fan coil units

VAV boxes

A VAV (variable air volume) box sits in the ceiling and controls how much of the AHU's supply air goes into one zone. It has a damper with an actuator, an airflow sensor (a cross-shaped tube that measures velocity pressure, read by a small DP transducer in the controller), and often a reheat coil or electric heater. The room sensor tells the controller the zone temperature.

Most VAV boxes use pressure-independent cascade control: the temperature loop does not move the damper directly. Instead it sets an airflow setpoint between a minimum and a maximum (say 40–200 L/s), and a second, fast loop moves the damper to hold that airflow. If the duct pressure changes, the airflow loop corrects it before the room even notices.

Sensible cooling (W) ≈ 1.2 × airflow (L/s) × ΔT (K) Worked example: a meeting room gains 2,000 W of heat (people, computers, sun). Supply air is 13 °C and the room should be 23 °C, so ΔT = 10 K. Airflow = 2,000 / (1.2 × 10) ≈ 167 L/s. If the VAV's maximum is set to 120 L/s, it can only remove 1.2 × 120 × 10 = 1,440 W, and the room will be hot whenever full. That is a real cause of "level 5 meeting room is always hot" complaints.

Fan coil units (FCUs)

An FCU is a small fan and coil in a ceiling, wall or cassette, fed with chilled water (and sometimes hot water). A 2-pipe FCU has one coil that gets either chilled or hot water depending on season; a 4-pipe FCU has separate cooling and heating coils. The controller runs the fan (often 3-speed relays or an EC fan with a 0–10 V speed signal) and opens the valve to hold room temperature. Hotels and apartment buildings use many FCUs.

Control loops: on/off, P, PI and PID

Every loop in a BMS follows the same picture: measure, compare with the setpoint, act, and measure again.

On/off with hysteresis

The simplest control: cooling on above 23 °C, off below 21 °C. The 2 K gap is the hysteresis (or differential). Without it, the compressor would switch on and off many times a minute and wear out. On/off is fine for small things (an exhaust fan, a frost heater) but makes temperature swing.

Proportional (P)

Output is proportional to the error. BMS tools often express gain as a proportional band (PB): the error that moves the output from 0 to 100 %. A PB of 4 K means 25 % per K.

output = Kp × error, Kp = 100 % / PB Worked example: PB = 4 K, room 1 K above setpoint → cooling valve 25 % open. Problem: if the room needs 57 % cooling to stay steady, a P-only controller can only give 57 % when the error is 2.3 K. The room settles above setpoint. That permanent error is called offset (or droop).

Integral (I): removing the offset

The integral term adds up error over time. As long as any error remains, it keeps pushing the output further. It stops only when error is zero. BMS tools often give integral time Ti (seconds or minutes) instead of Ki; Ki = Kp / Ti. Too much integral (short Ti) makes the loop overshoot and hunt.

Derivative (D)

The derivative term reacts to how fast the measurement is changing, braking the output before overshoot. It helps fast loops but amplifies sensor noise. Most HVAC loops are PI only, with D set to zero.

u(t) = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt e = measured − setpoint for a cooling loop (hotter room → more cooling). For heating, the sign is reversed; getting the "action" (direct or reverse) wrong makes a loop run away to 0 or 100 % immediately.

Bench tip

A practical tuning habit: start with integral off and raise the gain until the loop just starts to oscillate, then halve the gain. Then add integral slowly (start with a long integral time and shorten it) until the offset disappears in a reasonable time without overshoot. Record the old values before you change anything.

The economy cycle and ventilation

When outside air is cooler than return air, it is free cooling. An economy cycle (economiser) opens the outside air damper beyond the minimum and closes the return air damper, so the building is cooled by outside air before the chilled water valve opens. Simple versions compare temperatures (dry-bulb changeover); better versions compare enthalpy (total heat, including moisture), because humid outside air can be cool but still add a lot of latent load to the cooling coil.

Example logic: "If the AHU is in cooling and outside air temperature is at least 2 K below return air, and outside humidity is acceptable, modulate the outside air damper to hold supply air setpoint; only when the damper is fully open, start opening the chilled water valve." In Melbourne's climate the economy cycle runs for many hours a year; in humid tropical places like Darwin, outside air is rarely cool and dry enough to help.

Whatever the economy cycle does, the AHU must never go below the minimum outside air needed for ventilation. Many buildings use CO₂-based demand-controlled ventilation: when return CO₂ rises (more people), the minimum outside air rises too.

The Australian context

In Australia

NCC Section J. The National Construction Code (Volume One) Section J sets energy efficiency requirements for commercial buildings, including HVAC controls such as time switches, and, for some system sizes and climate zones, economy cycles; it also requires facilities for energy monitoring in larger buildings. The requirements depend on building class, size and climate zone, and change with each edition, so check the current NCC published by the Australian Building Codes Board (ABCB).

NABERS Energy. The National Australian Built Environment Rating System rates a building's measured energy performance over 12 months, on a scale up to 6 stars. Large office spaces offered for sale or lease must disclose a rating under the Commercial Building Disclosure program. Owners care a great deal about their rating, and BMS tuning (schedules, resets, removing forgotten overrides) is one of the cheapest ways to improve it. Confirm current rules and thresholds with NABERS and the CBD program.

Ventilation. Minimum outside air rates for mechanical ventilation are set by AS 1668.2 (referenced by the NCC). Refrigerant work needs an ARC licence, as above. Rules change and differ by state; always check the current version with the regulator.

On the job

Most HVAC complaints arrive as "too hot" or "too cold". A good BMS tech works from the zone up: room sensor reading correct? VAV or FCU doing what its controller asks? Is the AHU delivering the right supply air? Is the central plant delivering chilled water at the right temperature? The answer is usually found by reading trends before climbing a ladder. When the fault is mechanical (a refrigeration fault, a seized pump bearing), you hand it to the mechanical service tech with good evidence.

Interview questions

"Explain the refrigeration cycle in one minute."

The compressor raises refrigerant pressure; in the condenser the hot gas condenses and releases heat outside; the expansion valve drops the pressure; in the evaporator the cold liquid boils and absorbs heat from the air or water; the gas returns to the compressor. It moves heat using latent heat and the pressure–boiling point relationship.

"What's the difference between P and PI control, and why does it matter?"

P-only output is proportional to error, so it needs some error to produce output and leaves an offset. Adding integral accumulates error over time and removes the offset. Too much integral causes overshoot and hunting.

"What is an economy cycle?"

Using cool outside air for free cooling by opening the outside air damper above minimum, before mechanical cooling, when outside conditions (temperature or enthalpy) are better than return air.

"How does a VAV box control temperature?"

The zone temperature loop sets an airflow setpoint between min and max; a fast airflow loop moves the damper to hold that airflow (pressure-independent). Reheat is used if the zone is cold at minimum airflow.

"Do you need a licence to work on HVAC in Australia?"

Refrigerant handling needs an ARC licence; mains electrical work needs a state electrical licence. BMS controls work on the ELV side may not need either, depending on the role and state, but check the employer's requirements and the regulator.

Check yourself

Why does an evaporator absorb so much heat without the refrigerant getting warmer?

Because the refrigerant is boiling. While changing state from liquid to gas, it absorbs latent heat at constant temperature (set by its pressure). Latent heat is far larger than sensible heat for the same mass.

An AHU supplies 4,000 L/s of air at 13 °C to rooms at 23 °C. Roughly how much sensible cooling is that?

1.2 × 4,000 × 10 = 48,000 W ≈ 48 kW.

A loop has a proportional band of 5 K and no integral. The room needs 60 % cooling to hold steady. Where does it settle?

Kp = 100/5 = 20 %/K. 60 % output needs an error of 60/20 = 3 K, so it settles about 3 K above setpoint. Integral action would remove this offset.

A chiller rejects 600 kW at the condenser while drawing 100 kW. How much cooling does it provide and what is its COP?

Energy balance: heat rejected = cooling + compressor work, so cooling = 600 − 100 = 500 kW, and COP = 500/100 = 5.

Why is humid outside air at 20 °C not always good for an economy cycle?

Its moisture carries latent heat. The cooling coil must condense that moisture out, which can cost more energy than cooling drier return air. Enthalpy-based economy control accounts for this.

A heating valve loop is set to "direct acting" by mistake (output rises as temperature rises). What will the trend show?

As the room warms, the valve opens more, making it warmer still: the valve runs to 100 % and the room overheats. When cold, the valve shuts. Wrong action is a classic commissioning error, fixed by reversing the loop action.

Who in Australia may recover and recharge refrigerant in a chiller?

A person holding the appropriate refrigerant handling licence from the Australian Refrigeration Council (typically a refrigeration mechanic). A BMS technician without that licence must not.
