Soldering, crimping and wiring like a tradie

How solder actually works

Solder is not glue. When molten solder touches clean, hot copper, a thin layer of new metal compound (an intermetallic of tin and copper) forms at the boundary. That is a true metallic bond, and it is why a good joint conducts as well as the wire itself. For the bond to form, two things must happen together:

  1. Both surfaces must be hot enough: above the solder's melting point, the pad and the component lead.
  2. Both surfaces must be clean: copper grows an oxide skin in air, and solder will not bond to oxide. Flux (the resin in the solder's core) chemically strips the oxide while you solder, and helps the solder flow. Flux burns off within a few seconds, which is why you cannot take solder to the joint on the iron tip and expect it to work.

When it works, molten solder wets the metal: it spreads out in a thin film and climbs up the lead, like water on clean glass. When it fails, solder beads up like water on a waxed car. Wetting is the whole game.

Why set the iron about 150 °C above the melting point? Because the joint steals heat. The tip temperature is a reservoir: the bigger the joint (a thick wire, a ground plane), the faster it drains. For bigger joints use a bigger tip, which holds and delivers more heat, rather than cranking the temperature to 450 °C, which burns the flux off before it can work and can lift copper pads off the board.

Why it works

Heat flows from hot to cold at a rate proportional to the temperature difference and the contact area (much as current flows in proportion to voltage). A large contact area, made by a wet blob of solder bridging tip and joint, transfers heat far faster than a dry point. That is why a freshly tinned tip and a tiny "heat bridge" of solder at the contact point make joints quick and easy.

Tinning and tip care

The tip is iron-plated copper. The plating is wetted by solder; if it oxidises (goes black or grey and solder slides off), heat transfer collapses. Rules:

  • Tin the tip when it first heats: melt a little solder all over the working end so it is silver and shiny.
  • Clean, then solder. Wipe the tip in the brass wool just before each joint or two. Brass wool cleans without cooling the tip as much as a wet sponge.
  • Park it tinned. Before putting the iron in its stand, add a fresh blob of solder. The blob protects the tip from oxidising while it waits.
  • Turn it down or off when not soldering for more than a few minutes; many stations have a standby mode.
  • Never file or sand a plated tip. If it is blackened, a tip-tinner paste can restore it; otherwise replace it.

The method: heat the joint, then feed the solder

  1. Prepare. Glasses on, extraction on, iron at ~350 °C (leaded) or ~370 °C (lead-free). Bend the component legs slightly outward under the board so the part does not fall out.

  2. Clean and wet the tip. Wipe it in the brass wool. Touch a tiny bit of solder to the tip so it is shiny.

  3. Heat. Place the tip so it touches both the pad and the lead at once. Hold for about 1–2 seconds.

  4. Feed. Touch the solder wire to the joint on the opposite side from the tip. It should melt and flow around the lead within a second. Feed about 2–4 mm of 0.7 mm solder for a normal pad.

  5. Finish. Pull the solder away, then lift the iron straight off. Keep everything still for 2–3 seconds.

  6. Inspect, then trim. Look at the joint (next section). Then cut the lead just above the top of the solder cone with flush cutters, holding the leg so it does not fly.

Bench tip

Count it out loud: "heat one, feed two, off three". Most beginner problems come from rushing step 3 (the joint is not hot enough) or from melting solder onto the tip and dabbing it on (the flux has already burned away). If a joint goes wrong, do not keep heating it. Stop, add a touch of flux, and try again cleanly.

Common misconception

Wrong: "More solder makes a stronger joint."
Right: The strength and the conductivity are in the thin wetted layer, not the bulk. A huge blob hides whether the lead underneath is wetted at all, which is exactly how dry joints sneak through. Use just enough to form a concave fillet.

On the job

Intermittent faults ("the reader works until someone slams the door", "the camera drops out when the wind blows") are very often a dry joint or a poor crimp somewhere. Field technicians rarely solder circuit boards (faulty boards are swapped), but you will solder cable joins, repair leads, fit connectors and build small interface boards, and you will find bad joints by inspecting and wiggling while watching a meter.

Desoldering

Everyone makes mistakes. Two tools remove solder:

  • Desoldering braid (wick): fine copper braid soaked in flux. Lay it on the joint, press the iron on top of it, and molten solder is drawn up into the braid by capillary action. Cut off the used, silver section and repeat. Best for clearing bridges and flattening pads.
  • Solder sucker (pump): cock the spring plunger, melt the joint, bring the nozzle in alongside the tip, and press the button. It sucks the molten solder out of the hole. Best for clearing through-holes.
Bench tip

Old or factory joints often resist removal. Add fresh solder first: it brings fresh flux and makes a bigger molten pool that conducts heat better. Then braid or pump it out. It feels backwards, but it works every time.

Build: the P02 circuit on stripboard

Stripboard (often called Veroboard) has a grid of holes at 0.1 inch (2.54 mm) pitch, with copper strips on the underside running in one direction. Every hole on a strip is connected, like a very long breadboard strip. Where you need to break a strip, you cut the copper at a hole with a track cutter or a 3–4 mm drill bit twisted by hand. We will build two LEDs, each with its own resistor (the correct parallel circuit from P02), sharing the supply strips.

  1. Plan on paper first. Copy Figure 3 onto grid paper. Mark every cut with an X. Count holes twice; the board is mirrored when you turn it over, which catches everyone once.

  2. Cut the track at D6 from the copper side. Twist the cutter or drill bit by hand until the copper ring around the hole is completely gone. Check under a light, then confirm with the continuity beeper: D5 to D7 must not beep.

  3. Fit the lowest parts first: resistors, then LEDs (long leg into strip D). Bend legs on the copper side to hold them.

  4. Solder each leg using the method above. Trim legs. Inspect every joint under a light or magnifier.

  5. Check for bridges: run the continuity probe between neighbouring strips along the board. A to B, B to C... none should beep. A to F must read as open (or very high): if it beeps, the supply would be shorted.

  6. Solder the supply leads (red to A1, black to F1) and power up from the 3×AA pack. Measure each LED current by measuring the voltage across its resistor and dividing by R: about 8.5 mA for 330 Ω, and for the green LED on 220 Ω about (4.7 − 2.1)/220 ≈ 12 mA if it is an older yellow-green type, or (4.7 − 3.0)/220 ≈ 7.7 mA if it is a modern true-green LED near 3 V.

Try this

Bridge the cut at D6 with a blob of solder and power up. Watch the green LED dim or go out as the red LED hogs the current: the P02 lesson, now on a permanent board. Remove the bridge with braid, and confirm D5–D7 no longer beeps.

Wire: solid or stranded, and how big

Solid vs stranded

Solid core

One thick conductor. Holds its shape, goes cleanly into breadboards and insulation-displacement connectors (like Cat6 keystones and punch-down blocks). Breaks if flexed repeatedly. Used for fixed runs in walls and ceilings.

Stranded

Many fine strands. Flexible and tolerant of vibration and movement: patch leads, door loops, equipment wiring, anything that moves. Strands splay in screw terminals, so they need ferrules.

mm², AWG and strand notation

In Australia, wire is sized by its copper cross-sectional area in mm². American products (and many datasheets) use AWG (American Wire Gauge), where a bigger number means a thinner wire, and every 3 gauges roughly halves the area. Security cable is often described by strands: "14/0.2" means 14 strands of 0.2 mm diameter, so the area is 14 × π × (0.1 mm)² ≈ 0.44 mm².

Safety

Cheap cable is sometimes copper-clad aluminium (CCA): aluminium with a thin copper skin. It has roughly 1.5 times the resistance of copper of the same size, runs hotter, and breaks more easily. It is not accepted for many installations (for example Power over Ethernet and structured cabling). Scrape a cut end: silver-grey under the copper colour means CCA.

Worked example. A 12 V maglock draws 0.5 A, 50 m from the power supply, on 14/0.2 (0.44 mm²) cable. Loop resistance = 2 × 50 × (0.0172/0.44) = 3.9 Ω. Drop = 0.5 × 3.9 = 1.95 V, so the lock gets about 10 V (a 16% drop) and its holding force falls. On 1.5 mm²: 2 × 50 × 0.0115 = 1.15 Ω, drop 0.57 V (4.8%): fine. Or run a 24 V lock instead: same power at half the current, 0.25 A, and on 0.44 mm² the drop is 0.98 V of 24 V, only 4%. Doubling the voltage quarters the percentage drop for the same power, which is why 24 V systems are popular in buildings.

Terminations: ferrules, lugs and heatshrink

Bootlace ferrules

A bootlace ferrule is a thin metal tube with a plastic collar, crimped onto the end of a stranded wire. In a screw terminal, bare strands splay out, some get cut by the screw, and stray strands can short to the next terminal. A ferrule turns the strands into one solid pin that the terminal clamps properly. Almost every BMS controller, access-control panel and alarm panel uses screw or spring terminals, and neat ferrules are the first thing a supervisor looks at.

  • Size: match the ferrule to the wire (0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5 mm² ...). Too big and the crimp will not grip; too small and strands are cut off to fit. Twin ferrules take two wires into one terminal neatly.
  • Crimp profile: square or hexagonal ratchet crimpers both work. Pliers do not; the crimp must be compressed evenly all round.
  • Tug test every crimp. If it moves, cut it off and redo it.
  • Why not solder the end instead? Solder slowly flows under the constant pressure of a screw (it "creeps"), so the terminal loosens over months. A loose terminal has high resistance, and with current flowing it heats up. Ferrules do not creep.

Insulated crimp lugs and splices

Ring, fork (spade) and butt-splice terminals with plastic sleeves are used for battery leads, earth points, studs and joins. Their insulation is commonly colour-coded by wire size: red for about 0.5–1.5 mm², blue for about 1.5–2.5 mm², yellow for about 4–6 mm² (check the packet). Use the crimper's matching coloured die, and prefer a ring terminal on a screw stud, because it cannot slip off if the screw loosens.

Heatshrink

Heatshrink tubing shrinks to about half its diameter (2:1) or a third (3:1) when heated. It insulates and stiffens solder joints in wire, so the wire does not break at the edge of the solder. Pick a size that slides over the joint before shrinking and grips tightly after. Adhesive-lined (dual-wall) heatshrink seals out water for outdoor joints. Use a heat gun, move it steadily, and stop when the tube is snug.

Bench tip

Put the heatshrink on the wire before you solder the joint, and slide it well away from the heat until you are ready. Every technician has finished a beautiful joint and then seen the heatshrink sitting on the bench. For an inline splice: strip 8–10 mm, slide on heatshrink, twist or lay the strands together, solder (heat the wires, feed solder until it soaks in), let it cool, centre the tube, shrink.

Cable management: ties, labels and looms

Neat work is easier to fault-find, safer, and is what gets you hired again. The habits:

  • Plan the route. Run cables in straight lines with square corners, parallel, not crossing over each other. Leave a small service loop at the device so it can be re-terminated once or twice.
  • Cable ties: snug, not crushing. Cut the tail flush with a proper tie tool or flush cutters; a sharp angled tail cuts the next person's hand. Space ties evenly.
  • Data cable is different. Over-tightened ties deform Cat6 pairs and spoil its performance. Use hook-and-loop (Velcro) ties on data bundles, and respect the minimum bend radius (P20).
  • Label both ends of every cable, with the same label, using a scheme that matches the drawings (for example the controller, the input number and the device). Printed labels last; handwriting fades.
  • Keep ELV and data away from mains. The wiring rules and the telecommunications cabling rules require segregation between them (by distance or barriers). In a shared cabinet, run ELV in its own duct. C07 covers the details.
  • Strip only what you need. No bare copper visible outside a terminal, and the outer sheath taken into the cabinet so it does not chafe.
On the job

A typical day for a new BMS or security technician includes terminating a dozen field cables onto a controller: strip, ferrule, land each core on the right terminal per the drawing, label, tie into the loom and test. Supervisors judge quickly: ferrules on every stranded core, no nicked copper, labels that match the drawing, and a loom you could photograph for the company website. Take photos of your own practice terminations for your portfolio (C12).

In Australia

Cabling that connects to the telecommunications network (including data cabling in buildings) must be done by a registered cabler under the ACMA cabling provider rules, to the standard AS/CA S009. Mains wiring is licensed electrical work. ELV security and BMS field wiring is generally done by security or BMS technicians, but licences and rules differ by state and by system. Confirm current requirements with ACMA, your state's electrical regulator, and your state's security licensing body (C07, C11).

Safety: fumes, lead and burns

Safety

Fumes. The smoke from soldering is mostly burnt flux. Rosin (colophony) flux fume is a recognised respiratory sensitiser: repeated exposure can cause occupational asthma that lasts for life. Do not breathe the smoke. Use a fume extractor at the joint or at least a fan that draws the smoke away from your face towards an open window, and do not lean over the joint.

Lead. At soldering temperatures, lead does not evaporate in meaningful amounts; the risk is swallowing it from your hands. Wash your hands with soap after soldering and before eating, drinking or smoking. No food or drinks at the bench. Keep solder away from children. Lead-free solder avoids lead but still produces flux fume.

Burns. The tip is 350 °C+ and molten solder sticks to skin. Always return the iron to its stand; never leave it on the bench. If it falls, let it fall. Freshly soldered joints, heat gun nozzles and just-shrunk heatshrink stay hot for a while. For a burn: cool running water for 20 minutes.

Eyes. Glasses on for soldering and especially for trimming legs; solder can spit and clipped legs fly.

In Australia

Workplaces must control exposure to hazards like soldering fume under WHS laws; Safe Work Australia publishes guidance, and your state's WHS regulator enforces it. On site, expect a safe work method statement (SWMS) or job safety analysis for hot work, and follow any hot-work permit system in the building.

Check yourself

Why should solder be fed into the joint on the side away from the iron, not onto the tip?

If the solder melts when touched to the joint itself, the joint is hot enough to be wetted. Solder melted on the tip and carried over loses its flux (it burns off in seconds) and lands on a surface that may be too cold, giving a dry or cold joint.

A joint is dull, grainy and lumpy. Another has a neat blob on the pad but a visible ring around the lead. Name them and the fix.

The first is a cold joint (not hot enough, or moved while freezing): add flux and reheat until it flows smooth. The second is a dry (non-wetted) joint: the lead was dirty or oxidised, or the flux was gone. Remove the solder, clean or flux the lead, and solder again heating both pad and lead.

Your lead-free joints look satin rather than mirror-bright. Is that a problem?

No. Lead-free (tin-silver-copper) solder normally freezes with a satin, slightly dull surface. Judge it by its shape: smooth concave fillet, wetted pad and lead. Only leaded solder should be shiny.

What is the loop resistance of 30 m of 0.75 mm² copper twin cable, and the drop at 1 A?

Ω/m = 0.0172 / 0.75 = 0.0229. Loop = 2 × 30 × 0.0229 = 1.38 Ω. Drop = 1 A × 1.38 Ω = 1.38 V: 11% of 12 V (too much for many devices) but under 6% of 24 V.

Why not just tin the end of a stranded wire with solder before putting it in a screw terminal?

Solder creeps (slowly deforms) under the steady pressure of the screw, so the clamp loosens over time. The loose connection has higher resistance, heats under current, and can fail or become a fire risk. A crimped bootlace ferrule stays tight.

You have finished the stripboard. A and F strips beep together on continuity. What do you check?

A short between supply strips: look for a solder bridge between adjacent strips, a copper sliver from the track cutting, or a part in the wrong holes. Check strip by strip (A–B, B–C, ...) to find which adjacent pair is bridged, then clear it with braid or a clean tip.

What does "14/0.2" mean, and roughly what AWG is it?

14 strands, each 0.2 mm in diameter. Area = 14 × π × 0.1² ≈ 0.44 mm², which is between 22 AWG (0.33 mm²) and 20 AWG (0.52 mm²), closer to 21 AWG.

Which is the bigger health risk when soldering with leaded solder: lead vapour or the smoke, and what controls each?

The smoke (burnt rosin flux) is the main airborne hazard; it can cause occupational asthma. Control it with fume extraction at the joint. Lead does not vaporise much at soldering temperatures; its risk is ingestion, controlled by washing hands and keeping food and drink off the bench.

  • I built the two-LED circuit on stripboard with a verified track cut.
