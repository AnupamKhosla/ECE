Transformers

Two coils share one magnetic core, and AC on one appears on the other at a different voltage, with no wire joining them. That one idea built the power grid, makes your plug-pack safe, shrinks a laptop charger into your pocket and lets an energy meter measure 100 A without touching the cable. By the end you can size a 24 V AC control transformer and explain each of its losses.

Your safety line

In this course you only ever use sealed plug-in transformers (plug-packs) and the extra-low-voltage side of equipment. Connecting, opening or testing anything on the 230 V side of a transformer is mains work, and in Australia that is legally limited to licensed electricians. When this chapter mentions 230 V, it is to explain the physics, not to invite you to touch it.

A short history: Faraday's ring to the grid

The first transformer was the iron ring Faraday wound in 1831 (T06): two coils on one ring, a battery on one, a galvanometer on the other. It only gave a kick when the current changed, so it was a curiosity while electricity was DC. When AC generators appeared, that "only when it changes" property became the whole point: AC is always changing.

In the early 1880s Lucien Gaulard and John Dixon Gibbs demonstrated an open-core "secondary generator" for lighting. In 1884–85 three engineers at the Ganz works in Budapest, Károly Zipernowsky, Ottó Bláthy and Miksa Déri, built closed-iron-core transformers and connected loads in parallel, the basis of modern distribution. In 1886 William Stanley, working for George Westinghouse, lit shops in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, with an AC system that stepped voltage up and down with transformers. Together with Tesla's AC motor (T07), this is why the world's grids are AC: transformers let you send power at high voltage and low current, then bring it down to a safe level near the user.

How a transformer works: mutual induction

A transformer is two (or more) coils wound on one closed core of magnetic steel or ferrite. The coil you drive is the primary; the coil you take power from is the secondary.

  1. An AC voltage on the primary drives a small AC current, which makes an alternating flux Φ in the core (T06).
  2. The core guides almost all of that flux through the secondary as well. This sharing of flux is mutual induction.
  3. The changing flux induces an EMF in every turn of both coils, and it is the same EMF per turn in each, because each turn sees the same dΦ/dt.
Vp / Np = Vs / Ns = dΦ/dt → Vs / Vp = Ns / Np Np, Ns are the numbers of turns on primary and secondary. "Volts per turn" is the same on both sides. More secondary turns: step-up. Fewer: step-down.

The flux is set by the primary voltage, not by the load

This is the key insight that most short explanations skip. For a sine wave, Faraday's law gives the peak flux the core must carry:

Vrms = 4.44 · f · N · Bpeak · A A is the core's cross-section area in m², Bpeak the peak flux density in tesla, f the frequency. (4.44 = 2π/√2.) This "transformer equation" fixes how many turns a winding needs for a given core.

Because the supply voltage and frequency are fixed, the core flux is fixed too, whatever the load. So what happens when you connect a load? The secondary current makes its own flux, which (by Lenz's law) tries to reduce the core flux. The instant the flux starts to drop, the primary's back-EMF drops a little below the supply voltage, and the primary draws exactly enough extra current to cancel the secondary's flux. The core flux stays put; the primary current follows the secondary current. That is how power gets "pulled" across the gap.

With no load, the primary still draws a small magnetising current to keep the flux going. This current lags the voltage by nearly 90° (the primary is just an inductor), so it carries very little real power. That is why an unloaded transformer is only slightly warm.

Turns ratio, current ratio and power conservation

An ideal transformer has no losses, so power in equals power out. If the voltage steps down by a factor, the current must step up by the same factor:

Vp Ip = Vs Is → Is / Ip = Np / Ns (ampere-turns balance: NpIp = NsIs) The secondary of a step-down transformer uses thicker wire, because it carries more current. A side effect: an impedance on the secondary looks (Np/Ns)² times bigger from the primary. Audio and RF engineers use this to "match" impedances.

Worked example: a 230 V → 24 V AC, 40 VA plug-in transformer

This is the kind of sealed plug-pack you will use in the labs, and the same rating as many 24 V AC control transformers in buildings.

  1. Turns ratio. Np : Ns = 230 : 24 ≈ 9.6 : 1. Real designs add a few per cent extra secondary turns, because the voltage sags under load (see "regulation" below).
  2. Rated secondary current. The rating "40 VA" is apparent power (T07). Is = 40 VA / 24 V ≈ 1.67 A.
  3. Primary current at full load (ideal). Ip = 40 VA / 230 V ≈ 0.17 A, or Is / 9.6. The real figure is a little higher because of losses and magnetising current.
  4. Number of turns. Suppose the steel core's centre limb is 2 cm × 2 cm (A = 4 × 10⁻⁴ m²) and the designer allows Bpeak = 1.2 T. Np = 230 / (4.44 × 50 × 1.2 × 4 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 2160 turns. Volts per turn = 230 / 2160 ≈ 0.107 V. Ns ≈ 24 / 0.107 ≈ 225 turns, plus a few per cent for regulation.

Why VA and not watts? Because the transformer heats according to its current, whatever the phase. A 40 VA transformer feeding a load at power factor 0.7 can only deliver 28 W of real power, but it is still fully loaded.

Why DC does not work through a transformer

A transformer only couples a changing flux. Apply steady DC to the primary and you get one brief kick in the secondary as the current builds up, then nothing: dΦ/dt = 0, so the secondary voltage is zero.

Worse, the primary itself is in trouble. On AC its current is limited mainly by its reactance (XL = 2πfL, typically hundreds of ohms or more for a mains winding). On DC the reactance is zero, so only the copper resistance limits the current: often a few tens of ohms or less. The current becomes many times the design value, the core saturates, and the winding overheats in seconds. That is the physics behind the rule "never put DC on a transformer winding".

This blocking of DC is also a feature. Transformers pass AC signals while blocking DC and keeping the two sides electrically separate. Every RJ45 Ethernet port contains small signal transformers ("magnetics") for exactly this reason.

On the job: PoE and the Ethernet transformer

Power over Ethernet (P19) sends DC power to a camera down the same pairs that carry data. How does DC get past the port's transformers? It is fed into the centre tap of each winding. On a pair, the DC splits equally between the two wires and flows in opposite directions through the two halves of the winding, so its magnetic effects cancel and the core does not saturate. The data signal, which is a voltage between the two wires, still couples through normally. Meanwhile the transformer isolates the camera's electronics from the cable (Ethernet ports are tested to withstand 1500 V RMS between cable and device).

Losses, laminations and why transformers hum

Real transformers waste some energy, which appears as heat. There are two families of loss.

Copper loss (load loss)

The windings have resistance, so they heat as I²R. Copper loss grows with the square of the load current: at half load it is a quarter of the full-load figure. It is also the main cause of regulation: the secondary voltage falls as load rises, because some voltage is lost in the winding resistance. Small transformers have relatively high winding resistance, so a 24 V plug-pack might read 27–30 V with no load and 24 V at full load. That is normal.

Core loss (iron loss)

Core loss happens whenever the transformer is energised, even with no load, because the flux is always cycling.

  • Hysteresis loss: the energy spent flipping the core's magnetic domains every cycle, equal to the area of the B–H loop (T06). Grain-oriented silicon steel has a thin loop.
  • Eddy-current loss: the core is a conductor sitting inside a changing flux, so currents circulate in it, like the magnet in the copper pipe. In a solid core these loops are large and the I²R heat is serious. The cure is to build the core from thin steel sheets (laminations, typically about 0.3–0.5 mm thick) insulated from each other by a coating. The flux runs along the sheets, but the eddy currents would have to cross the insulation to make big loops, so they are confined to tiny paths. Eddy loss falls roughly with the square of lamination thickness.

Why transformers hum at 100 Hz

Steel changes length very slightly when magnetised (magnetostriction). It stretches whether the flux points one way or the other, so it vibrates twice per cycle: at 100 Hz in Australia. Loose laminations add a rattle. A transformer that hums louder than usual, or buzzes, is often overloaded or has loose mounting.

Common misconception

"A transformer with no load connected uses no power." It still draws magnetising current and has core loss every cycle. An old-style iron plug-pack left plugged in with nothing attached stays warm and might waste a watt or two continuously. Modern switch-mode plug-packs have very low no-load consumption.

Isolation: why a transformer makes things safer

Mains in Australia is supplied with the neutral connected to earth (the MEN system, T09). The active conductor is therefore about 230 V above the ground you stand on. If you touch active, current can flow through you to earth and back to the transformer at the substation. That is how most electric shocks happen.

A transformer's secondary has no conductive connection to the primary. Energy crosses only as a magnetic field through insulation. If the secondary is not connected to earth anywhere, touching one secondary wire gives no complete path, because the current has no way back. Combine this with a low secondary voltage and a well-insulated construction and you get a power source that stays safe even if the mains side has a fault. Plug-packs and the power supplies for ELV equipment are built as safety isolating transformers, with reinforced or double insulation between the windings so that a single insulation failure cannot put mains on the low-voltage side. The relevant standards family is AS/NZS 61558 (safety of transformers); check the current edition if you ever need to specify one.

Common misconception

"An isolated output can never hurt you." Isolation removes the path to earth, but the secondary voltage itself can still hurt you if it is high, and touching both secondary wires completes the circuit. Isolation transformers used by electricians for testing are tools for licensed workers, not a licence to touch mains. For you, the safety comes from the combination: isolated and extra-low voltage and sealed.

In Australia

Buy plug-packs that carry the Regulatory Compliance Mark (the RCM, a tick inside a triangle), which shows the supplier claims compliance with Australian electrical safety requirements. Cheap unbranded imports from online marketplaces sometimes have poor insulation between windings. Jaycar and Altronics sell compliant 12 V, 16 V and 24 V AC plug-packs. Electrical equipment safety schemes are run by the state regulators, so details vary; check your state's regulator if you are supplying equipment to others.

Switch-mode supplies: why the transformer got tiny

Look at the transformer equation again: N · A = V / (4.44 · f · Bpeak). For a given voltage, the product of turns and core area is inversely proportional to frequency. Raise the frequency and you need fewer turns on a smaller core.

Worked comparison. For a 24 V winding:

  • At 50 Hz on a steel core of 4 cm² at 1.2 T: N = 24 / (4.44 × 50 × 1.2 × 4 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 225 turns.
  • At 100 kHz on a ferrite core of 0.5 cm² at 0.2 T (ferrite saturates at a lower flux, and losses rise at high frequency, so designers use a modest B): N = 24 / (4.44 × 100 000 × 0.2 × 0.5 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 5.4 turns.

The 12 V and 24 V DC supplies that power cameras, access control and BMS controllers are built this way. Their 230 V input is wired by an electrician; their output terminals are your side. You will compare a switch-mode supply with a linear one in P10.

Current transformers (CTs)

Turn the transformer idea around. Pass a single conductor through a ring-shaped core, and wind many turns (N) on the ring. The conductor is a primary of one turn. By ampere-turn balance, the secondary current is the primary current divided by N. This is a current transformer, and it measures large AC currents without any electrical connection to the conductor.

Is = Ip / N Vout = Is × Rburden The resistor across the secondary that turns the current into a measurable voltage is called the burden.

Worked example. An energy monitor uses a 100 A : 50 mA CT (N = 2000). The load draws 40 A. Is = 40 / 2000 = 20 mA. Across a 10 Ω burden: V = 0.02 × 10 = 0.2 V RMS, a safe small signal for the meter's ADC (P12).

Split-core CTs open like a clothes peg and clip around an existing cable, which is why they are used for retrofit energy monitoring, building sub-metering on a BMS, and solar and home-battery systems that need to know how much power the house is importing or exporting.

Safety

Two hard rules. First: fitting a CT around conductors inside a switchboard is LV work for a licensed electrician; your part is the low-voltage signal wiring afterwards. Second: a CT with a current output (for example a 5 A or 1 A secondary) must never be left open-circuit while its primary carries current. The CT tries to force its current through whatever is connected; with no burden, the secondary voltage rises to hundreds of volts or more, which can kill and destroys the CT's insulation. Such CTs are installed with shorting links. CTs with a built-in burden that output a small voltage (such as 333 mV) avoid this hazard, which is one reason they are popular for monitoring.

Common misconception

"A CT can measure battery current." No: a CT is a transformer, so it only works on AC. In a battery management system (also called a BMS, confusingly) the DC charge and discharge current is measured with a shunt (a precise low-value resistor, millivolts per amp) or a Hall-effect sensor, which measures the magnetic field directly and works on DC. The CTs in a solar-battery installation sit on the AC side, measuring grid and household current.

24 V AC control transformers in buildings

Walk into a plant room or the back of an access control cabinet and you will find small transformers stepping 230 V down to 24 V AC (sometimes 12, 16 or 18 V AC). Why AC, and why 24 V?

  • It is extra-low voltage, so the field wiring to thermostats, valve actuators and dampers can be run by non-electricians in many situations, and a touch is not dangerous.
  • 24 V is high enough to drive relays, actuators and solenoid valves over long cable runs without excessive current or voltage drop.
  • A transformer is simple and tough: no electronics to fail, it survives surges, and it naturally limits fault current through its winding resistance.
  • HVAC history: thermostats and controls standardised on 24 V AC decades ago, and the parts ecosystem (actuators, relays, controllers) followed.

Typical sizes are 20 VA to 100 VA (40 VA and 75 VA are common). Many intruder alarm panels are powered from a 16–18 V AC plug-pack and rectify it internally to run the panel and charge a 12 V backup battery.

Worked example: a VA budget

A small BMS panel runs one controller (10 VA), four valve actuators (6 VA each) and two damper actuators (8 VA each), all on 24 V AC. Total = 10 + 24 + 16 = 50 VA. Add about 25 % margin: 62.5 VA. A 75 VA transformer is the right size; a 40 VA one would overheat or trip its protection. Secondary current at 50 VA = 50 / 24 ≈ 2.1 A, which also tells you the fuse and cable size on the 24 V side. Always use the actuator's VA rating from its datasheet: some actuators draw more while driving than while holding.

On the job

Two classic 24 V AC faults. 1. Swapped "24 V" and "common". Many controllers and actuators share one transformer and use the common terminal as their signal reference. If one device is wired with 24 V and common reversed, the shared reference can short the transformer through the devices, blowing fuses or damaging boards. Keep polarity consistent and follow the manufacturer's wiring diagram, including whether the common is to be earthed. 2. Mixing AC and DC devices. A 24 V DC-only device on 24 V AC may be destroyed, and an AC coil on DC can burn out (T07). Read the label every time.

Check yourself

A transformer has 1150 primary turns and 120 secondary turns. With 230 V on the primary, what is the secondary voltage, and is it step-up or step-down?

Vs = 230 × 120 / 1150 = 24 V. Step-down: fewer secondary turns means fewer volts, because each turn gets the same volts per turn (0.2 V here).

A 24 V, 40 VA transformer supplies 1.5 A. What is the ideal primary current at 230 V, and is the transformer overloaded?

Load = 24 × 1.5 = 36 VA, within the 40 VA rating (90 %). Ideal primary current = 36 / 230 ≈ 0.157 A (or 1.5 / 9.58). Real primary current is slightly higher because of losses and magnetising current.

Why does a transformer not work on DC, and why can DC damage it?

Induction needs a changing flux; steady DC gives dΦ/dt = 0, so no secondary voltage after the first instant. The primary's current is then limited only by its small copper resistance (no reactance at DC), so it draws far too much current, saturates the core and overheats.

Why is a 50 Hz transformer core made of thin laminations rather than a solid block of steel?

The core is a conductor in a changing flux, so eddy currents circulate in it and waste power as heat. Insulated thin sheets confine those currents to narrow paths with small EMF and high resistance, cutting eddy-current loss dramatically (roughly with the square of sheet thickness).

Why are switch-mode transformers so much smaller than 50 Hz ones of the same power?

From V = 4.44 f N B A, the turns × core area needed for a given voltage is inversely proportional to frequency. At 100 kHz instead of 50 Hz (2000 times higher), even allowing for ferrite's lower usable flux density, the transformer needs far fewer turns on a far smaller core.

Someone clamps an energy-monitor CT around a whole two-core cable and reads almost zero. Why?

The active and neutral carry equal currents in opposite directions, so their fields cancel inside the CT and the net flux is near zero. A CT must enclose only one conductor. (An RCD deliberately uses this cancellation to detect leakage, see T09.)

Why must a current-output CT never be open-circuited while its primary is carrying current?

The CT forces a secondary current set by the primary current. With no burden, there is nothing to carry that current, so the core is driven hard and the secondary voltage rises to very high, dangerous levels, which can shock someone and break down the insulation. Use shorting links or voltage-output CTs.

Can you measure a home battery's DC charging current with a split-core CT?

No. A CT only responds to changing current. DC current needs a shunt resistor or a Hall-effect sensor. CTs in solar-battery systems measure the AC side.
