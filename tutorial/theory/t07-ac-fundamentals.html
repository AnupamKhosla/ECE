AC: sine waves, RMS, phase and impedance

What "230 V 50 Hz" really means, why the peak is 325 V, how capacitors and inductors behave when the current keeps reversing, and why the electricity network cares about power factor. You will finish able to calculate reactance, impedance and real power for any simple AC load.

Safety first

This chapter explains mains electricity so you understand the systems you work beside. You will never measure or connect to 230 V yourself: in Australia that is licensed electricians' work. Every AC experiment in this course uses a sealed plug-pack (wall transformer) that gives 9–24 V AC, which is extra-low voltage.

A short history: the war of the currents

Faraday's 1831 discovery (T06) said that a changing flux makes a voltage. In 1832 the French instrument maker Hippolyte Pixii spun a magnet near a coil with a hand crank. The output reversed every half turn: it was alternating current (AC). People at first saw the reversal as a nuisance and added a mechanical switch (a commutator) to turn it into one-way direct current (DC).

In 1882 Thomas Edison opened the Pearl Street Station in New York, supplying DC at about 110 V to nearby customers. DC had a big problem: you cannot easily change its voltage. Low voltage means high current for a given power, and the I²R losses in the cables (T03) limited each station to customers within roughly a kilometre or two.

Nikola Tesla patented a practical AC induction motor and polyphase (multi-phase) system in 1888. George Westinghouse bought the patents. AC had a killer advantage: the transformer (T08) can step its voltage up for transmission and back down for use, which cuts current and losses. Edison's side campaigned that high-voltage AC was deadly. Westinghouse lit the 1893 Chicago World's Fair with AC, and in 1895–96 the Niagara Falls hydro station sent AC power to Buffalo, about 40 km away. AC won the grid.

In Australia

Australia's grid is AC at 50 Hz, like the UK, Europe and India (the USA uses 60 Hz). DC has made a comeback for very long or undersea links, because DC cables have no reactive losses: Basslink joins Victoria and Tasmania with a high-voltage DC cable under Bass Strait. Solar panels and batteries are DC too; inverters convert them to 50 Hz AC.

Generating AC with a rotating coil

Put a coil of N turns and area A in a uniform field B and spin it at a steady rate. The angle between the coil's axis and the field is θ = ωt, where ω (omega) is the angular frequency in radians per second. The flux through the coil is

Φ = B A cos(ωt) → ε = −N dΦ/dt = N B A ω sin(ωt) When the coil faces the field (θ = 0), the flux is maximum but changing slowest, so the EMF is zero. When the coil is edge-on (θ = 90°), the flux is zero but changing fastest, so the EMF is maximum. Peak EMF = NBAω.

Worked example. N = 100 turns, A = 0.01 m² (10 cm × 10 cm), B = 0.2 T, spinning at 50 revolutions per second. ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad/s. Peak EMF = 100 × 0.2 × 0.01 × 314 ≈ 63 V. Spin it twice as fast and both the frequency and the voltage double. That is why a car alternator needs a regulator.

Real power-station generators spin an electromagnet (the rotor) inside fixed coils (the stator), but the physics is the same. A two-pole generator must turn at 3000 rpm to give 50 Hz; a four-pole one at 1500 rpm. In general f = (number of poles × rpm) / 120. Every generator on the eastern Australian grid turns in lock-step so that the frequency is the same everywhere.

In Australia

The nominal low-voltage supply is 230 V, 50 Hz (Australian Standard AS 60038), with a tolerance of +10 % / −6 %, so roughly 216 V to 253 V at the customer's point of supply. Many homes measure 240 V or more, because the network was originally built for 240 V. Your distributor's rules may add details; confirm current figures with the relevant standard or your state's energy regulator if you need them for work.

RMS: the "equivalent DC" value

A sine wave spends most of its time below its peak, and half its time negative. So what single number describes how "strong" it is? The useful answer is heating power. The RMS (root-mean-square) value of an AC voltage is the DC voltage that would heat the same resistor by the same amount.

Deriving it

Power in a resistor at any instant: p = v²/R. With v = Vp sin ωt:

p(t) = (Vp² / R) · sin²(ωt) = (Vp² / R) · ½ (1 − cos 2ωt) The cos 2ωt part averages to zero over a cycle, so the average power is P = Vp² / 2R. We want a voltage Vrms with Vrms² / R = Vp² / 2R.
Vrms = Vp / √2 ≈ 0.707 Vp Vp = √2 · Vrms ≈ 1.414 Vrms The name says the recipe: take the Root of the Mean (average) of the Square. It works for any waveform; the √2 factor is only true for a pure sine.

Worked example. 230 V RMS → peak = 230 × 1.414 ≈ 325 V. At the top of the tolerance (253 V RMS), the peak is about 358 V. Twice per cycle the active conductor is about 325 V above neutral and twice per cycle about 325 V below it, so the swing is about 650 V peak-to-peak. A 2400 W kettle on 230 V draws 2400 / 230 ≈ 10.4 A RMS, and heats exactly as it would on 230 V DC.

Common misconception

"230 V AC is the same as 230 V DC, so the insulation only needs to handle 230 V." For heating, yes, they match. For insulation and component ratings, the peak matters: a capacitor across 230 V AC sees 325 V (or more) every cycle. The same applies at ELV: a 24 V AC plug-pack feeding a bridge rectifier and a smoothing capacitor gives about 24 × 1.414 − 1.4 ≈ 32 V DC, not 24 V (more when lightly loaded). Your filter capacitor must be rated for at least 35 V, preferably 50 V. You will measure exactly this in P06.

Bench tip

Cheap multimeters are often "average-responding": they measure the average of the rectified wave and multiply by 1.11, which is only correct for a pure sine. On the distorted current of an LED driver or a switch-mode supply they can read 20–40 % wrong. A meter marked True RMS computes the real RMS. For AC work on the job, buy True RMS.

Phase and phasors

Two sine waves of the same frequency can be shifted in time relative to each other. We measure the shift as an angle, the phase difference φ, where a full cycle is 360°. At 50 Hz, 1 ms of shift is 360° × 1/20 = 18°. If the current reaches its peak after the voltage, the current lags; if before, it leads.

Drawing two sine waves every time is slow. Engineers use a phasor: an arrow whose length is the amplitude and which rotates anticlockwise at ω. The vertical projection of the arrow's tip, as it turns, traces out the sine wave. Two waves of the same frequency rotate together, so their arrows keep a fixed angle between them: the phase difference. We can freeze the picture and just draw the arrows. Adding two AC voltages then becomes adding two arrows head-to-tail, which is much easier than adding two sine waves point by point.

Reactance: how C and L behave at AC

Capacitor: current leads by 90°

A capacitor's current is I = C dV/dt (T05). If v = Vp sin ωt, then i = C Vp ω cos ωt. A cosine is a sine shifted 90° earlier, so the current leads the voltage by 90°. Physically: the current is largest when the voltage is changing fastest (at the zero crossing) and zero at the voltage peak, where the capacitor is momentarily full. The ratio of peak voltage to peak current is the capacitor's reactance:

XC = 1 / (2π f C) (ohms) Bigger capacitor or higher frequency → smaller XC → more current. At DC (f = 0) XC is infinite: a capacitor blocks DC.

Inductor: current lags by 90°

An inductor's voltage is V = L di/dt (T06). If i = Ip sin ωt, then v = L Ip ω cos ωt. Now the voltage leads, or equivalently the current lags the voltage by 90°. The inductor's "inertia" makes the current arrive late.

XL = 2π f L (ohms) Bigger inductor or higher frequency → bigger XL → less current. At DC XL = 0: an inductor passes DC.
Memory aid

ELI the ICE man. In an inductor (L), voltage E comes before current I: ELI. In a capacitor (C), current I comes before voltage E: ICE.

Impedance: resistance and reactance together

Real loads have both resistance and reactance. A relay coil has copper resistance and inductance; a sensor cable has resistance and capacitance. The total opposition to AC is called impedance Z, also in ohms. You cannot simply add R and X, because the voltages across them are 90° apart in time: when one is at its peak, the other is at zero. You add them like the two sides of a right-angled triangle.

|Z| = √(R² + X²) tan φ = X / R I = V / |Z| Engineers often write Z = R + jX, where j = √−1 marks the 90°-shifted part (j instead of i, because i is current). Inductive reactance is +jXL, capacitive is −jXC. In series they simply subtract: X = XL − XC.

Worked example 1: a relay coil on AC. R = 240 Ω, L = 0.1 H, at 50 Hz. XL = 2π × 50 × 0.1 = 31.4 Ω. |Z| = √(240² + 31.4²) ≈ 242 Ω, and φ = tan⁻¹(31.4 / 240) ≈ 7.5°. At 50 Hz, this small DC relay coil is almost a pure resistor. (That is the "relay coil" preset in the widget.)

Worked example 2: series RC on a 24 V AC plug-pack. R = 1 kΩ, C = 1 µF, f = 50 Hz. XC = 1 / (2π × 50 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 3183 Ω. |Z| = √(1000² + 3183²) ≈ 3336 Ω. I = 24 / 3336 ≈ 7.2 mA, leading the voltage by tan⁻¹(3183/1000) ≈ 73°. Now measure with a meter: VR = 7.2 mA × 1000 Ω ≈ 7.2 V, and VC = 7.2 mA × 3183 Ω ≈ 22.9 V.

Common misconception

"Kirchhoff's voltage law fails at AC: 7.2 V + 22.9 V = 30 V, but the supply is only 24 V!" KVL still holds at every instant. The two RMS readings are of waves that peak at different times, so they add as phasors (arrows at 90°): √(7.2² + 22.9²) = 24.0 V. A meter shows sizes only and throws away the phase. When AC readings "don't add up", think phasors.

Common misconception

"A 24 V AC relay and a 24 V DC relay are interchangeable." An AC coil relies on its reactance XL to limit current; its copper resistance is quite low. Put it on 24 V DC and only the resistance limits the current: for example, a coil with 50 Ω resistance and 150 Ω reactance draws about 150 mA on AC but 480 mA on DC, and its copper heating rises by about ten times. It burns out. A DC coil on AC draws less current than it needs and chatters or buzzes, because the force drops to zero 100 times a second. Match the coil to the supply. This mistake is common in BMS and access-control panels where 24 V AC and 24 V DC sit side by side.

Resonance

Look again at Figure 3. At one frequency the inductor's reactance equals the capacitor's. In a series LC circuit, X = XL − XC = 0 there, so the two reactances cancel and only the small resistance is left: the impedance is at its minimum and the current is at its maximum. In a parallel LC circuit it is the opposite: the impedance is at its maximum. Setting 2πfL = 1/(2πfC):

f₀ = 1 / (2π √(LC)) For L = 10 µH and C = 100 nF: √(LC) = √(10⁻¹²) = 10⁻⁶ s, so f₀ = 1 / (2π × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159 kHz.

The intuition is a pendulum or a mass on a spring. Energy sloshes back and forth: all in the capacitor's electric field (voltage maximum, current zero), then all in the inductor's magnetic field (current maximum, voltage zero), then back. Push it at its natural frequency and a small push builds up a large swing. The Q factor (quality factor) says how sharp the resonance is: a high-Q circuit responds strongly over a narrow band of frequencies (bandwidth ≈ f₀ / Q).

Worked example, an FM radio tuner. A 0.25 µH coil with a 10 pF capacitor: √(LC) = √(2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸) ≈ 1.58 × 10⁻⁹ s; f₀ ≈ 1 / (2π × 1.58 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 100 MHz, in the middle of the FM band. Tuning the capacitor picks out one station.

On the job

Resonance is everywhere in the systems you will install. Proximity access cards and NFC phones use tuned coils at 125 kHz or 13.56 MHz; the card harvests power from the reader's resonant field. Inductive vehicle loop detectors at boom gates and car-park entries run a loop of wire buried in the slab as the L of an oscillator; a car's metal changes the inductance, the frequency shifts, and the detector switches a relay. Every antenna (T10) is a resonant structure.

Real, reactive and apparent power

Instantaneous power is p = v × i. Multiply two sine waves that are φ apart and average over a cycle (a trigonometric identity does it in one line):

P = Vrms · Irms · cos φ Only the part of the current that is in phase with the voltage does net work. The 90°-shifted part carries energy into fields and back again every cycle.

So there are three "powers", each with its own unit to stop confusion:

NameFormulaUnitMeaning
Real (active) power PV I cos φwatt, WTurns into heat, light or mechanical work. What your energy meter bills as kWh.
Reactive power QV I sin φvolt-ampere reactive, varEnergy borrowed and returned each cycle by magnetic and electric fields. No net work.

Worked example. An induction motor takes 2.3 kW of real power on 230 V at a power factor of 0.8 lagging. S = P / PF = 2300 / 0.8 = 2875 VA. I = S / V = 2875 / 230 = 12.5 A. If the power factor were 1, the same work would need only 2300 / 230 = 10 A. Q = √(2875² − 2300²) ≈ 1725 var. The cable losses scale as I², so this motor wastes (12.5/10)² ≈ 1.56 times as much in the cables as a PF-1 load doing the same work.

Why utilities care

The network has to be built for the current, not the watts. Transformers are rated in kVA for this reason (T08). A building full of motors with a poor power factor makes the network carry extra current that does no useful work, heating cables and transformers and using up their capacity. Large commercial and industrial customers are often charged on their peak kVA demand, so a poor power factor costs them real money. That is why plant rooms in larger buildings often have a power-factor correction cabinet: banks of capacitors switched in automatically. Capacitors draw leading current, which cancels part of the motors' lagging current, so the supply only has to deliver the real part.

Modern electronics adds a second kind of poor power factor. A simple rectifier with a smoothing capacitor only draws current in short spikes near the voltage peaks. Even if those spikes are in phase, their distorted shape means the RMS current is high for the power delivered: this is distortion power factor, and the spikes inject harmonics (currents at 150 Hz, 250 Hz and so on) into the network. Larger switch-mode power supplies and LED drivers include PFC (power factor correction) circuits to draw a sine-shaped current.

In Australia

Home customers are billed for real energy in kWh. Many large customers' network tariffs include a demand charge based on kVA, which rewards good power factor. Tariff structures differ between distributors and change over time, so check the actual tariff for any site you work on. You will meet power-factor numbers when reading energy meters and BMS energy dashboards (C01).

Check yourself

An Australian outlet is 230 V. What are the peak and peak-to-peak voltages, and why do component ratings care about the peak?

Peak = 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V; peak-to-peak ≈ 650 V (higher at the top of the tolerance, about 358 V peak at 253 V). Insulation and capacitors break down on the instantaneous voltage, so they must withstand the peak plus transients, not the RMS value.

Why is the RMS value of a sine wave peak/√2, and not the simple average of the wave?

The simple average of a full sine wave is zero. RMS is defined by heating: power ∝ v², and the average of sin² over a cycle is ½. So the equivalent DC voltage satisfies Vrms² = Vp²/2, giving Vp/√2.

What is the reactance of a 100 nF capacitor at 50 Hz and at 1 MHz? What does that tell you about decoupling capacitors?

About 31.8 kΩ at 50 Hz and 1.59 Ω at 1 MHz. To slow signals the capacitor is nearly an open circuit, so it does not load the supply; to fast noise it is nearly a short circuit to ground, so it removes the noise.

In a pure inductor on AC, current flows but the average power is zero. Explain physically.

For a quarter cycle the current is growing and energy flows into the magnetic field (½Li²). For the next quarter the current shrinks and the field returns that energy to the source. The current is 90° out of phase with the voltage, so v·i is positive half the time and negative half the time, averaging to zero.

A 1.5 kW load has a power factor of 0.6 on 230 V. What current does it draw? What would it draw at PF 1?

S = 1500 / 0.6 = 2500 VA, so I = 2500 / 230 ≈ 10.9 A. At PF 1, I = 1500 / 230 ≈ 6.5 A. The extra current does no useful work but still heats the wiring and uses network capacity.

What is the resonant frequency of 10 µH and 100 nF, and what happens to the impedance of a series LC there?

f₀ = 1/(2π√(10⁻⁵ × 10⁻⁷)) = 1/(2π × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159 kHz. In series, XL and XC cancel, so the impedance falls to just the circuit's resistance, and current is maximum. (In parallel, impedance is maximum.)

You measure 7 V across a resistor and 23 V across a capacitor in series on a 24 V AC supply. Is something wrong?

No. The two voltages are 90° out of phase, so they add as phasors: √(7² + 23²) ≈ 24 V. RMS meter readings give magnitudes only.

Why does a 24 V AC plug-pack with a bridge rectifier and capacitor give about 32 V DC?

The capacitor charges to the peak of the AC, not the RMS: 24 × 1.414 ≈ 34 V, minus two diode drops (about 1.4 V) ≈ 32.5 V. With light load, the plug-pack's own output is also above its rated 24 V, so the DC can be even higher.
