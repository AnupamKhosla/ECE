Fibre optics in the field: splicing, connectors and OTDR

By the end you will know single-mode from multimode at a glance, never mate the wrong connectors, understand every step of a fusion splice, work out a loss budget, and read an OTDR trace well enough to answer an interviewer's "what is this event?"

Time 75 min readLevel Beginner to job-readyBuilds on T10, C07, C08Roles Splicer, fibre tech, data centre tech

Fibre in one minute

An optical fibre is a thread of very pure glass. It has a core in the centre and a cladding around it. The core's glass has a slightly higher refractive index than the cladding's. Light hitting the boundary at a shallow angle is reflected back in by total internal reflection, so it stays trapped in the core for kilometres. Outside the cladding is a soft plastic coating (usually 250 µm across) that protects the glass. Inside a building you often see a thicker 900 µm tight buffer over that.

The cladding is always 125 µm, about the thickness of a hair. What changes is the core:

  • Single-mode (SM): core about 9 µm. So small that light can only travel one way down it (one "mode"). No smearing between paths, so it carries huge bandwidth over tens of kilometres. Every carrier and NBN network uses it.
  • Multimode (MM): core 50 µm (modern) or 62.5 µm (old). Light travels many paths of slightly different length, so pulses smear out. Good for short links inside buildings and data centres, with cheaper light sources.

Outdoor cables are usually black whatever the fibre type, so read the print on the jacket. Inside a cable, individual fibres follow a colour code (blue, orange, green, brown, slate, white, red, black, yellow, violet, rose, aqua is the common 12-colour order). Splicers learn it by heart, because "fibre 7 in tube 3" is how jobs are written.

Why those wavelengths? Glass absorbs and scatters less light at some wavelengths than others. Around 1310 nm and 1550 nm, single-mode glass is very clear (roughly 0.35 and 0.25 dB per km). Around 850 nm, cheap fast light sources (VCSELs) exist, which suits short multimode links. Scattering (Rayleigh scattering) falls steeply with longer wavelength, which is one reason 1550 nm loses less than 1310 nm.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Multimode is better because it carries more modes." Right: more modes means more pulse smearing (modal dispersion), so multimode is limited to short distances. Single-mode cable is not dearer than multimode; the multimode advantage is cheaper transceivers for short runs. For anything outside a building, expect single-mode.

Connectors and the APC/UPC rule

A fibre connector holds the fibre in a precise ceramic ferrule and presses two ferrules end to end in an adaptor (a coupler, often called a bulkhead or through adaptor). Two you will use every day:

LC

Small, 1.25 mm ferrule, a latch like an RJ45. Used in patch panels, switches and SFP transceivers. Often in duplex pairs (transmit and receive).

SC

Square push-pull body, 2.5 mm ferrule. Common in PON networks and older panels. Easy to handle with gloves.

The polish matters as much as the shape:

  • UPC (ultra physical contact): face polished nearly flat. Body usually blue (single-mode). Multimode connectors are usually beige or aqua.
  • APC (angled physical contact): face polished at 8°. Body always green. Very low back-reflection, because any reflected light hits the angled face and leaves the core. PON networks commonly use APC because reflections upset the shared optics.
Never do this

Never mate APC (green) with UPC (blue). They fit in the same adaptor, which is the trap. The angled face meets a flat face, so the cores touch at the edge only and leave a wedge of air. Loss jumps to several dB, reflections are high, and you can chip both ferrules, ruining two expensive connectors. Green goes to green, blue goes to blue.

Inspect, clean, inspect

The single most common cause of fibre faults is a dirty connector. A 9 µm core is smaller than a speck of dust, and one particle on the core can block a big share of the light. Worse, mating a dirty connector presses the dirt into the other face and can pit the glass permanently.

  1. Inspect. Look at the end-face with a fibre inspection scope (a probe microscope on a screen or phone). Many scopes give an automatic pass/fail against IEC 61300-3-35, the common end-face standard.

  2. Clean. Use a one-click pen cleaner for connectors in adaptors, or a cassette cleaner for loose plugs. For stubborn dirt: a lint-free wipe dampened with fibre-grade IPA (isopropyl alcohol), then immediately a dry wipe, so no film is left.

  3. Inspect again. Only mate it when the core zone is clean. If it still fails after two cleans, look for scratches or pits: the connector may need replacing.

  4. Cap it. Keep dust caps on every unused connector and port. Caps get dirty too, so do not assume a capped connector is clean.

Bench tip

Clean both sides every time: the plug and the port it goes into. Technicians who do this every time, even when "it's brand new", fix more faults on the first visit. Interviewers love hearing "inspect, clean, inspect".

Fusion splicing, step by step

A fusion splice welds two fibres into one continuous piece of glass using an electric arc. A good one loses only a few hundredths of a dB and lasts for decades. A fusion splicer is a precision machine with cameras that look at the fibres from two directions, motors that line them up to within a fraction of a micrometre, and two electrodes that strike the arc.

What each step is really protecting against

  • Strip: the stripper must remove the coating without scratching the glass. A scratch becomes a crack later.
  • Clean: coating residue on the glass lands in the V-grooves and pushes the fibre out of line. Wipe once, from the coating towards the tip, then do not touch the glass.
  • Cleave: a cleaver scores the glass with a diamond or carbide blade and snaps it under tension, giving a mirror-flat face. A chipped or angled cleave makes a bubble or a lossy joint. The splicer shows the cleave angle; re-cleave anything above the limit you have set.
  • Align: core alignment splicers line up the cores using the camera images; cladding alignment splicers line up the outside and trust the core is centred. Core alignment is usual for carrier single-mode work.
  • Arc: a short pre-arc cleans and softens the ends, then the fibres are pushed together and fused. The machine estimates the loss from the image.
  • Proof test and protect: many machines pull gently on the joint to prove its strength. The heat-shrink sleeve contains a steel rod that keeps the joint straight for life.
Bench tip

The splicer's estimated loss is a guess from an image. The real check is an OTDR or loss test afterwards. An image that looks perfect can still hide a problem, and an occasional "high" estimate can measure fine. Run arc checks (calibration) when you change site, altitude or weather, as the machine's instructions say.

Mechanical splices (fibres held end to end in a small gel-filled clamp) and splice-on or field-installable connectors also exist. They need less equipment but have higher loss and are used for repairs and some drop installs. Fusion is the standard for carrier joints.

Typical losses and the loss budget

Every part of a link takes away some light, measured in decibels (dB). Losses in dB simply add. The light that leaves the transmitter, minus all the losses, must still be above what the receiver needs. That difference is the loss budget.

ItemTypical loss (planning value)
Single-mode fibre at 1310 nm~0.35 dB per km
Single-mode fibre at 1550 nm~0.25 dB per km
Multimode fibre at 850 nm~3 dB per km (links are short)
Fusion splice~0.05–0.1 dB
Mated connector pair (clean)~0.3–0.5 dB
1:2 / 1:8 / 1:32 splitter~3.5 / ~10.5 / ~17 dB

These are typical planning figures. Design documents and standards set the exact values a project uses, and real components vary.

Total loss = (km × dB/km) + (connector pairs × loss) + (splices × loss) + splitter Margin = budget − total loss. Budget = transmitter power − receiver sensitivity, both in dBm. Keep a few dB of spare margin for ageing, repairs and dirt.

Worked example: a PON drop

A GPON link: 6 km of single-mode at 1310 nm (the upstream wavelength, the worse case), 4 mated connector pairs, 8 fusion splices, one 1:32 splitter. Budget 28 dB (a common GPON optics class).

  • Fibre: 6 × 0.35 = 2.1 dB
  • Connectors: 4 × 0.5 = 2.0 dB
  • Splices: 8 × 0.1 = 0.8 dB
  • Splitter: 17 dB
  • Total: 21.9 dB. Margin: 28 − 21.9 = 6.1 dB. Pass, with room for a few repair splices later.

Notice that the splitter is three-quarters of the loss. That is why PON techs are fussy about every connector: the splitter leaves little room for dirt.

Test tools: VFL, power meter, OTDR

Visual fault locator (VFL)

A bright red (visible, about 650 nm) laser you plug into a fibre. Where the glass is broken or bent too tightly, red light leaks out and glows through the jacket. Great for finding a break in a patch lead or tray, and for identifying which fibre is which. Range is only a few km.

Light source + power meter

The source sends a known, steady light; the meter at the far end reads dBm. Subtract to get the end-to-end loss (an "OLTS" or Tier 1 test). This is the number that decides pass or fail on many jobs. A meter alone also reads live network light, such as at a PON connection box.

OTDR

Optical time-domain reflectometer. Tests from one end only and draws a map of the fibre: where every connector, splice, bend and break is, and how much each loses. The splicer's main tool (Tier 2 test).

Safety

Never look into a fibre, a connector or a port, and never point a fibre at anyone. Telecom lasers at 1310 and 1550 nm are invisible: you will not blink or feel anything while they damage your retina. Assume every fibre is live until a power meter says otherwise. Look at end-faces only through an inspection scope, which shows you a camera image. A VFL is visible red, but it is still a laser: do not stare into it. Some OTDRs use an out-of-band wavelength (such as 1625 or 1650 nm) for testing live PON fibres with filters; follow your employer's rules on testing live fibres, because a normal OTDR pulse can disrupt live customers.

Glass shards

Bare fibre offcuts are tiny, sharp and nearly invisible. They go into skin and eyes and are hard to remove. Put every offcut straight into a labelled scrap container (many cleavers have a built-in bin). Work on a dark mat so you can see them, wear safety glasses, do not eat or drink at the splicing bench, and never brush offcuts off the bench with your hand. IPA is flammable: keep it capped and away from the splicer's arc and the heat oven.

Reading an OTDR trace

An OTDR fires a short, strong pulse of light into the fibre and listens to what comes back. Two things come back:

  • Backscatter: glass always scatters a tiny fraction of light backwards (Rayleigh scattering), all along its length. As the pulse travels and weakens, the backscatter gets weaker too. Plotting it gives a straight sloping line on a dB scale. The slope is the fibre's loss in dB/km.
  • Reflections: wherever the light meets a sudden change, such as a glass–air gap at a connector or the cut end of the fibre, a strong pulse bounces straight back. These show as spikes.

The OTDR converts the return time into distance. The light goes out and back, so it divides by 2:

distance = (c × t) ÷ (2 × n) = (3×108 × 50 µs) ÷ (2 × 1.468) ≈ 5.1 km c is the speed of light in vacuum, t the round-trip time, n the group index of the glass (about 1.47 for single-mode, set in the OTDR). A wrong n setting gives wrong distances, which matters when you send a crew to dig.

Bench tip

Pulse width is a trade-off. A short pulse gives a small dead zone and separates close events, but has little energy, so the trace gets noisy over long distances. A long pulse sees far but blurs close events together. Start short for building and FTTP drops; go longer for long carrier spans. Average for longer to lower the noise.

On the job: where fibre skills are used

Carrier and NBN splicing

Joint enclosures in pits and on poles, street cabinets, exchange frames. Splicing large-count cables (hundreds of fibres) in order, then OTDR testing and reporting. Often a two-person crew with a splicing van or tent.

FTTP installs and assurance

Drops, connection boxes, power-meter checks, cleaning, swapping damaged leads. Covered in C08.

Data centres and buildings

Multimode and single-mode trunks, MPO/MTP multi-fibre cassettes, LC patching, certification testing with loss test sets. Very clean, very neat work.

Security, BMS and industrial

CCTV over fibre between buildings, campus backbones, fibre rings in mines and plants where copper would pick up interference or lightning. See C05 and C10.

On the job

A typical splicing day: get the job pack (cable plan, splice chart saying which fibre joins which), set up a clean, sheltered spot near the pit or cabinet, prepare the enclosure, splice tube by tube following the colour code, then test with an OTDR from the exchange end. You save the traces with clear names, because the client checks them. A neat tray, correct labels and a clean report get you more work than speed alone.

Try this

A cheap way to practise at home, all at safe low power: buy a pen-style VFL (~A$20–40 online) and a couple of SC or LC patch leads (a few dollars each). Plug in the VFL and wrap the lead tightly around a pencil: you will see red light glow at the bend. That is a macrobend leaking light. Loosen it and the glow stops. Add a cheap optical power meter (~A$30–60) and a 1310 nm source if you like, and measure a lead's loss before and after cleaning the connectors. Never look into the lead's end.

In Australia: registration and training

In Australia

Fibre cabling on the customer side is "customer cabling" under the ACMA's cabling provider rules, and must be done by a registered cabler with an optical fibre endorsement. Employers on carrier and NBN networks commonly require the same registration and endorsement as a baseline, plus their own network accreditation and inductions. Registration is issued through ACMA-accredited registrars after you complete the required training. Requirements change: confirm the current rules with the ACMA or a registrar, and confirm what a particular employer requires before you pay for a course.

Training is usually a short registered course in optical fibre cabling (splicing, termination, testing) on top of open cabling registration, from TAFEs or private RTOs. Practical hours on a real splicer matter most. Many entrants start as cable haulers or trainee installers and learn splicing on the job from a senior splicer. Read C07 for the cabling rules and C11 for the full tickets list.

Typical interview questions

"How do you tell single-mode from multimode?"

Jacket colour (yellow OS2, aqua OM3/OM4, orange old OM1/OM2), the print on the jacket, connector colour (blue or green usually single-mode), and the equipment wavelength (1310/1550 SM, 850 MM). If in doubt, check the drawings.

"Can you plug a green connector into a blue one?"

No. Green is APC (8°), blue is UPC. Mating them leaves an air gap, causes high loss and reflections, and can damage both ferrules.

"Walk me through a fusion splice."

Sleeve on, strip, clean with IPA, cleave and check the angle, load, align, arc, check estimated loss, proof test, sleeve and shrink, tray and label, then confirm with an OTDR.

"You see a step at 1550 nm much bigger than at 1310 nm. What is it?"

A macrobend. Find the location from the OTDR distance and look for a tight bend in a tray, clip or pit.

"Why use a launch cable?"

To move the first connector out of the OTDR's dead zone so its loss can be measured; a receive cable does the same for the last connector.

"What do you do with fibre offcuts?"

Straight into a labelled scrap container, work on a dark mat, glasses on, never brush them away by hand, no food at the bench.

Check yourself

Why does a dirty connector matter more on single-mode than on multimode?

The single-mode core is about 9 µm across, smaller than many dust particles. One particle can cover much of the core and block or scatter a large share of the light. A 50 µm multimode core is less sensitive, though dirt still matters there too.

Work out the loss: 12 km single-mode at 1550 nm, 2 connector pairs at 0.5 dB, 6 splices at 0.1 dB, no splitter.

Fibre 12 × 0.25 = 3.0 dB, connectors 1.0 dB, splices 0.6 dB. Total 4.6 dB. With typical point-to-point optics this is a comfortable link.

An OTDR shows a step of 0.4 dB with a clear spike at 2.3 km, where the drawings show a joint enclosure with fusion splices only. What do you suspect?

A spike means a reflection, which a good fusion splice does not make. Suspect a mechanical splice or connector that is not on the drawings, a cracked splice, or a poor joint with an air gap. Go and look. Also check the distance setting (index) in case the event is somewhere else.

Why is 1550 nm used to find bends, when 1310 nm is the usual "worst-case" wavelength for loss?

Fibre itself loses more at 1310 nm, but light at 1550 nm is less tightly held in the core, so it leaks out of bends far more easily. Testing at both and comparing the step size reveals bends.

The OTDR group index is set to 1.50 instead of 1.468. Does it report breaks too near or too far?

Distance = c·t / (2n). A larger n gives a smaller distance, so it reports breaks about 2% too near. At 5 km that is about 100 m: enough to send a crew to the wrong pit.

A splice shows +0.05 dB (a gain) from end A and 0.15 dB loss from end B. What is the real loss?

Average the two directions: (−0.05 + 0.15) / 2 = 0.05 dB. The apparent gain comes from the two fibres scattering differently, not from real gain.

Why must the heat-shrink sleeve go on before you strip the fibre?

After the splice the fibre is one continuous piece with no free end, so a sleeve cannot be threaded on. Forgetting it means breaking and redoing the splice.

Your 1:32 PON link calculates at 26.5 dB against a 28 dB budget. Is it fine?

It passes on paper, but with only 1.5 dB of margin. One dirty connector or a repair splice could make it fail. Re-check the actual measured loss, clean everything, and flag the low margin to the supervisor.

Skills checklist

  • I can identify OS2, OM3/OM4 and old OM1/OM2 fibre by colour and print, and state their wavelengths.
  • I can tell LC from SC and APC (green) from UPC (blue), and I never mate APC with UPC.
  • I follow inspect, clean, inspect on every connector, both sides.
  • I can list the fusion splicing steps in order, starting with the sleeve.
  • I can calculate a loss budget with fibre, connectors, splices and a splitter, and judge the margin.
  • I can explain what a VFL, a power meter and an OTDR each tell me.
  • I can identify launch dead zone, connector, splice, gainer, bend, splitter, end and ghost on a trace.
  • I handle lasers and glass offcuts safely, and I know to confirm ACMA registration and the optical fibre endorsement with the ACMA or a registrar.
