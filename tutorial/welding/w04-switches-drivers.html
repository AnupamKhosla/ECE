MOSFETs, IGBTs and the gate-driver neighbourhood

Learn loose-device screening at 9 V, then understand why a failed power switch sends you toward the driver, sensing and protection circuits.

Study + exercises 2–3 hoursLevel W02, W03; loose-part labContext India · family repair shop

Identify the device before testing

Copy the full marking and suffix. Use the manufacturer’s datasheet to identify the device type, pin order, tab connection, internal diode and gate limits. TO-220 or TO-247 describes a package, not a pinout or technology. A MOSFET has gate, drain and source; an IGBT has gate, collector and emitter.

A loose-switch test you can do at low voltage

Use a known-good, loose N-channel enhancement MOSFET whose datasheet specifies on-resistance at 4.5 V or lower and permits a 9 V gate voltage. Use a 9 V battery, 1 kΩ load resistor (¼ W), 10 kΩ gate resistor and 100 kΩ gate-to-source pull-down. For an IGBT, use this lab only if its datasheet supports conduction at the chosen gate voltage; many welding IGBTs require a higher specified drive and this 9 V lab will not be conclusive.

  1. Build with the battery disconnected. Battery negative will be the source/emitter reference. Connect battery + → 1 kΩ load → drain/collector. Connect source/emitter to battery −. Put 100 kΩ from gate to source/emitter.

  2. Off state. Connect the battery, keeping the gate drive disconnected. Measure DC voltage across the 1 kΩ load: near 0 V is expected. D–S or C–E is near battery voltage. A hard short would put nearly the full battery voltage across the load, with current still limited by 1 kΩ.

  3. On state. Connect battery + to the gate through 10 kΩ. The gate divider gives about 8.2 V on a 9 V battery. Measure gate-to-source/emitter and the load voltage. A suitable MOSFET should deliver nearly the battery voltage to the load; an IGBT may lose a larger on-state voltage.

  4. Turn off. Disconnect the 10 kΩ drive. The 100 kΩ pull-down removes gate charge and the load voltage falls. Repeat and write what each terminal voltage means.

  5. Disconnect all power. Follow the device’s gate-handling instructions before using diode mode. Compare with the datasheet’s diode arrangement. Keep loose parts on an appropriate ESD bench.

This demonstrates controlled conduction at only a few milliamps. It does not test hundreds of volts of blocking, high-current loss, switching speed or short-circuit survival. A device that fails to conduct at 9 V might be unsuitable for this exercise rather than faulty. Do not perform this circuit on a transistor still connected to a welder board.

Why the pull-down matters

The gate is a capacitor. Removing the driving wire does not instantly remove its stored charge; a floating gate can retain an on state. A defined gate-to-source/emitter resistor gives charge a discharge path. See T05.

Screen an isolated power device

After the machine’s safe-access process, a technician checks for a hard D–S/C–E short with the gate held off, verifies any expected diode polarity and screens gate leakage/shorts using the correct device’s test guidance. A stored gate charge can make a good MOSFET appear conductive. A gate resistor or driver on the PCB can dominate an in-circuit reading. Photograph and record the original topology before lifting anything.

Do not test arbitrary gates with an insulation tester or inject an unknown external voltage into a connected driver. If the datasheet includes a protection network, its behaviour must be included in the interpretation.

Read a gate waveform as evidence

Study recorded waveforms obtained by your mentor before making live measurements yourself. Ask which reference was used: VGS means gate relative to source; VGE means gate relative to emitter. A high-side emitter moves with the switching node. A gate-to-chassis trace can look impressive while saying little about actual drive.

Look for the intended on/off levels, repetition rate, pulse width, dead time where appropriate, supply droop and ringing. A flat region during switching can be the Miller plateau, not necessarily a fault. Values and acceptable overshoot come from the real design. Probe bandwidth, common-mode rejection and lead inductance can create apparent spikes.

One IGBT is shorted. Is replacing all the IGBTs enough?

No. Follow the manufacturer’s guidance for the failed group, then investigate the gate drivers, related passives, snubbers, output stage, sensing and cooling. Replacing a group may be required, but it does not establish why the original group failed.

Skills checklist
