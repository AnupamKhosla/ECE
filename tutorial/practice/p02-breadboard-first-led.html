Breadboard and your first LED circuit

You will learn how a breadboard is wired inside, read resistor colour codes, find which leg of an LED is which, and build and measure a battery–resistor–LED circuit until Ohm's law is something you have seen with your own meter.

Inside a breadboard

A breadboard looks like a field of identical holes, but underneath it is a set of metal spring clips. Each clip grips several holes and joins them electrically. Once you know which holes share a clip, the breadboard stops being mysterious. Two words first:

  • A node is any set of points joined by wire (or a clip) so they are all at the same voltage.
  • A terminal strip is one clip. On a standard board each terminal strip joins 5 holes in one numbered column: a–e, or f–j.

The rules, in words

  1. In the main area, the 5 holes a–e of one numbered column are joined. Holes f–j of the same column are a separate strip.
  2. Neighbouring columns are not joined. That is how you put a part's two legs into two different nodes: one leg per column.
  3. The centre channel separates the two halves. A chip (IC) straddles it so each of its pins gets its own strip.
  4. The power rails along each long edge run horizontally. Each rail pair is marked with a red line (use for +) and a blue or black line (use for −). The markings are only a suggestion; the plastic does not know which is which.
  5. On some full-length boards, each rail is split in the middle: the left half and right half are not connected. A gap in the printed red/blue line, or a wider gap between hole groups, is the clue. Never assume: check.
Bench tip

Test a new breadboard with your meter's continuity beeper before you trust it. Push a jumper into one end of a rail and another into the far end, and touch the meter probes to the jumpers. Beep = joined. No beep = split rail; bridge the halves with a short jumper and leave it there for good. Do the same with two holes in one column (should beep) and two neighbouring columns (should not).

Common misconception

Wrong: "Holes in the same horizontal row (all the a's) are connected."
Right: In the main area, connection runs across the short direction (a–e of one column). Only the power rails run along the long direction. Most beginner faults are two legs accidentally in the same strip, which shorts the part out.

Worked example. Bands orange, orange, brown, gold. Digits 3 and 3 give 33. Brown multiplier is ×10. Value = 33 × 10 = 330 Ω, ±5%, so anywhere from 313.5 Ω to 346.5 Ω is in spec. The same 330 Ω as a 5-band ±1% part is orange, orange, black, black, brown: 330 × 1 = 330 Ω ±1%.

If you decode a value that is not in the series, you have probably read the resistor backwards.

Bench tip

Colour codes are hard to read under warm light, and brown, red and orange look alike. Do not trust your eyes alone: measure every resistor with the ohms range before it goes in, at least until you are fluent. Keep the paper tape strips from the assortment; they are labelled.

LED polarity

An LED (light-emitting diode) is a diode: it lets current flow one way only. Current must enter the anode (+) and leave by the cathode (−). Put it in backwards and nothing happens (no damage at these voltages, just no light). There are three clues:

Bench tip

Once you cut the legs, the "long leg" clue is gone. Bend the anode leg slightly before cutting, or test it: your meter's diode-test range lights a red LED dimly and reads about 1.6–1.9 V when the red probe is on the anode.

Build it: battery, resistor and LED

The circuit is a loop: battery + → 330 Ω resistor → LED anode → LED cathode → battery −. The resistor's job is to limit the current. An LED has almost no resistance once it starts conducting, so without a resistor the current is limited only by the battery's internal resistance, and the LED overheats.

  1. Check the battery. Switch the holder off. With the meter on DC volts, measure across the battery leads. Expect 4.6–4.8 V with fresh alkaline cells (each cell is about 1.55–1.6 V new).

  2. Check the resistor. Read the bands (orange, orange, brown, gold) and confirm with the ohms range: 314–346 Ω.

  3. Place the parts as in Figure 3: resistor from + rail to a10, LED anode (long leg) in c10 and cathode in c11, jumper from a11 to the − rail.

  4. Connect the battery: red lead to the + rail, black to the − rail. Switch on. The LED should glow a clear red.

  5. Measure voltages (meter on DC volts, power on, probes across each part): battery, resistor, LED. Write them down.

  6. Measure current. Switch off. Pull out the black jumper. Move the meter's red lead to the mA socket and set the dial to DC mA. Touch the red probe to strip 11 (a11) and the black probe to the − rail. The meter is now part of the loop. Switch on and read. Afterwards, move the red lead back to the V socket (P03 explains why this matters).

Measure it: expected readings

I = (Vbattery − VLED) / R = (4.7 V − 1.9 V) / 330 Ω ≈ 8.5 mA The battery provides 4.7 V. The LED "uses" about 1.9 V (its forward voltage, set by the physics of its junction). Whatever is left, 2.8 V, sits across the resistor, and Ohm's law on the resistor sets the current. With a nominal 4.5 V the same sum gives about 7.9 mA.

Now do two checks that turn readings into understanding:

  • Kirchhoff's voltage law: VR + VLED should equal Vbattery. Example: 2.80 + 1.90 = 4.70 V. The voltages round a loop always add up. (P04 proves this properly.)
  • Ohm's law on the resistor: I = VR/R = 2.80 / 330 = 8.48 mA. Compare with the current you measured. They should agree within a few percent; the difference is resistor tolerance and meter accuracy.
Why it works

The resistor obeys Ohm's law: its current is proportional to its voltage. The LED does not: its current rises exponentially with voltage, so its voltage hardly changes (about 1.8–2.0 V) over a huge range of currents. That is why we treat the LED as a fixed "voltage drop" and let the resistor set the current. Without the resistor, the battery would try to push the LED well past 2 V, and the exponential would demand a current far beyond the LED's 20–30 mA rating.

Safety

Never connect an LED straight across a battery or supply without a resistor. On 3×AA it will get hot and may fail; on a 12 V supply it fails instantly and the case can crack. Wear glasses when testing anything new.

Change the resistor

Swap the 330 Ω for each resistor in turn (power off while you swap). For each one, measure VLED, VR and current, and judge the brightness. Typical results with a fresh 4.7 V pack:

RVLEDVRCurrent (VR/R)Brightness
220 Ω~1.95 V~2.75 V~12.5 mABright
330 Ω~1.90 V~2.80 V~8.5 mABright, only slightly less
1 kΩ~1.80 V~2.90 V~2.9 mAClearly dimmer, fine indoors
10 kΩ~1.65 V~3.05 V~0.3 mAFaint glow; easy to see in a dim room

Three lessons hide in this table:

  1. The LED voltage barely moves. Current changed by a factor of 40, but VLED changed by only about 0.3 V. This is the exponential diode curve in action.
  2. Your eye is logarithmic. 12.5 mA looks only a little brighter than 8.5 mA, but 0.3 mA is still visible. Modern LEDs are efficient: indicator LEDs on equipment often run at 1–5 mA.
  3. Resistor power is small. For 220 Ω: P = VR × I = 2.75 × 0.0125 ≈ 34 mW, far below the ¼ W (250 mW) rating. You only need to worry about resistor power at higher voltages and currents.
Try this

Choose a resistor for a target current. For 5 mA from 4.7 V: R = (4.7 − 1.85) / 0.005 = 570 Ω. The nearest E12 values are 560 Ω and 680 Ω. Use 560 Ω and measure. Did you get 5 mA within 10%? Later, for a 12 V supply and 10 mA: R = (12 − 2)/0.01 = 1 kΩ, and P = 10 V × 0.01 A = 0.1 W. Still fine for ¼ W.

This pattern, "the whole supply voltage appears across the part that is open", is the most useful fault-finding fact you will ever learn. On a job, if 24 V appears across a switch, a fuse or a relay contact, that is the open point in the circuit.

Safety

Most LED datasheets give a maximum reverse voltage of about 5 V. On 4.5 V a reversed LED is safe. On 9 V or 12 V, a reversed LED can break down and fail. In P06 you will add a protecting diode when running LEDs from AC.

Two LEDs: series and parallel

Series

Put two red LEDs in series with the 330 Ω (anode of the second to the cathode of the first). Both glow, but dimmer. The LEDs now need about 2 × 1.85 = 3.7 V, leaving only 4.7 − 3.7 = 1.0 V for the resistor: I = 1.0 / 330 ≈ 3 mA. Add a third red LED and they need about 5.5 V, more than the battery has: they stay dark (perhaps a faint glow in a dark room). On a 9 V battery, two red LEDs and 470 Ω give (9 − 3.8) / 470 ≈ 11 mA.

On the job

Cut a common 12 V LED strip open and you will see this exactly: groups of three LEDs in series with one small resistor, repeated along the strip (the cut marks are between groups). Three LEDs use about 9 V and the resistor drops the rest. That is why a 12 V strip glows dimly or not at all on a sagging 9 V supply at the end of a long run, a fault you will meet on site.

Parallel, and why each LED needs its own resistor

Now put a red and a green LED side by side, both fed from the one 330 Ω (Figure 5B). Usually the red lights and the green stays dark or very dim. Both LEDs are forced to the same voltage. The red one starts conducting strongly at about 1.9 V, which pins the node there, and that is not enough for the green one (2.1 V for old yellow-green types, nearer 3 V for modern true-green).

Even two "identical" red LEDs do not share fairly. Their forward voltages differ by tens of millivolts from the factory. Because LED current rises by about 10 times for every ~0.1 V, a 50 mV difference gives roughly a 2–3 times difference in current. Worse, the hotter LED's forward voltage drops (about 2 mV per °C), so it takes even more current and gets hotter. Fix: one resistor per LED (Figure 5C). Try it: each LED now has its own current, set by its own resistor.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Parallel parts share current equally, like water pipes of the same size."
Right: Parallel parts share the same voltage. How the current splits depends on each part's own I–V curve. For resistors the split is gentle and predictable. For diodes and LEDs the curve is exponential, so tiny voltage differences give big current differences.

The physics in your hand

Why a red LED drops about 1.9 V

An LED is a p–n junction made from a semiconductor such as gallium arsenide phosphide. When it conducts, electrons cross the junction and fall into "holes" on the other side. Each fall releases a fixed parcel of energy, equal to the material's band gap, as one photon of light. Energy and wavelength are linked: λ (nm) ≈ 1240 / E (eV). Red light at about 630 nm means E ≈ 1240/630 ≈ 1.97 eV. To give each electron about 2 eV of energy you need about 2 V across the junction. That is the forward voltage. Blue (≈ 470 nm, ≈ 2.6 eV) needs about 3 V, which is why a blue or white LED will not light from two AA cells.

Why it works

The diode's one-way behaviour comes from the depletion region and the built-in field at the junction. The light colour comes from the band gap. Both stories are told from Class 12 physics upward in the theory chapters.

LED vs filament bulb

Filament bulb

A thin tungsten wire heated to about 2500–2800 °C glows because it is hot (black-body glow). Most of the energy comes out as infrared heat, not light. It is a resistor: no polarity, works either way round. Its cold resistance is roughly a tenth of its hot resistance, so it takes a surge of current when switched on.

LED

Light comes directly from electrons crossing a junction, at one colour set by the band gap. Little heat, so far more light per watt. It is a diode: polarity matters, and it needs a series resistor (or a constant-current driver) because its current rises exponentially with voltage.

On AC: flicker at 50 Hz

Australian mains is a 50 Hz sine wave, and so is the output of your AC plug-pack. The voltage passes through zero 100 times a second. A filament bulb hardly notices: the hot wire cannot cool down in 10 ms, so its light only ripples slightly. An LED reacts in nanoseconds. On AC, with its resistor and a protecting diode, it lights only on the positive half-cycles: 50 flashes per second with dark gaps between. It looks steady when still, but wave it quickly and you see a dotted trail. You will see this for yourself in P06, with a safe 12 V AC plug-pack.

Check yourself

You put a resistor in holes a12 and d12. What does the resistor do in your circuit?

Nothing: both legs are in the same 5-hole strip, so the strip shorts the resistor out. A part must have its legs in different strips (different nodes) to be in the circuit.

Decode yellow, violet, red, gold. Then brown, black, black, red, brown.

Yellow (4), violet (7), red (×100) = 4700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ (often written 4k7), ±5%. The 5-band: brown (1), black (0), black (0), red (×100) = 100 × 100 = 10 000 Ω = 10 kΩ, ±1% (brown).

Calculate the resistor for a red LED at 10 mA from a 9 V battery. What power does it dissipate?

R = (9 − 1.9) / 0.010 = 710 Ω. Nearest E12: 680 Ω (gives 10.4 mA) or 820 Ω (8.7 mA). With 680 Ω: P = 7.1 V × 0.0104 A ≈ 74 mW, well under ¼ W.

The LED is reversed. Why is the full battery voltage across the LED and none across the resistor?

No current flows, so the resistor's drop is I × R = 0. The voltages round the loop must still add up to the battery voltage (KVL), so all of it appears across the only part that is blocking: the LED. On a job, "full supply across a part" means "this part is open".

Three red LEDs in series with 330 Ω on 4.5 V stay dark. Why, and what supply would work?

Three red LEDs need about 3 × 1.85 ≈ 5.5 V before significant current flows, more than 4.5 V. A 9 V battery would work: (9 − 5.6)/330 ≈ 10 mA.

Why does the LED's voltage change only from about 1.95 V to 1.65 V when the current changes 40 times?

LED current rises exponentially with voltage: roughly 10 times more current for each extra ~0.1 V. A 40× change is about 1.6 decades, so about 0.16 × 2 ≈ 0.2–0.3 V. The resistor, being linear, takes the rest of the voltage change.

Why does a filament bulb not visibly flicker on 50 Hz AC, while an LED on AC does?

The filament's light depends on its temperature, and its thermal mass cannot cool noticeably in the 10 ms between voltage peaks. The LED's light follows its current almost instantly, and as a diode it conducts only on one half of each cycle, so it flashes 50 times a second with dark gaps.

How do you test whether the power rails on your breadboard are split?

Push a jumper into each end of one rail and test between them with the meter's continuity beeper. A beep means the rail is continuous. No beep means it is split; bridge the halves with a short jumper. Check a gap in the printed red/blue line as a clue, but always confirm with the meter.
