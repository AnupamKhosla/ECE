The central answer: p-type and n-type silicon each conduct in both directions. Joining them creates a barrier whose height and width change with battery polarity. Holes carry current within the valence band; they do not need to move into the conduction band.

01 · What a band actually means

Start by separating where an electron is from which quantum state it occupies. A band is a collection of allowed electronic energies, not a physical layer inside the wafer. Valence and conduction bands exist at the same locations throughout the silicon.

An isolated atom has discrete energy levels. In a crystal, interactions among enormous numbers of atoms produce closely spaced states grouped into bands. Silicon’s valence band is associated with bonding; its conduction band is the next higher band. The intervening bandgap is about 1.12 eV at room temperature. One electronvolt is the energy an electron gains in moving through a potential difference of one volt.

Why doesn’t a full valence band conduct? Electrons already occupy all its states. In the ordinary band description, contributions from opposite velocities cancel, and the Pauli exclusion principle prevents redistributing electrons into already occupied states. A weak electric field cannot establish the imbalance of occupied states needed for a sustained conduction current. This is more fundamental than unusually frequent collisions. A filled band can still polarize; polarization is different from a steady transport current.

Remove some electrons and there are vacancies. The remaining valence electrons can now redistribute, giving a net current. Put a few electrons into the almost empty conduction band and those electrons can redistribute too. Either a nearly full band with holes or a nearly empty band with electrons can conduct.

A slower explanation, starting from Class 12 physics

Your follow-up, rephrased: “I remember that electrons have allowed energy levels and can absorb light to move between them. Why, then, can’t a sufficiently strong electric field pull an electron out of the valence band? Can I understand this using the forces and polarization of a dipole? Please explain what ‘quantum state,’ ‘Pauli exclusion,’ ‘balanced velocities,’ and ‘collisions’ mean before using them to explain conduction. Assume Class 12 physics and an ECE diploma studied a decade ago; build the unfamiliar concepts step by step with physically accurate explanations.”

The earlier paragraph brings together six ideas: allowed electron states, energy bands, available final states, net current, polarization, and scattering. We can understand their roles without first learning the full mathematics of quantum mechanics. Fermi statistics, mentioned elsewhere in this lesson, can wait until the last step.

1. What is an electron “state”?

In Class 12 mechanics, you might describe a particle by its position and velocity. For an electron in an atom, that description is inadequate: it does not follow a definite little orbit around the nucleus. Quantum mechanics describes it through a mathematical wave pattern that tells us the probabilities of different measurement results.

A quantum state is that complete description. An orbital is one example of a spatial wave pattern. A state can have a definite energy, but energy alone does not always identify it: distinct states can have the same energy. Electron spin supplies another part of the description. For this lesson, you only need to remember that an allowed energy is associated with an allowed electron pattern, rather than an arbitrary orbit we choose.

When an electron absorbs light and changes state, energy conservation still applies. For absorption of one photon between two particular energy states, the photon must supply their energy difference: hν = Efinal − Einitial. Here h is Planck’s constant and ν is the light frequency. Matching the energy is necessary, but other quantum rules also affect whether the transition can occur.

2. How do atomic energy levels become bands?

When atoms form a crystal, their electron patterns interact. A level in the isolated-atom picture gives rise to a very large number of closely spaced allowed levels in the solid. With an enormous number of atoms, those levels form a band. There are also energy intervals with no ordinary allowed bulk states: bandgaps.

Consequently, a semiconductor does not absorb only one exact frequency corresponding to its gap. Many initial and final states are available across the two bands. Photons with a range of energies can cause transitions if energy and momentum can be conserved. For silicon, lattice vibrations commonly participate in near-edge absorption because silicon has an indirect bandgap. You do not need that extra detail to understand the essential point: the 1.12 eV gap is the minimum separation between the band edges, not the only possible transition energy.

For the distinction between atomic lines and semiconductor absorption, see Georgia Tech: optical transitions and NPTEL: semiconductor absorption.

3. What does Pauli exclusion prevent?

Two electrons cannot occupy the same complete single-electron quantum state. This is the Pauli exclusion principle. It does not say that two electrons cannot have the same energy. For example, one spatial orbital can hold two electrons with opposite spins because these are distinct complete states.

In a completely filled band, all its available electron states are occupied. An electron cannot simply change into an already occupied state. In a partly filled band, empty states are available, so the distribution of electrons can change under an electric field.

This does not mean that all electrons are fixed in position, that their velocities are zero, or that a large force can never change their band. It tells us which electronic rearrangements are possible. To explain why a filled band gives no ordinary current, we must also consider what current measures.

4. “Balanced velocities” means zero net charge transport

Current measures the net charge crossing a surface per second. If equal amounts of negative charge cross a surface in opposite directions each second, the two contributions cancel. The charges can be moving even though an ammeter reads zero.

In an ordinary silicon crystal, states with opposite velocity contributions occur in matching pairs. At equilibrium, their occupancies balance. A partly filled band can respond to an applied field by developing a small imbalance: contributions in one direction outweigh those in the other, producing current.

For a completely filled band, every state is occupied, including every matching contribution. Under the usual weak-field description, the total remains zero. The precise result comes from adding the velocity contributions across the whole band. Pauli exclusion prevents the simple redistribution into occupied states that the classical “just push every electron faster” picture assumes.

Creating a hole removes one contribution from that complete cancellation. The remaining electrons can then carry current within the valence band. The mobile positive hole is a useful way to describe that current. It never needs to become a conduction-band electron.

5. Your dipole intuition explains polarization

A bound positive and negative charge can shift slightly relative to one another under an electric field. That produces or changes an electric dipole. This is polarization: a small rearrangement of charge inside the material.

During the rearrangement there can be a temporary current. Under a constant field, once the charges settle into their shifted distribution, that rearrangement stops. It does not continuously transport charge from one end of the material to the other. This is why an insulating capacitor can respond when you connect a battery without becoming a steady conducting wire.

So your dipole idea is useful. It explains one response of bound electrons. Sustained conduction requires continuing net charge transport, which is a further response.

6. Can a very strong field free valence electrons? Yes.

The filled-band statement assumes a field weak enough that transitions between bands can be neglected. It is not a claim that no electric field can ever create a conduction electron.

A strong field can enable band-to-band tunnelling: a quantum transition from an occupied valence-band state into an empty conduction-band state. Alternatively, a carrier already present can gain energy from the field and transfer enough of it in a collision to create another electron–hole pair. That second process is impact ionization; repeated multiplication produces avalanche breakdown. Both processes provide new mobile carriers.

A steady electric field does not need to imitate a photon of one particular frequency. A photon transfers energy in a discrete absorption event. A static field changes the electronic potential energy across space and can do work as charge moves. The allowed transition mechanism must be considered in each case.

Use your Class 12 relations: the force magnitude is F = eE, and the work magnitude over a distance d along a uniform field is eEd. A 1 V drop corresponds to 1 eV of work per electron. But the voltage across the entire sample is not automatically deposited into each bond. The local field, electron motion, and interactions determine energy transfer. Comparing “battery voltage” directly with “bond excitation energy” skips those steps.

7. What do collisions have to do with resistance?

Once mobile carriers exist, the field changes their motion. Their interaction with lattice vibrations, impurities, and defects can change their direction and energy. We call these processes scattering; “collisions” is a shorter, less precise description. They limit how much directed drift develops for a given field.

A perfectly periodic lattice is already included in the band structure. It is not best pictured as a random obstacle course of atoms. Vibrations and deviations from that periodic structure are major sources of scattering.

Scattering helps explain the resistance of a material with mobile carriers. A completely filled valence band has a more basic restriction on ordinary conduction: its occupied states cancel and there are no empty states within that band for the needed redistribution. These are different parts of the explanation.

8. Where does “Fermi” fit?

Fermi–Dirac statistics describes how likely each available state is to be occupied at a given temperature, subject to Pauli exclusion. The Fermi level is an energy parameter used in that occupancy rule. It is not an extra band, a force, or a physical layer.

For now, you can understand the diode using occupied states, empty states, electrons, holes, and electric fields. Later, Fermi statistics lets us calculate the carrier concentrations and how they change with doping. It supplies quantitative bookkeeping for the picture you have just built.

Read the original paragraph with these conditions attached: under ordinary fields, a full valence band contributes no sustained conduction current. Introduce vacancies and valence-band current becomes possible. Promote electrons into the conduction band and both electrons and holes become available. Sufficiently strong fields can cause that promotion through mechanisms that the weak-field explanation leaves out.

02 · Can enough voltage free a bonded electron?

Yes, sufficiently strong fields can generate carriers. But ordinary conduction and creation of new carriers are different processes. An electric field first changes the motion of carriers already available. Heat or absorbed light can excite an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, creating an electron–hole pair. Strong fields can also enable tunnelling or accelerate existing carriers enough to create additional pairs through collisions.

A total battery voltage is not an energy packet automatically delivered to every bond. The local field and distance over which an electron gains energy matter. Five volts across a centimetre and five volts across ten nanometres are very different situations. Scattering also interrupts energy gain.

In the bond picture, excitation leaves a missing bonding electron. It does not mean an atom is knocked out of position or a permanent crack forms. Recombination can restore occupancy while releasing energy to lattice vibrations or light. Excessive current and heating can eventually damage a device, but ordinary generation and recombination are reversible electronic processes.

See MIT, generation and recombination and carrier multiplication.

03 · Phosphorus, boron, and the missing electron

A silicon atom has four valence electrons and four nearest neighbours. The familiar bond diagram has each covalent bond sharing two electrons, one contributed by each neighbour. This is useful bookkeeping; actual electronic states belong to the crystal and are not little planetary orbits.

Phosphorus has five valence electrons. Substituting it for silicon supplies the usual four bonding contributions plus an extra electron. The extra electron is weakly bound, with a donor level roughly 0.045 eV below the conduction-band edge. At room temperature, many such electrons are thermally released into the conduction band. The phosphorus donor left behind is a stationary positive ion relative to the neutral crystal.

Boron has three valence electrons. It occupies a silicon site with four neighbouring bond directions, but the electron count is one short. Its acceptor state lies roughly 0.045 eV above the valence-band edge. A valence electron can occupy that acceptor state, leaving a mobile valence-band vacancy. The occupied acceptor is a stationary negative ion; the mobile hole is positive.

So boron does not donate a freely moving silicon electron. Nor is the essential mechanism a special coordinate bond that creates a one-way channel. The electron-counting picture and the acceptor-state picture describe the same shortage at different levels. An initially bound hole can become a mobile valence-band hole when the acceptor is ionized.

Creating a hole in undoped silicon by promoting an electron across the full gap costs about 1.12 eV. Ionizing a shallow acceptor requires much less energy. No “negative energy” is required, and a hole does not need to climb into the conduction band. The energy required to create or free a carrier is distinct from the small energy exchanges involved in its subsequent motion.

Both doped regions remain approximately neutral away from boundaries: mobile electrons balance positive donor ions in n-type silicon; holes balance negative acceptor ions in p-type. The names identify majority carriers, not a net charge on the whole piece. Both carrier types exist in both regions.

04 · How an absence carries current

Picture a row of occupied seats with one empty seat. A person steps into the vacancy; another follows. People move left while the empty seat moves right. Likewise, a valence electron can occupy an available state, making the vacancy appear elsewhere. Electron motion and hole motion are two descriptions of the same charge transport.

This analogy explains the accounting, not the quantum mechanics: band states can extend over many atoms, and transport in clean silicon is not necessarily a literal sequence of localized bond-to-bond jumps. The useful physical result is that a valence-band vacancy responds as a mobile positive carrier with its own mobility.

Under a field pointing right, conduction electrons drift left; holes drift right. Both produce conventional current to the right. A hole is not a positron and not an empty atom. It is a missing electron relative to an otherwise filled valence band.

A uniform p-type resistor can conduct in either direction. So can a uniform n-type resistor. Reversing their field reverses their current. The rectification arises from the junction and its response to bias, not from either bulk material enforcing a permitted direction.

05 · What joining p and n does

Imagine p on the left and n on the right within one continuous silicon crystal. Initially there are far more conduction electrons on the right and far more holes on the left. Random thermal motion produces diffusion down these concentration gradients: electrons spread n → p; holes spread p → n. Diffusion is a statistical flow, not a separate force pushing each electron.

Near the boundary, mobile carriers are removed by redistribution and recombination. The dopant atoms cannot follow: positive donor ions remain on the n side, negative acceptor ions on the p side. The resulting space-charge region is depleted of mobile majority carriers. It is still silicon containing atoms and electronic states; it is not an empty gap.

The field points from positive n-side ions toward negative p-side ions: right → left. That field pushes electrons right and holes left, opposing their diffusion. Equilibrium occurs when field-driven drift balances diffusion, with zero net current. Carriers still move microscopically.

No intrinsic silicon spacer is necessary in an ordinary p–n diode. A p–i–n diode deliberately adds a lightly doped or intrinsic region, but that is a different structure. Pressing two wafer fragments together usually introduces oxides, surface states, and poor contact; a clean, electronically intimate junction is essential.

The redistribution is concentrated near the boundary. The field that develops prevents the whole n piece from simply emptying into p. The less heavily doped side generally contains more of the depletion width.

06 · Follow both battery terminals

Forward bias: positive to p, negative to n. The negative terminal supplies electrons through the n contact. The positive terminal removes electrons through the p contact, which supplies holes to p. In the bulk, holes move toward the junction from the left and electrons approach it from the right. Those carriers reduce the exposed space charge at the depletion edges, narrowing depletion and lowering the barrier.

Your worry that the positive terminal makes n-side depletion more positive reverses the local sequence. The battery’s positive terminal directly contacts p and removes electrons there. Its negative terminal replenishes n. The forward-bias condition reduces the junction’s potential drop rather than increasingly stripping n of electrons near its junction edge.

Electrons can then be injected from n into the p-side conduction band, where they are minority carriers. Holes are injected from p into n’s valence band. They travel into the neutral regions and may recombine. Contacts and the battery maintain the carrier supply; depletion is not a container steadily filling with swallowed electrons.

Reverse bias: positive to n, negative to p. Electrons are withdrawn from the n-side junction neighbourhood; holes are withdrawn from the p-side neighbourhood. More fixed ions are exposed, widening depletion. The barrier against majority-carrier injection increases. This is why adding more reverse voltage does not make electrons climb over the original forward barrier: it changes the barrier in the opposing direction.

Reverse current is small, not exactly zero. Minority carriers generated thermally or by light can be swept across the junction. Surface leakage and generation inside depletion also matter in real devices.

In the ideal low-injection model, forward voltage primarily increases injection and the associated diffusion current. Describing it as “more opposing drift generates holes” misses the mechanism. Drift and diffusion coexist, with their contributions varying across the device.

08 · If an electron recombines, why does current continue?

Suppose a conduction electron injected from n into p falls into a valence-band hole. That particular mobile electron–hole pair disappears. Charge has not disappeared: an electron has changed its quantum state. Meanwhile, other valence electrons move and the p contact removes electrons, sustaining hole transport. The electron that eventually reaches the battery need not be the one that crossed the junction.

Imagine a queue continuously entering and leaving a building. A person sitting down inside does not prevent throughput if others stand up or leave elsewhere. Recombination couples the electron and hole currents; it does not require a single identifiable electron to make the whole journey.

In steady state, total conventional current is the same through every cross-section of a two-terminal diode. Electron current can decrease through a recombining region while hole current increases correspondingly. During switching, temporary charge storage and displacement current also enter the bookkeeping.

In silicon, recombination energy commonly ends up as heat through lattice interactions and other processes. Suitable LED materials favour radiative emission. An LED is a semiconductor diode; a filament bulb glows because electrical power heats a wire.

09 · A small model with actual numbers

Take an abrupt silicon junction at 300 K with equal acceptor and donor densities NA = ND = 1016 cm−3. For illustration use intrinsic concentration ni = 1010 cm−3 and thermal voltage VT = kT/q = 0.02585 V. Material parameters vary somewhat with the model and temperature.

Vbi = VT ln(NAND / ni²) ≈ 0.714 V

This built-in voltage is the internal electrostatic potential difference across the junction at equilibrium. It is not a 0.714 V source available across the external leads. At equilibrium the electrochemical potential, represented by the Fermi level, is uniform through the connected device.

W ≈ √[(2εSi/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)(Vbi − VD)]

With εSi ≈ 11.7ε₀, this depletion approximation gives W ≈ 0.43 μm at zero bias. At +0.50 V forward bias the estimated width is 0.24 μm; at −5 V reverse bias it is 1.22 μm. Reverse voltage makes the barrier about 5.714 V in this internal electrostatic model. The formula ceases to describe the device well near flat-band conditions or strong injection; do not extend it past its assumptions.

The ideal diode current is:

I = IS[exp(VD / ηVT) − 1]

Here η is the ideality factor, not the electron concentration n. For η = 1, increasing junction voltage by about 60 mV multiplies forward current by ten at room temperature. Thus 0.7 V is a convenient operating approximation for some silicon diodes, not a universal turn-on threshold. The bandgap, built-in voltage, and operating forward voltage are three different quantities.

At low injection, the minority-electron concentration at the p-side depletion edge scales as np(0) = (ni²/NA) exp(VD/VT). The bias changes the injected carrier population exponentially. That is the mathematical link between lowering the barrier and producing a large diffusion current.

10 · Why the initial diffusion is not a current ceiling

Doping determines an equilibrium carrier concentration; it does not provide a bag of electrons that gets used up once. A wire also contains a finite density of mobile electrons yet carries current continuously because charges enter and leave. Battery-driven contacts maintain flow through the semiconductor.

In a simple drift model, J = q(nμn + pμp)E. Concentration, mobility, field, and device area all affect current. Carrier density alone does not specify a maximum current. At high fields the simple mobility model fails, and drift velocity can approach saturation.

The hypothetical instant of joining previously isolated p and n regions is a transient with particular initial conditions, not a universal maximum. An arbitrarily sharp starting concentration step is itself an idealization. Forward bias subsequently maintains nonequilibrium carrier populations and can produce high-level injection, where injected minority carriers are comparable to or exceed the background doping.

Real limits include series resistance, carrier transport, contacts, heat removal, and maximum ratings. A practical diode eventually departs from the simple exponential law. So “limited dopant count” is not a fixed throughput limit, although doping strongly influences resistance and device behaviour.

Your broader intuition about controlling conductivity is right. Doping changes carrier populations; gates in field-effect transistors change them electrically; illumination and temperature also affect them. Scattering by lattice vibrations and impurities limits mobility. These effects are distinct from the junction’s polarity-dependent injection barrier.

MIT, nonideal diode effects.

11 · Five times the built-in voltage: solve the whole circuit

Five times 0.714 V is about 3.57 V. That number alone cannot determine current. Include a series resistor and find how the supply voltage divides between resistor and diode. With R = 1 kΩ and an assumed 0.7 V forward drop, I ≈ (3.57 − 0.7)/1000 = 2.87 mA. A 5 V supply gives approximately 4.3 mA. Directly connecting a stiff voltage source can produce excessive current: the diode does not automatically acquire a protective high resistance.

The Zener example is a deliberately different diode. Ordinary rectifiers have their own reverse ratings; an LED can tolerate much less reverse voltage. Leakage and breakdown voltage require a particular device specification.

Heating must be compared under stated conditions. For an ohmic resistor, P = I²R at fixed current, but P = V²/R at fixed voltage. Larger resistance therefore does not always mean more heat. A forward diode dissipates approximately VDI; at 0.7 V and 4.3 mA this is 3.0 mW. The 1 kΩ resistor dissipates about 18.5 mW. Reverse-biased below breakdown, a diode can have a large voltage and tiny power because its current is tiny.

12 · What breakdown actually changes

A reverse-biased junction has a strong field across depletion. Two mechanisms can make its reverse current rise sharply.

Avalanche: an existing carrier gains enough energy from the field to create an additional electron–hole pair in a collision. Those carriers accelerate and can create more pairs. The field separates the pairs and sustains a large reverse current.

Zener tunnelling: in a sufficiently heavily doped junction, the depletion region is very thin and the field very strong. Electrons can tunnel from occupied valence-band states on the p side into available conduction-band states on the n side. This is a quantum transition, not ordinary thermal climbing over the forward barrier.

Both mechanisms may contribute in a real device. Breakdown is an electrical regime, not necessarily immediate physical destruction. Zener and avalanche-rated devices are designed to operate there within current, power, and temperature limits. An unprotected diode may overheat and fail.

Reverse conduction beyond breakdown therefore does not contradict the ordinary one-way approximation. That approximation explicitly assumes reverse voltage below breakdown.

13 · Shorting, grounding, or touching depletion with a wire

Shorting the external terminals: in a dark diode at uniform temperature, an external short sets terminal voltage to zero. After transients, the equilibrium junction barrier remains. Equal electrochemical potentials around the complete circuit prevent a sustained current powered by the built-in field. Contact potentials and carrier concentration effects matter; the internal electrostatic step alone is not an accessible battery.

If the diode was previously biased, its stored depletion or injected charge can discharge transiently. If light continuously generates carriers, a short-circuit photocurrent can flow, powered by the light. Those are different conditions from extracting endless energy from an equilibrium junction.

Grounding one point: ground is a chosen potential reference or a conducting connection, not an unlimited sink for every positive and negative charge. Grounding one terminal does not specify the voltage of the other. A real contact placed inside depletion changes local electrostatics and may create a multi-terminal structure.

Bridging the charged regions with nanoscale metal: the ions are dopant atoms bound in the lattice, not two exposed capacitor plates. A conducting bridge can shunt the junction and cause local charge rearrangement. The new metal–semiconductor interfaces can be ohmic or rectifying, depending on material, doping, and geometry. A tiny contact can also perturb states and introduce leakage.

Some ions can change charge state as local electron occupancy changes, but connecting a wire does not permanently cancel the doping-induced equilibrium everywhere. The full structure settles into new self-consistent fields and carrier populations. Without dimensions and contact details there is no single quantitative answer for this proposed nanodevice.

14 · Did theory come before the diode?

The development involved repeated feedback between experiments and theory. Rectifying crystal contacts were used before the modern quantum explanation was complete. Quantum mechanics and band theory then supplied the language for allowed states, filled bands, electrons, and holes.

A key silicon p–n junction discovery was experimental: Russell Ohl at Bell Labs observed unusual light sensitivity in a silicon sample in 1940. Investigation with Jack Scaff connected its different regions to different impurities. The junction was discovered through material behaviour, rather than simply assembled after a complete textbook prediction.

That discovery fed later deliberate device design. Bardeen and Brattain demonstrated the point-contact transistor in 1947; Shockley developed the junction-transistor concept in 1948. As theory and materials processing improved, engineers intentionally controlled doping profiles and junction geometry to obtain predictable characteristics.

A vacuum-diode analogy can help with rectification: a heated cathode emits electrons readily, while an unheated anode usually does not, so reversing voltage changes which charge supply is available. But its one-way mechanism is thermionic emission and collection across a vacuum. A p–n diode uses carrier populations, injection barriers, diffusion, and drift inside a solid; it does not need a heated emitting cathode.

15 · Rebuild your original mental picture

Keep the useful part of your model: electrons, bonding, available states, and an electric field must account for the current everywhere. Replace the idea that p is an electron-blocking substance with this sequence:

  1. Doping sets the equilibrium populations. Electrons dominate in n; holes dominate in p. Both regions can conduct in either direction.
  2. Joining them makes a concentration gradient. Diffusion exposes fixed ions near the boundary.
  3. The ions establish a barrier. At equilibrium, drift and diffusion balance.
  4. The battery changes that barrier. Forward bias lowers it and sustains injection; reverse bias raises it and ordinarily leaves only small leakage.
  5. Current passes between carrier descriptions. Injection, valence-band transport, recombination, and contacts maintain continuity without requiring one electron to travel the entire loop.
