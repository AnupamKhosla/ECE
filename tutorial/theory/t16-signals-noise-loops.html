Signals: sampling, decibels, noise and 4–20 mA loops

How a smooth real-world signal becomes a stream of numbers without lying to you, how to read any dB figure in your head, where noise comes from and how cables beat it, and why every plant room in the world sends its measurements as a current between 4 and 20 milliamps.

Reading ~70 minLevel Beginner → intermediateBuilds on T04, T15Used in P12, C01, C10

Analogue and digital signals

A signal is a quantity that changes in time and carries information. The voltage from a temperature sensor, the current in a pressure transmitter's cable and the light pulses in an optical fibre are all signals.

  • An analogue signal is continuous in time and in value. Between 1.20 V and 1.21 V there are infinitely many possible values, and the signal has a value at every instant.
  • A digital signal is a list of numbers: values taken only at certain instants, and each one rounded to one of a fixed set of levels.

Turning analogue into digital therefore needs two separate steps, and each has its own way to go wrong:

  1. Sampling (cutting time into slices): measure the signal at regular instants, say 1000 times per second. Risk: aliasing, where a fast signal pretends to be a slow one.
  2. Quantisation (cutting value into steps): round each sample to the nearest level the ADC can represent. Risk: quantisation error, a small rounding error on every sample.

Chapter T15 showed how a successive-approximation ADC does both in about 100 µs on an Arduino. This chapter is about choosing how often and how finely to do it, and about protecting the analogue signal on its way to the ADC.

Sampling and the Nyquist idea

Think of a film camera. It takes 24 still pictures every second. For a person walking, that is plenty. But in old Westerns, wagon wheels sometimes seem to turn slowly backwards. The spokes move almost one full spoke-spacing between frames, so each new picture looks like the spokes moved a little backwards. The camera has created a motion that is not really there. This is aliasing: a fast signal, sampled too slowly, appears as a slower false signal, an alias.

The rule

To capture a sine wave of frequency f without aliasing, you must take more than two samples per cycle. The sampling rate fs must be greater than 2f. Half the sampling rate, fs/2, is called the Nyquist frequency: the highest frequency a given sampling rate can represent honestly.

fs > 2 fmax     falias = | f − k·fs | (k = the whole number that makes this smallest) Worked: f = 900 Hz, fs = 1000 Hz, k = 1 → alias = |900 − 1000| = 100 Hz. Another: 50 Hz mains hum sampled at 60 samples per second → |50 − 60| = 10 Hz, a slow wobble that looks like a real change in your reading. Any frequency above fs/2 folds back below it.

Examples you know: audio CDs sample at 44.1 kHz because human hearing ends near 20 kHz (2 × 20 = 40 kHz, plus some room). Telephone speech is sampled at 8 kHz because the phone network only passes up to about 3.4 kHz.

Two practical rules follow:

  • In practice, sample 5–10 times faster than the highest frequency you care about. Exactly 2× is not enough: if the samples happen to land on the zero crossings, you see a flat line.
  • Remove what you cannot sample before the ADC. Noise and interference above fs/2 will alias down into your band. An anti-aliasing filter, even a simple RC low-pass filter in front of the ADC input (T05), removes it first. Industrial analogue input cards include such filters for exactly this reason.

On the job

Aliasing shows up in the field more often than people realise. A BMS that logs a value every 15 minutes cannot see a fan that cycles on and off every 10 minutes; the trend log may show a slow, false wave or a flat line. A cheap scope set to a slow timebase can show a 50 Hz signal as a strange slow wobble. When a trend looks odd, ask: "Is my sampling fast enough for what I am looking at?"

Quantisation and resolution

After sampling, each value is rounded to one of 2ⁿ levels, where n is the number of bits of the ADC. The voltage difference between neighbouring levels is the step size, also called 1 LSB (one least-significant bit).

Step (1 LSB) = Vref / 2ⁿ Vref is the ADC's full-scale reference voltage. (Some datasheets divide by 2ⁿ − 1 instead; the difference is tiny for 10 bits and above.)

Example: a 4–20 mA signal into an Arduino through a 250 Ω resistor gives 1–5 V (explained below). 1 V is code 1/5 × 1024 ≈ 205, 5 V is code 1023. So the useful range is only about 818 steps, not 1024, because the bottom 20 % of the ADC range is spent on the "live zero". For a 0–10 bar transmitter that is 10 / 818 ≈ 0.012 bar per step, still plenty.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A 12-bit ADC is more accurate than a 10-bit one." Right: bits give resolution, the size of the smallest step. Accuracy is how close the reading is to the truth, and it depends on the reference voltage, the sensor, offsets, gain errors and noise. A 12-bit ADC on a noisy, drifting 3.3 V rail can be less accurate than a 10-bit ADC with a good reference. If the bottom 3 bits of a reading jump around randomly, you do not really have 12 bits; you have 9 bits and some noise.

Decibels: the language of ratios

Electronics deals with huge ranges. A Wi-Fi signal arriving at your phone may be ten million times weaker than one measured near the access point. Writing such ratios as plain numbers is clumsy, so engineers use a logarithmic scale: the decibel (dB). A decibel always describes a ratio between two values, never an absolute amount on its own (unless a reference is named, like dBm below).

Logarithms have one magic property: they turn multiplication into addition. If an amplifier multiplies power by 100 and a cable then divides it by 4, the total is 100/4 = 25 times. In decibels: +20 dB − 6 dB = +14 dB. Engineers add and subtract dB in their heads all day for exactly this reason.

Power ratios: 10 log

GdB = 10 · log₁₀(P₂ / P₁) log₁₀(2) = 0.301, so doubling power is 10 × 0.301 = +3.01 dB, written "+3 dB". Halving power is −3 dB. Ten times the power is +10 dB; a hundred times is +20 dB; a thousand times is +30 dB.

Voltage ratios: 20 log

Power in a resistor is P = V²/R. If the voltage doubles, the power goes up four times. So for a voltage (or current) ratio, the square turns into a factor of 2 in front of the logarithm:

GdB = 10 · log₁₀(V₂²/V₁²) = 20 · log₁₀(V₂ / V₁) Doubling voltage is 20 × 0.301 = +6 dB (four times the power, which is also +6 dB: the two formulas agree). Ten times the voltage is +20 dB (a hundred times the power). This works when both voltages are across the same resistance, which is the normal case.

dBm: decibels with a reference

Add a letter and the dB figure becomes an absolute quantity. dBm means "dB relative to 1 milliwatt".

PdBm = 10 · log₁₀(P / 1 mW)    PmW = 10^(PdBm / 10) 0 dBm = 1 mW. +10 dBm = 10 mW. +20 dBm = 100 mW (a typical Wi-Fi transmitter). +30 dBm = 1 W. −30 dBm = 1 µW. −60 dBm = 1 nW. Every 10 dB is a factor of 10.

Wi-Fi survey tools show received signal strength in dBm. A common rule of thumb in the industry: around −30 dBm is right next to the access point, −67 dBm or better is good for voice and video, and below about −80 dBm the link becomes unreliable. Note that −67 dBm is 0.2 nanowatts. Radios work with astonishingly small powers.

Worked example: a fibre loss budget

Fibre technicians live in dB. A single-mode link has a transmitter at −3 dBm. The fibre is 10 km long with a typical loss of 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm. There are 2 connectors at about 0.5 dB each and 4 fusion splices at about 0.1 dB each. What arrives?

Loss = 10 × 0.35 + 2 × 0.5 + 4 × 0.1 = 3.5 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 4.9 dB
Received = −3 dBm − 4.9 dB = −7.9 dBm = 10^(−0.79) mW ≈ 0.16 mW Notice you just subtract. Without dB you would multiply 0.923¹⁰ × 0.891² × 0.977⁴. If the receiver needs at least, say, −20 dBm, the margin is 12.1 dB. Career chapter C09 uses this exact method with an OTDR.

Signal-to-noise ratio

Every real signal arrives with some noise on top. What matters is not the noise alone but how big it is compared with the signal. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) says this in dB:

SNR = 10 · log₁₀(Psignal / Pnoise) = 20 · log₁₀(Vsignal,rms / Vnoise,rms) Example: a 1 V signal with 10 mV of noise → 20 × log₁₀(100) = 40 dB. The same 10 mV of noise on a 50 mV thermocouple-sized signal → 20 × log₁₀(5) = 14 dB, which is poor. Small signals are the ones that suffer.

An ADC adds its own rounding noise. For an ideal n-bit ADC measuring a full-scale sine wave, the best possible SNR is about 6.02 n + 1.76 dB. Each extra bit buys about 6 dB (each bit halves the step, and halving a voltage is 6 dB). A 10-bit ADC tops out near 62 dB, a 12-bit near 74 dB and a 16-bit near 98 dB. If the analogue noise at the input is worse than this, extra bits are wasted.

Where noise comes from

Thermal noise: the floor you cannot remove

The electrons in any resistor jiggle randomly because of heat, just like gas molecules. At any instant there are a few more moving one way than the other, so a tiny random voltage appears across every resistor, even one sitting on the bench with nothing connected. This is thermal noise (Johnson–Nyquist noise):

vn,rms = √(4 k T R B) k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann's constant), T = temperature in kelvin, R = resistance, B = bandwidth in Hz (the range of frequencies you are measuring). Example: 10 kΩ at 300 K over 10 kHz: √(4 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 300 × 10⁴ × 10⁴) ≈ 1.3 µV. That is about 3,800 times smaller than one 4.88 mV step of an Arduino ADC.

So for typical sensor work, thermal noise is far below what your ADC can see. It matters in radio receivers and precision instruments. Notice that it grows with bandwidth: measuring over a narrower range of frequencies (filtering, averaging) lets in less noise. That is why averaging 16 ADC readings reduces random noise by √16 = 4 times.

Interference: the noise you can do something about

On a real site, nearly all trouble comes from interference: energy from other equipment coupling into your cable. There are three main paths:

Capacitive (electric field)

A conductor at changing voltage, such as a 230 V cable, forms a tiny capacitor with your signal wire and pushes small currents into it. Worst for high-impedance inputs (voltage inputs, floating wires). Beaten by shielding and distance.

Inductive (magnetic field)

A conductor carrying changing current, such as a motor cable, makes a changing magnetic field. Any loop of wire in that field gets an EMF (Faraday, T06). Beaten by small loop area: twisted pairs and keeping signal and return wires together.

Conducted and radiated

Noise travelling along shared supply and earth wires, or radio-frequency energy from radios and fast-switching electronics. Variable speed drives (VSDs) in plant rooms are notorious sources. Beaten by filtering, good earthing and separation.

In Australia the most common interference you will see on a scope is 50 Hz hum and its harmonics (100, 150 Hz …) from the mains supply, because mains cables run everywhere in a building.

Fighting noise: twisted pairs, shields and ground loops

Twisted pairs cancel magnetic pickup

A changing magnetic field induces an EMF in a loop proportional to the loop's area (Faraday's law). A signal wire and its return, run separately, form a big loop. Twist them together and the loop is divided into many small loops, and each twist reverses the loop's direction. The EMF induced in one half-twist is cancelled by the opposite EMF in the next. Twisting also makes both wires pick up almost exactly the same capacitive noise. Noise that appears equally on both wires is called common-mode noise, and a receiver that looks only at the difference between the wires (a differential receiver) ignores it. Ethernet, RS-485, CAN, 4–20 mA loops and telephone lines all use twisted pairs for this reason.

Shields catch electric-field pickup

A shield is a foil or braided metal layer wrapped around the conductors. Electric-field interference lands on the shield instead of on the signal wires, and the shield drains it away to earth. A shield is only useful if it is connected to earth somewhere; a floating shield can actually make things worse. At low frequencies, a shield does little against magnetic fields; that is the twisting's job.

Ground loops, and why shields are usually earthed at one end

In a big building, "earth" is not one perfect point. Earth conductors carry small leakage currents and occasionally fault currents, and they have resistance, so the earth bar in a plant room and the earth at a field device 200 m away can sit at slightly different voltages, often with a 50 Hz component. Now connect a cable shield (or a signal 0 V) to earth at both ends. You have made a closed loop: shield → earth at one end → building earth → earth at the other end → back to the shield. The voltage difference drives a current around this loop, and the loop is also a large one-turn antenna for magnetic fields. That current flows in the shield right next to your signal and couples into it. This is a ground loop, and it is the classic cause of hum in audio systems and wobbling readings in instrumentation.

That is why, for analogue instrument cables (4–20 mA, thermocouples, RTDs), the common practice is to earth the shield at one end only, normally at the control panel, and to cut back and insulate it at the field end. For high-frequency (radio-frequency) shielding the rules differ, and data cables such as shielded Ethernet are terminated as their manufacturer's system specifies. On any real job, follow the site specification and the equipment manual.

Bench tip

A floating multimeter or a battery-powered handheld scope breaks ground loops for free. If a reading is noisy on the mains-powered bench scope but clean on a battery meter, suspect a ground loop through the scope's earth lead. Never "fix" this by cutting the earth pin off a mains plug: the earth pin is a safety device, and only a licensed electrician may work on mains plugs and wiring in Australia.

In Australia

The wiring rules (AS/NZS 3000) and the telecommunications cabling standard (AS/CA S009) both require separation or barriers between low-voltage power cabling and ELV, data and telecom cabling. Good separation is also good noise practice: distance weakens both capacitive and magnetic coupling. Confirm the current separation distances with the standards themselves (or ACMA and your state electrical regulator), because rules are revised and site specifications may be stricter.

Why industry sends current: 4–20 mA

Imagine a pressure sensor on a pump 300 m from the control panel. If it sends its reading as a voltage, three things go wrong:

  • Voltage drop. Any current drawn through 600 m of wire (out and back) causes a drop, and the panel sees less than the sensor sent.
  • Earth differences. If the sensor's 0 V and the panel's 0 V differ by some millivolts or volts (see ground loops), that difference adds straight onto the signal.
  • Noise pickup. A voltage input usually has a high input resistance, so small induced currents create large noise voltages across it.

Now send a current instead. The sensor's transmitter, the cable and the receiver are all in series in one loop. Kirchhoff's current law (T04) says the current is the same at every point in a series loop: charge cannot pile up anywhere. If the transmitter forces 12.00 mA, then 12.00 mA flows through every metre of cable and through the receiver, whether the cable is 10 Ω or 100 Ω. The wire resistance changes the voltage needed to push the current around, but not the current itself, as long as the supply has enough voltage to spare. The receiver measures the current, so it reads exactly what the transmitter sent. And because the receiver is a low resistance (typically 250 Ω or less), induced noise currents make only small noise voltages.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Current gets used up along a long cable, so the far end sees less." Right: current is not used up; energy is. In a series loop the current is identical everywhere. What the cable consumes is voltage (as I × Rwire), and that only matters if the supply runs out of voltage. This is the whole reason 4–20 mA works.

Why 4 mA and not 0 mA: the live zero

The range is 4 mA = 0 % and 20 mA = 100 % of the measurement. Starting at 4 instead of 0 gives two big benefits:

  1. Broken wires are detectable. A cut cable, a loose terminal, a blown fuse or a dead transmitter gives 0 mA. With a 0–20 mA scale, 0 mA would look like a valid "0 %" reading. With 4–20 mA, anything well below 4 mA is clearly a fault. A widely used recommendation (NAMUR NE 43) treats readings below 3.8 mA or above 20.5 mA as outside the valid measurement range, and transmitters signal a detected internal fault by driving the loop to about 3.6 mA or below, or 21 mA or above.
  2. The transmitter can power itself. Even at 0 %, 4 mA flows, and the transmitter's own electronics can run on the part of it that they use. That makes the two-wire transmitter possible: the same two wires carry power and signal.
I = 4 mA + 16 mA × (value − LRV) / (URV − LRV)    value = LRV + (I − 4) / 16 × (URV − LRV) LRV and URV are the lower and upper range values (for a 0–10 bar transmitter, 0 and 10 bar). The span is 16 mA. Worked: 12 mA → (12 − 4)/16 = 50 % → 5.0 bar. 7.2 mA → 20 % → 2.0 bar. For a 0–50 °C transmitter, 15.2 mA → (15.2 − 4)/16 = 70 % → 35 °C.

Two-wire loop-powered transmitters

The most common field device in industry and in building plant rooms is the two-wire (loop-powered) transmitter. It has only two terminals, + and −. It does not have its own power supply. Instead:

  1. A 24 V DC power supply in the control panel pushes current around the loop.
  2. The current goes out on one wire to the transmitter's + terminal, through the transmitter, out of its − terminal, back along the second wire, through the receiver's measuring resistor, and back to the supply's 0 V.
  3. The transmitter acts as a controlled current sink: it measures the process (pressure, temperature, level) and adjusts how much current it lets through, between 4 and 20 mA. Its electronics run on the voltage left across its own terminals and on less than 4 mA of the loop current.

The 250 Ω resistor: from current to voltage

ADCs measure voltage, so the receiver converts current to voltage with a precision resistor, using nothing more than Ohm's law:

V = I × 250 Ω → 4 mA → 1.00 V, 12 mA → 3.00 V, 20 mA → 5.00 V The resistor should be a precision type (0.1 % tolerance is common), because any error in R is an error in every reading. Many PLC and BMS analogue input cards contain this resistor already and just need the right jumper or configuration (for example "4–20 mA" instead of "0–10 V"). The 250 Ω value is also handy for HART, a digital signal that rides on top of the 4–20 mA current and needs some loop resistance to work.

On an Arduino with a 5 V reference, 20 mA gives exactly 5 V, the very top of the ADC, so over-range and fault-high currents (20.5–22 mA) cannot be seen. A slightly smaller resistor, such as 200 Ω (20 mA → 4.0 V, 24 mA → 4.8 V), leaves headroom. On a 3.3 V board, use around 150 Ω (20 mA → 3.0 V). Always check that the resistor's voltage at the highest current stays inside the ADC's input range.

The loop voltage budget

The current does not depend on wire resistance, but the loop still needs enough voltage to push that current through everything in series. Kirchhoff's voltage law (T04) says the drops around the loop must add up to the supply. The transmitter only gets what is left over, and it stops working properly if that falls below its minimum (from its datasheet; for many 2-wire transmitters it is somewhere around 10–12 V). Always check at the highest current, because that is where the resistive drops are largest.

Safety

A 24 V DC loop is extra-low voltage and safe to work on, and everything in this course stays at ELV. Some field instruments, however, are "4-wire" devices with a separate 230 V mains supply in the same enclosure, and some panels contain mains circuits next to the 24 V terminals. In Australia only a licensed electrician may work on the mains side. If a device or panel has mains wiring, do not open or measure it; ask the site's electrician to isolate it. Some sites also have hazardous-area (explosive atmosphere) instruments that need specific training and certification before anyone touches them.

Bench tip

To measure loop current without breaking the loop, many transmitters have "test" terminals across a diode, and clamp meters made for milliamps can read the current through the insulation. Otherwise, measure the voltage across the known 250 Ω resistor and divide: 3.20 V / 250 Ω = 12.8 mA, which is 55 % of span. A loop calibrator (a handheld tool that can source, sink or measure 4–20 mA) is the instrument technician's everyday tool.

0–10 V signals and their weak spots

Building services use a lot of 0–10 V signals (and the live-zero version, 2–10 V): valve and damper actuator commands, variable speed drive speed references, some room sensors. They are cheap and simple: the controller output drives a voltage, and the actuator's input has a high resistance, often tens of kilohms or more, so it draws only a fraction of a milliamp. Over short runs inside a plant room they work well. Over long runs they have three weaknesses:

  1. Shared common wires. Many 24 V actuators use three wires: 24 V supply, common (0 V) and the 0–10 V signal. The actuator's supply current returns on the same common wire that the signal is measured against. Example: an actuator drawing 0.2 A, with 50 m of 0.75 mm² common wire (R = 1.72×10⁻⁸ × 50 / 0.75×10⁻⁶ ≈ 1.15 Ω), drops about 0.23 V on the common. The actuator measures its signal against its own, shifted 0 V, so the position is off by about 2.3 % of span. With an AC-powered actuator that shift is a 50 Hz ripple instead.
  2. No broken-wire detection with 0–10 V. A cut signal wire reads 0 V, which is a perfectly valid "0 %, valve closed". The 2–10 V version adds a live zero for the same reason 4–20 mA has one.
  3. Noise and earth differences add directly. Any voltage between the controller's 0 V and the device's 0 V, and any induced noise, adds straight onto the signal. In a current loop, the receiver only sees the current.

On the job

"The valve says 100 % on the BMS screen but it's only half open" and "the pressure reading drops to zero every afternoon" are classic service calls. For the first, measure the 0–10 V at the actuator terminals, not at the controller, and check the common for voltage drop. For the second, a reading at or below about 3.8 mA (often a loose terminal moving with heat) means a wiring fault, not a real pressure. Knowing the scaling formula, the live zero and the loop voltage budget lets you diagnose both with a multimeter in minutes.

Check yourself

A vibration of 180 Hz is sampled at 200 samples per second. What frequency do the samples show, and how would you prevent this?

The Nyquist frequency is 100 Hz, so 180 Hz aliases. falias = |180 − 1 × 200| = 20 Hz. The data shows a convincing but false 20 Hz vibration. Prevent it by sampling faster (5–10 times the highest real frequency, so 1–2 kHz here) and by putting a low-pass anti-aliasing filter in front of the ADC to remove anything above fs/2.

What is the step size of a 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference, and what temperature step does that give with a 10 mV/°C sensor?

3.3 / 4096 = 0.806 mV per step. With 10 mV per °C, one step is 0.0806 °C. That is the resolution; the accuracy will be worse, set by the sensor and reference.

An amplifier has a voltage gain of 10. A cable after it loses 6 dB. What is the overall gain in dB and as a voltage ratio?

Voltage gain 10 = 20 log₁₀(10) = +20 dB. Overall: 20 − 6 = +14 dB. As a voltage ratio, 10^(14/20) ≈ 5.0. Check: 6 dB is a voltage halving, and 10 ÷ 2 = 5. dB lets you add instead of multiply.

A Wi-Fi reading changes from −50 dBm to −60 dBm. How much did the received power change?

A drop of 10 dB is a factor of 10 in power. −50 dBm = 10 nW; −60 dBm = 1 nW. The power fell to one tenth.

Why are analogue instrument cable shields usually earthed at one end only?

Earthing at both ends closes a loop through the building's earth system. Any voltage difference between the two earth points (and any magnetic field through the loop) drives current along the shield, right next to the signal pair, injecting hum. Earthing at one end (normally the panel) still drains electric-field pickup, but leaves no closed loop for current to flow in.

A 0–10 bar 4–20 mA transmitter reads 0.0 mA at the panel. A colleague says "the pump has no pressure". What do you say?

No pressure would give 4 mA, not 0 mA. 0 mA means no current flows in the loop at all: a broken or loose wire, a blown fuse, missing 24 V supply, or a dead transmitter. This is the "live zero" doing its job. Check loop supply voltage, then terminals, then the transmitter.

A loop has a 24 V supply, a 250 Ω receiver, 60 Ω of cable and a loop-powered display that drops 4 V. The transmitter needs 12 V. Does it work at 20 mA?

Drops at 20 mA: receiver 0.02 × 250 = 5 V, cable 0.02 × 60 = 1.2 V, display 4 V. Total 10.2 V. Left for the transmitter: 24 − 10.2 = 13.8 V, which is above 12 V, so yes, with 1.8 V to spare. At a 22 mA fault current it would be 24 − 5.5 − 1.32 − 4 = 13.2 V, still fine.

What current corresponds to 35 °C on a −20 to 80 °C transmitter, and what voltage appears across 250 Ω?

Percentage of span = (35 − (−20)) / (80 − (−20)) = 55 / 100 = 55 %. Current = 4 + 0.55 × 16 = 12.8 mA. Voltage = 12.8 mA × 250 Ω = 3.2 V.
