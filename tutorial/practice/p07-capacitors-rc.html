Capacitors: timing, smoothing and decoupling

You will identify and read capacitors, time an RC circuit charging with a phone stopwatch and check it against the 63% rule, smooth the bridge rectifier from P06 and predict its ripple, and understand why every chip on every board has a small 100 nF capacitor right next to it.

Safety

Everything here is extra-low voltage: a 9 V battery and the low-voltage side of a sealed AC plug-pack. Electrolytic capacitors connected backwards, or above their voltage rating, heat up, vent and can burst with a bang and a spray of hot electrolyte. Wear safety glasses, check the stripe every time, and keep your face away the first time you power a new circuit. Never open mains equipment to look at its capacitors: large capacitors inside power supplies can stay charged to hundreds of volts after unplugging, and that work belongs to licensed people.

1 · Know your capacitors

A capacitor is two conductors separated by a thin insulator (the dielectric). Push charge onto one plate and the same amount leaves the other; a voltage builds up between them. Capacitance C tells you how much charge it stores per volt: Q = C × V. One farad is huge, so real parts are in microfarads (µF, 10−6), nanofarads (nF, 10−9) and picofarads (pF, 10−12).

Voltage ratings: leave margin

The voltage rating is the maximum the capacitor may see, including peaks and ripple. Exceed it and the dielectric can break down. A good habit is to choose a rating of at least 1.5 times the highest voltage the capacitor will see. For the P06 bridge on a 12 V AC plug-pack, the capacitor sees about 16 V, and up to about 20 V with no load on a pack that reads high. So use 25 V or, better, 35 V parts there.

Bench tip

Most multimeters have a capacitance range (marked F or with the capacitor symbol). Discharge the capacitor first, then measure. It takes a few seconds for big values. Expect electrolytics to read anywhere within ±20% of the marking. Your measured value of the 1000 µF is the number to use in the timing lab.

2 · The RC charging lab: τ = 10 seconds

A resistor in series with a capacitor makes the simplest timer in electronics. The resistor limits how fast charge can flow in. As the capacitor fills, its voltage rises, the voltage left across the resistor falls, so the current falls, so it fills more slowly. That "the fuller it gets, the slower it fills" gives the famous exponential curve.

τ = R × C = 10 000 Ω × 0.001 F = 10 s τ (tau) is the time constant, in seconds when R is in ohms and C is in farads. After one τ the capacitor has charged 63% of the way. After 5τ it is effectively full (over 99%).

  1. Measure first. Battery voltage (a fresh "9 V" often reads 9.3–9.6 V), the resistor (expect 9.9–10.1 kΩ), and the capacitor on the meter's capacitance range if it has one. Calculate your own τ = R × C.

  2. Start empty. Short the capacitor through the 100 Ω resistor for a few seconds (not with a bare wire). The meter should read close to 0 V.

  3. Get the stopwatch ready. Easiest method: film the meter and a running phone stopwatch together with a second phone, then read the values off the video. Otherwise, one person calls out times while the other reads the meter.

  4. Charge. Move the flying lead to battery + and start timing at the same moment. Record the voltage at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 seconds.

  5. Discharge. At about 60 s, move the lead to 0 V and restart timing. Record again at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 s.

  6. Compare with the table and the curve. Work out your real τ: the time at which the voltage reached 63% of the battery voltage.

Why it works

Current is charge per second, and the capacitor's voltage is its stored charge divided by C. At the start the capacitor is empty, so the whole 9 V sits across the resistor and the current is 9 V / 10 kΩ = 0.9 mA. As charge builds, the capacitor voltage pushes back against the battery, leaving less voltage across the resistor, so less current. The rate of filling is always proportional to the gap still left, which is exactly what produces an exponential. The energy ends up split: half stored in the capacitor (½CV²), half turned to heat in the resistor, whatever the resistor value.

Theory T05 · Electric fields and capacitors
Common misconception

Wrong: "A capacitor stores charge, like a battery stores charge." Right: a capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates. The total charge on it is zero: +Q on one plate, −Q on the other. Charging moves electrons from one plate, round the circuit, to the other. And unlike a battery, its voltage falls steadily as you use the energy, which is why capacitors are poor for running things for long but excellent at handling fast changes.

Why your numbers may differ

  • Tolerance. Electrolytics are often ±20%, so a "1000 µF" part gives τ anywhere from 8 to 12 s. Your measured τ tells you the real capacitance: C = τ / R.
  • Leakage. Electrolytics leak a little current through the dielectric. With a 10 kΩ series resistor, a leakage of 20 µA costs 0.2 V, so the final voltage may stop a few tenths below the battery. A capacitor that has sat unused for years leaks more at first and improves after some time powered ("reforming").
  • The meter. A digital meter's input is about 10 MΩ, a thousand times bigger than 10 kΩ, so it barely loads the circuit here. With 1 MΩ resistors it would matter.
  • Reaction time. Half a second of stopwatch error is 5% of τ. Filming the meter and stopwatch together removes most of it.
On the job

RC timing is everywhere you look once you know it: the delay on a security light, the debounce on a door contact input, the soft start in a power supply, the "hold-up time" that lets a controller ride through a short power dip. When a BMS or alarm input "misses" short pulses, an RC filter on that input is often the reason. The same maths sets how fast a temperature sensor responds inside its pocket.

3 · Smoothing the bridge rectifier from P06

The bridge gave you pulses: 100 humps per second, from 0 V up to about 15.6 V. A big capacitor across the output fills up at each peak and then supplies the load between peaks. The output becomes nearly flat DC with a small sawtooth ripple.

Between peaks, the capacitor alone supplies the load current I for about one ripple period (10 ms for full-wave). Charge lost = I × t, and the voltage drop is that charge divided by C.

ΔV ≈ I / (f × C) ΔV is the peak-to-peak ripple. I is the load current. f is the ripple frequency: 100 Hz for a full-wave bridge on Australia's 50 Hz, 50 Hz for half-wave. C is in farads. It slightly overestimates the ripple, which is the safe direction.

Worked examples

  • Bridge, 1000 µF, 1 kΩ load at about 15 V (15 mA): ΔV = 0.015 / (100 × 0.001) = 0.15 V.
  • Same, 470 Ω load (32 mA): ΔV = 0.032 / 0.1 = 0.32 V.
  • Half-wave, 1000 µF, 32 mA: f = 50 Hz, ΔV = 0.032 / 0.05 = 0.64 V (double the bridge).
  • Design: a 12 V camera supply needs 0.5 A with at most 1 V ripple from a bridge. C = I / (f × ΔV) = 0.5 / (100 × 1) = 0.005 F = 5000 µF, so use 4700 µF if you accept slightly over 1 V, or 6800 µF for margin.

  1. Rebuild the P06 bridge with the 1 kΩ load. Measure V DC: about 10 V (average of the pulses).

  2. Power off. Add 1000 µF 25 V across the bridge output: + leg to the bridge +, stripe to the bridge −. Check the stripe twice. Glasses on.

  3. Power on. V DC jumps to about 15–16 V: the capacitor holds the peaks.

  4. Read the ripple. Switch the meter to V AC across the capacitor. Many meters ignore the DC part on this range and show the RMS of the ripple, about 0.3 × the peak-to-peak value. Expect around 0.04–0.06 V for 0.15 V peak-to-peak. Not every meter behaves this way, so treat it as a rough check; a scope (P15) shows the real sawtooth.

  5. Change the load to 470 Ω (1 W). DC drops slightly, ripple roughly doubles.

  6. Swap the capacitor for 100 µF. The ripple is about ten times bigger, and the DC reading falls because the average is now well below the peak.

  7. Power off and discharge the capacitor through 100 Ω before you pull it out.

Bench tip: the hidden cost of smoothing

Because the diodes conduct only in short windows near each peak, they carry pulses several times bigger than the average load current. That makes the transformer's RMS current higher than the DC current you draw, roughly 1.5–2 times for this kind of circuit. So a "1 A" AC plug-pack feeding a bridge and capacitor should only be asked for about 0.5–0.6 A DC. A big capacitor also draws a large inrush current at switch-on, which is one reason fuses for these supplies are "slow-blow".

In Australia

Mains here is nominally 230 V at 50 Hz (AS 60038), so full-wave ripple is at 100 Hz. Equipment designed for North America (60 Hz mains, 120 Hz ripple) has slightly smaller smoothing capacitors for the same ripple. That rarely matters for low-voltage plug-packs sold locally, which are designed for 50 Hz, but it is one reason imported transformers can run hotter here. Confirm supply details for a specific site with the network operator or the electrician.

4 · Decoupling: the 100 nF next to every chip

Open any circuit board and you will see small capacitors sitting right beside every integrated circuit (IC), from the power pin to ground. They are almost always 100 nF ceramics. They are called decoupling or bypass capacitors. Here is why they are there.

A digital chip does not draw a smooth current. Every time its internal transistors switch (millions of times per second), it gulps a short spike of current, lasting a few nanoseconds. The supply is far away along wires and copper tracks. Those have inductance: they resist changes in current. The voltage lost across an inductance is V = L × (ΔI / Δt).

V = L × ΔI / Δt = 100 nH × 0.05 A / 5 ns = 1 V A 10 cm jumper wire has roughly 100 nH of inductance (about 1 nH per millimetre is a useful rule). A 50 mA spike lasting 5 ns makes the chip's supply dip by about 1 V. On a 5 V or 3.3 V chip, that can corrupt data, reset a microcontroller, or make a 555 timer trigger at random.

A 100 nF capacitor a few millimetres from the pins acts as a tiny local tank. The spike draws its charge from the capacitor instead of from the distant supply. How much does the capacitor's voltage dip?

Q = I × t = 0.05 A × 5 ns = 0.25 nC ΔV = Q / C = 0.25 nC / 100 nF = 2.5 mV 2.5 mV instead of 1 V. The supply then refills the capacitor slowly, between spikes, which the long wires can manage.

The rules that work

  • One 100 nF ceramic per IC power pin, as close to the pin as possible, straight to ground. On a breadboard, put it across the chip's own power and ground pins, not at the end of the rail.
  • One bulk capacitor per board or breadboard, 10–100 µF electrolytic, where power comes in. It covers slower changes, like a motor starting or a relay clicking.
  • Why 100 nF and not bigger? A small ceramic has very low internal inductance, so it still behaves like a capacitor at tens of megahertz. A big electrolytic has more internal inductance and resistance, so at those speeds it stops helping. That is why boards use both: each covers a different speed range. 100 nF is also cheap, tiny and has become the habit that works for most logic.
  • Datasheets win. Some chips (radios, fast processors, regulators) specify their own values and placement. Follow them.
Try this (in P09)

When you build the 555 timer, first leave out the 100 nF across its power pins and run a relay or a motor from the same supply. You may see the timer glitch or restart when the load switches. Add the 100 nF right at pins 8 and 1, and add 100 µF where the supply enters the breadboard. The glitches usually stop. That is decoupling you can see.

On the job

The capacitor that most often fails in the field is the electrolytic in power supplies: plug-packs, DVR and NVR power boards, PoE switches and controller PSUs. Heat dries out the electrolyte, capacitance falls and internal resistance rises, ripple grows, and the equipment starts rebooting, or the camera picture shows rolling bars. A failed electrolytic often has a domed or split top, or brown crust at the base. On site you replace the power supply, not the capacitor. But recognising the symptom ("reboots when warm, fine when cold") saves hours.

5 · Capacitor safety

Capacitors are the one component that can hurt you after the power is off. The energy stored is:

E = ½ × C × V² 1000 µF at 16 V: ½ × 0.001 × 16² = 0.13 J. Harmless, but shorting it with a screwdriver still makes a spark and pits the tip. 10 000 µF at 50 V: 12.5 J. That is a loud bang, a hot wire and a possible burn. The 400 V capacitors inside mains power supplies can hold tens of joules at a lethal voltage, which is one reason you do not open mains equipment.
  • Discharge before touching. Use a resistor, not a bare wire: 100 Ω to 1 kΩ for the ELV capacitors in this course. Hold it across the terminals for a few τ (100 Ω × 1000 µF = 0.1 s, so a second is plenty). Then confirm with the meter that it reads close to 0 V.
  • Polarity, every time. Reversed electrolytics conduct, heat, build gas pressure and vent. The scored cross on the top is a pressure vent designed to split before the can explodes. It is still violent.
  • Voltage rating, every time. A 16 V capacitor on a "12 V AC" bridge (about 17–20 V) is over-voltage and will fail, sometimes slowly, sometimes loudly.
  • Bulging or leaking parts: replace, do not reuse. The electrolyte is corrosive; wash hands.
  • Stored charge in "off" equipment: some devices keep a charge for a long time, and big capacitors can "recover" some voltage after being discharged (dielectric absorption). Measure before touching, even if you discharged earlier.

Check yourself

What is the value of a ceramic capacitor marked 473K?

47 followed by 3 zeros = 47 000 pF = 47 nF = 0.047 µF. K means ±10% tolerance.

With 1000 µF and 10 kΩ from 9 V, what will the meter show after 20 seconds of charging, and why?

τ = 10 s, so 20 s is 2τ. After 2τ the capacitor has charged 1 − e−2 = 86% of the way: about 7.8 V. Each τ closes 63% of the remaining gap: 63% after the first, then 63% of the remaining 37% after the second.

You measure 63% of the supply after 12.5 s with a 10 kΩ resistor. What is the real capacitance?

τ = 12.5 s, so C = τ / R = 12.5 / 10 000 = 1.25 mF = 1250 µF. That is within the +20% tolerance of a 1000 µF electrolytic, or the resistor is a little high. Measure R to separate the two.

Why is the ripple frequency 100 Hz for a bridge rectifier in Australia, and what happens to the ripple with a half-wave rectifier?

Australian mains is 50 Hz. A bridge produces two humps per cycle, so the capacitor is topped up 100 times a second and only has to carry the load for about 10 ms. A half-wave rectifier tops it up 50 times a second, so the capacitor must carry the load for 20 ms and the ripple doubles for the same C and I (ΔV = I / (f × C)).

A bridge must supply 2 A with no more than 2 V of ripple. What capacitor do you need?

C = I / (f × ΔV) = 2 / (100 × 2) = 0.01 F = 10 000 µF. Choose a voltage rating with margin above the peak, and a type rated for high ripple current. Also remember the transformer must be rated well above 2 A because the charging pulses are large.

Why does a 100 nF capacitor need to be within a few millimetres of the chip, and not just "somewhere on the board"?

The capacitor only helps if the spike current can reach it through a low-inductance path. Every millimetre of track adds roughly 1 nH. A long path means V = L × ΔI/Δt is large, so the supply pin still dips. Close placement keeps the current loop (capacitor → VCC pin → chip → GND pin → capacitor) small.

On an SMD tantalum capacitor, the band is at one end. Which lead is it?

The positive lead. This is the opposite of an aluminium electrolytic, where the stripe marks the negative. Reversing a tantalum can make it fail short and burn. Always check the datasheet or the + marking.

Why is shorting a charged capacitor with a screwdriver a bad habit, even at 16 V?

With almost no resistance, the current is huge for a very short time. That damages the capacitor internally, pits the tool, and makes a spark. With larger capacitors or higher voltages the energy (½CV²) becomes dangerous. A resistor limits the current and turns the energy into gentle heat over a few time constants.

Go deeper (optional): why exactly 63%?

While charging, the current is i = (Vs − v) / R, and the capacitor voltage rises at dv/dt = i / C. Combine them: dv/dt = (Vs − v) / (RC). The rate is proportional to the remaining gap. The function whose rate of change is proportional to itself is the exponential, so the gap shrinks as e−t/RC. At t = RC the gap is e−1 = 0.368 of the start, so the capacitor has covered 1 − 0.368 = 0.632 of the way. Nothing about 63% is special to electronics: a cup of tea cooling, a tank filling through a pipe, and a sensor warming up in its pocket all follow the same curve.
