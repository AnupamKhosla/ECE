Theory Book · T21

Switching converters, gate drivers and feedback

Understand how a welder converts rectified mains into controlled arc current: timed switching, changing magnetic flux, charge-driven gates and feedback that can inhibit the output.

Study + exercises 90–120 minLevel After T05, T06, T08, T13Context India · family repair shop

Why switch instead of burning off the voltage?

An ideal switch has nearly zero voltage across it when on and nearly zero current through it when off, so its instantaneous loss vi is small in both states. A real switch has conduction drop, leakage and finite transitions; much of the engineering controls those losses while transferring power through inductors and transformers.

A welding inverter uses high-frequency switching so that its transformer can transfer power at a smaller magnetic size than a comparable 50 Hz design. The transformer does not convert steady DC continuously. Switching must create changing flux, and the design must reset the core flux to avoid saturation.

v = N × dΦ/dt
ΔB = (1 / NAe) × ∫v(t) dtN is winding turns and Ae is effective core area. Applied volt-seconds change flux density. Too much volt-second imbalance can drive the core toward saturation, causing a sharp current rise. Higher frequency alone does not make arbitrary transformer drive safe.

Frequency is different from duty cycle

Frequency counts cycles per second. Duty cycle is the on fraction within one period for a specified signal. At 20 kHz, a period is 50 µs. A 40% pulse lasts 20 µs and leaves 30 µs off in this simple single-signal example. Real bridge controls impose their own timing limits, and oscillator frequency need not equal an individual output’s switching frequency.

This is a timing model, not a recommended gate waveform or welder duty range. Output current is set by the whole converter, load and control loop; it is not simply the duty percentage times the front-panel amp rating.

Why the gate needs a driver

A gate is insulated but capacitive. Every transition needs charge moved onto or off it. A tiny DC leakage does not imply tiny switching current. The driver’s local supply and decoupling capacitor deliver brief current pulses; the gate loop and series resistor influence the transition.

Igate,avg ≈ Qg × fsw
ttransition ≈ Qinterval / Igate
Pdrive ≈ Qg × ΔVgate × fswIllustration: 100 nC per cycle at 40 kHz averages only 4 mA, yet moving 30 nC of Miller charge in 100 ns needs about 0.3 A during that interval. The correct charge comes from the relevant datasheet curve, not a fixed input-capacitance number alone.

As collector/drain voltage changes, gate-to-collector/drain coupling also needs charge moved. This can create a Miller plateau in the gate trace. Fast switching-node changes can inject unwanted gate current into a supposedly off device. The driver’s sink path, impedance, layout and any clamp/off-bias scheme help control that risk.

The gate voltage is defined relative to that device’s source/emitter. A high-side device’s reference moves. Isolated or level-shifting drive and correctly specified probing are necessary to interpret it. See TI SLUA618A for detailed gate-drive circuits.

Dead time, overlap and reset

In a half-bridge leg, if the upper and lower devices conduct together, they can short the DC bus: shoot-through. A suitable dead interval allows one device to turn off before its complement conducts. Propagation delay, IGBT tail, diode recovery, temperature and tolerances matter. “More dead time” is not an unlimited improvement; it also changes commutation and losses.

Do not confuse complementary-leg dead time with transformer reset time. For example, a two-switch forward converter intentionally switches its two primary devices together; its off interval and reset network serve a different function. Timing must be interpreted within the actual topology.

From a knob to regulated welding current

In constant-current MMA operation, the knob supplies a current command. A sensor estimates actual current. A control circuit compares command with feedback and adjusts the power stage’s switching. Arc voltage varies with arc conditions, so a fixed pulse percentage would not reliably hold current.

Controller terminology: ST calls the SG3525 a voltage-mode PWM controller. External circuitry can close a welding-current loop around it. That is distinct from a controller whose internal pulse-by-pulse modulation is peak-current-mode. Identify the chip and the surrounding feedback separately.

Auxiliary supply and startup sequence

The control circuit needs power before it can drive the main power stage. A startup path may charge an auxiliary controller’s supply until it begins switching; a winding or converter then sustains the rail. Undervoltage lockout prevents operation below the controller’s intended supply range. A shorted rail or dried auxiliary capacitor can produce repeated start/stop behaviour.

Precharge, relay closure, references, sensor checks and drive enable may happen in a specific order. This is why a series lamp or low input voltage can change the symptoms. Use the exact startup description before deciding that a relay or missing PWM is faulty.

A 40 kHz oscillator is present, but one output repeats at 20 kHz. Is the chip faulty?

Not necessarily. Controllers with alternating outputs may divide oscillator cycles between them. Read the timing description and the actual topology before treating the frequency difference as a fault.
