Fire detection and EWIS basics

Fire detection is life-safety electronics: detectors, supervised circuits and a panel that must never miss a fire and must always report its own faults. This chapter explains the fire indicator panel, detector types, conventional zones and addressable loops, end-of-line supervision, the links to HVAC, lifts and the fire brigade, and emergency warning systems, with the Australian standards and entry roles around them.

What a fire detection system must do

A fire detection and alarm system has three jobs: detect a fire early, warn the people in the building so they can leave, and call the fire brigade. It also controls other building systems so they help instead of spreading smoke: air handling units stop or switch to smoke control, lifts return to a safe floor, doors held open by magnets close.

Because lives depend on it, the design philosophy is different from a BMS. Every wire is supervised: the panel constantly checks that each circuit is intact, and raises a fault if a wire breaks or shorts. A failure must never be silent. The system has its own battery backup. And the fire system is always the master: it tells the BMS what to do, never the reverse.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A fire alarm system is just smoke alarms wired together."
Right: the battery or 230 V smoke alarms in a house (to AS 3786) are self-contained alarms. A commercial system is built around a fire indicator panel that supervises every detector circuit, records events, signals the brigade, and drives interfaces to other systems. They are different products under different standards.

The fire indicator panel (FIP)

The FIP is the brain. It is usually near the main entrance, where the fire brigade arrives, with a block plan (a simple floor plan showing zones) mounted beside it. Its main functions:

  • Monitor detector circuits (zones or loops) and manual call points, and show alarms and faults on its display and indicators.
  • Signal the fire brigade (or a monitoring centre) through alarm signalling equipment.
  • Drive outputs: warning devices, the EWIS or occupant warning system, and relay outputs to other systems.
  • Allow control: acknowledge, silence alarms, reset, and isolate (disable) a zone, a device or an output for testing or maintenance.
  • Log events with time and date, which is evidence after an incident.

The FIP runs from a dedicated 230 V circuit (installed by an electrician) and has standby batteries, typically two 12 V sealed lead-acid batteries in series for 24 V. If mains fails, the batteries must run the system in standby for a set time and then still sound the alarm for a set time.

Battery Ah ≈ (Istandby × tstandby + Ialarm × talarm) × margin Worked example with illustrative numbers: standby current 0.35 A for 24 h = 8.4 Ah; alarm current 2.0 A for 0.5 h = 1.0 Ah; total 9.4 Ah; with a 25 % ageing margin, 11.75 Ah, so you would fit 2 × 12 V batteries of the next standard size up (for example 12 Ah or larger). Use the standby and alarm times required by the current standard and the panel manual, not these example figures. A battery calculation is part of every commissioning file.
Safety

The FIP's mains terminals and power supply are 230 V: licensed electrician work only. The 24 V batteries are ELV but can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit, enough to melt a spanner and burn you; disconnect them with insulated tools and never short the terminals. Detector testing often means ladders or elevated work platforms; boom-type platforms of 11 m or more need a high risk work licence in Australia.

Detectors and call points

Photoelectric smoke detectors

The most common detector in Australian buildings. Inside a dark plastic chamber, an infrared LED sends a beam across, and a photodiode sits at an angle where it cannot see the beam directly. Clean air: almost no light reaches the photodiode. Smoke: particles scatter the light, some of it into the photodiode, and the signal rises. When it passes a threshold (and stays there long enough), the detector goes into alarm.

Other detector types

Heat detectors

Fixed temperature types alarm above a set temperature; rate-of-rise types alarm when temperature climbs quickly. Used where smoke detectors would false alarm: kitchens, car parks, plant rooms, dusty areas.

Multi-criteria

Combine smoke and heat sensing (some add other sensors) and use an algorithm to decide. Better at rejecting false alarms from steam or cooking fumes while still catching real fires.

Aspirating (ASD)

A fan draws air through a network of pipes with small holes to a very sensitive detector. Used in data centres, comms rooms, warehouses, heritage buildings and cold stores. Often known by a well-known brand name, VESDA.

Beam detectors

A light beam across a large open space (atrium, warehouse); smoke reduces the received light.

Ionisation (legacy)

Older detectors that use a tiny radioactive source. Rarely installed now. When removed, they must be disposed of according to the applicable rules, not thrown in a bin.

Manual call points

The red "break glass" or push-in point near exits. Anyone can raise the alarm by hand. Often resettable with a key.

The FIP also monitors other fire equipment: sprinkler flow switches (water flowing means a sprinkler head has opened), valve monitoring switches, and suppression systems.

Conventional zones and end-of-line supervision

The older and simpler arrangement is the conventional system. The building is divided into zones (for example "Level 3 east"). Each zone is a pair of wires running from the panel past every detector on that zone, all connected in parallel, ending at an end-of-line (EOL) resistor. The panel knows only which zone is in alarm, not which detector.

How the panel tells normal, alarm and fault apart

This is just Ohm's law and parallel resistors, which you proved on the bench in Practice P04. In our example panel (values vary between makers):

StateWhat is connectedResistance the panel seesPanel shows

Notice two important facts. First, the EOL must be at the end. If it is fitted at the panel "to clear a fault", a break anywhere on the cable would leave the EOL still connected: the panel would show normal while half the detectors were cut off. Second, a detector in alarm latches: it keeps drawing its alarm current until the panel resets the zone by briefly removing power.

Why it works

The panel drives the zone through a known series resistance and measures the zone voltage, exactly a voltage divider. Each state gives a distinct voltage band. Cable resistance (about 24 Ω per kilometre of loop for 1.5 mm² two-core) is tiny compared with the kΩ values, so a long zone still reads correctly.

Addressable loops

Most new commercial systems are addressable. Instead of radial zones, a loop leaves the panel, passes through every device, and returns to the panel. Each device has its own address (set by DIP switches, a programmer tool or automatically), and the panel polls each one with a digital protocol over the same two wires that power it. The panel shows exactly which device is in alarm ("Detector 2.045, Level 3 meeting room"), and analogue addressable detectors report their smoke level, so the panel can flag a dirty detector before it false alarms.

Conventional

  • Alarm reported by zone only
  • EOL resistor supervision
  • Cheap, simple, small buildings
  • Many brands of detector work with many panels

Addressable

  • Each device reported by address and text
  • Loop polled digitally, with isolators
  • Detector sensitivity and dirt monitoring
  • Loop protocol usually belongs to the panel maker, so devices are mostly brand-matched
Bench tip

On an addressable loop, an input/output module lets the panel read a contact (a sprinkler flow switch, a fire damper end switch) or drive a relay (to the BMS, a door holder, a lift) at any point on the loop. Many interface faults turn out to be a module programmed with the wrong function or an inverted contact.

Warning devices and brigade signalling

Sounders and strobes (visual warning devices) tell occupants to leave. In larger buildings the warning is given by an EWIS (below) with tones and voice messages. Warning circuits are supervised too, often with EOL resistors and polarity-reversal monitoring: the panel checks the circuit with reverse polarity in standby (blocking diodes in each sounder stop current), and switches to forward polarity to sound them.

The alarm signalling equipment (ASE) beside the FIP sends alarm and fault signals to the fire brigade or to a monitoring service, depending on the state and the building. When the brigade arrives, they use the FIP (or a brigade panel) and the block plan to find the fire.

In Australia

Fire services in several states can charge building owners for repeated unwanted (false) alarms. That is why every test must start by telling the monitoring provider and isolating the brigade signal as the site procedure requires, and why detector choice and cleaning matter. Arrangements for brigade monitoring differ by state; check with the state fire service.

Interfaces: fire mode in the rest of the building

  • HVAC. On alarm, the fire system sends a "fire trip" to the mechanical services switchboard. Depending on the design, AHUs stop (so they do not spread smoke) or switch to a smoke control mode: stair pressurisation fans start, smoke exhaust fans run, and dampers move. Smoke control in HVAC is covered by AS 1668.1. A fire fan control panel near the FIP lets the brigade override fans manually.
  • BMS. The BMS usually receives fire status as a DI (see C01) for monitoring and to put its own logic in a safe state. The BMS must never be the only path that stops a fan in fire mode unless the design says so; the hard-wired fire trip at the MSSB does that.
  • Lifts. Lifts are recalled to a designated floor and taken out of normal service, so people do not ride into a fire floor.
  • Doors and access control. Magnetic door holders release so fire doors close. Fail-safe electric locks on exit paths unlock (see C06).
On the job

"Fire mode testing" is a classic joint test at commissioning and during annual services. The fire tech triggers an alarm; the BMS tech watches AHUs stop on the front end and in the plant room; the lift tech watches lifts home; the security tech checks doors. Then the fire tech resets, and everyone checks that their plant restarts correctly. A BMS tech who understands the fire side is valuable in these tests.

EWIS: emergency warning and intercommunication

In larger or taller buildings, simple bells are not enough. An EWIS (emergency warning and intercommunication system) gives staged, controlled evacuation:

  • A master emergency control panel (MECP), usually near the FIP, where the chief warden or brigade can choose which zones get which signal and make live announcements.
  • Speakers in every area, on supervised amplifier circuits, giving two distinct tones: an alert tone (prepare to evacuate) and an evacuate tone (leave now), each followed by recorded voice messages.
  • Warden intercommunication points (WIPs), the red phones on each floor, so floor wardens can talk to the chief warden at the MECP.
  • Visual warning devices where needed, for example for people with hearing impairment or in noisy areas.

Typically the fire floor (and sometimes adjacent floors) gets the evacuate signal while other floors get alert, so stairs do not overflow. Simpler buildings may use an occupant warning system with the same tones and messages but without the full intercom. EWIS speaker circuits are often 100 V line audio (a constant-voltage audio system); treat amplifier outputs with respect.

In Australia

Licensing and accreditation differ by state. In Queensland, the QBCC (Queensland Building and Construction Commission) issues fire protection occupational licences, and fire protection work generally needs the right licence class. In NSW, owners must provide an Annual Fire Safety Statement, and certain fire safety assessment and certification work requires an accredited practitioner, for example through the Fire Protection Accreditation Scheme (FPAS) run by Fire Protection Association Australia. Victoria has essential safety measures reporting. Other states have their own rules. Any work on the FIP's 230 V supply needs an electrical licence. These rules change and differ by state, so check with the state regulator before you take on any fire work.

What a fire alarm technician does on site

Much of the entry-level work is routine testing to AS 1851: visiting buildings on a schedule (for example monthly, six-monthly and yearly routines) and testing a set portion of devices and functions each time, then recording the results in the site logbook and the company's system. A typical test visit:

  1. Sign in and notify. Sign the logbook, tell building management, and contact the monitoring provider to put the system "on test" as the procedure requires.

  2. Isolate what must not operate. Isolate the brigade signal and any interfaces that should not trip during the test (for example lifts or AHUs), following the site's isolation procedure. Record every isolation.

  3. Check the panel. Indicators, display, event log, battery voltage and charger, and that there are no existing faults or forgotten isolations.

  4. Test devices. Use a detector tester pole with a smoke cup (test aerosol) or a heat cup on the scheduled detectors; operate call points with the test key; confirm each alarm at the panel. Test warning devices and EWIS speakers in the scheduled areas.

  5. Test interfaces scheduled for this visit, with the other trades where needed.

  6. Restore and reset. De-isolate everything you isolated, reset the panel, confirm the system is normal with no faults, and tell the monitoring provider the test is complete.

  7. Record. Fill in the logbook and report defects (dirty detectors, damaged call points, faults) with clear locations, so they can be quoted and fixed.

Safety

The most dangerous mistake in fire work is leaving a system isolated when you go home. A building with an isolated brigade signal or zone has no protection there. Keep a written list of every isolation you make, and do not leave site until the panel shows no isolations and the monitoring provider confirms normal.

Entry roles and training

  • Fire alarm test technician / trainee: routine AS 1851 testing, under supervision at first. This is the most common way in.
  • Fire alarm service technician: fault-finding, repairs, panel programming, defect rectification.
  • Installer / commissioning technician: new systems on construction sites (White Card needed); often electricians or electrical apprentices.

Training: there are nationally recognised Certificate II and Certificate III qualifications in fire protection (for example in inspection and testing), often done as a traineeship while working. Search training.gov.au for the current qualification names and codes. Employers value electronics knowledge (Ohm's law, supervised circuits, multimeter skills), a driver's licence, comfort working at heights, and tidy record-keeping.

Try this

Build a conventional zone on your breadboard at 12 V: a 470 Ω "panel" resistor feeding a pair of wires, three push-buttons each with a 680 Ω resistor in series (your "detectors"), all in parallel, and a 4.7 kΩ EOL at the far end. Measure the zone voltage in normal, with one button pressed (alarm), with a wire pulled out before the EOL (open), and with the two wires shorted. The 470 Ω series resistor limits the short-circuit current to about 25 mA, but it then dissipates about 0.3 W, so use a 0.5 W (or larger) resistor there. Then program an Arduino to read the zone voltage on an analogue input (through a divider to stay under 5 V) and print NORMAL, FIRE, OPEN or SHORT. Labs: P04, P12.

Interview questions

"What does an end-of-line resistor do?"

It gives the panel a known resistance at the far end of a circuit so it can supervise the wiring: normal reads about the EOL value, an open circuit reads very high, a short reads near zero, and an alarm reads a distinct lower value. It must be at the end of the line, after the last device.

"What's the difference between a conventional and an addressable system?"

Conventional reports alarms by zone using EOL-supervised radial circuits. Addressable puts every device on a polled loop with its own address, reports the exact device, can monitor detector sensitivity, and uses isolators so a single short or break does not disable the loop.

"What do you do before testing a fire system?"

Sign in, notify building management and the monitoring provider, isolate the brigade signal and interfaces per the site procedure, record the isolations, check the panel for existing faults. After testing, de-isolate, reset, and confirm normal with the monitoring provider.

"What happens to the air conditioning in a fire alarm?"

The FIP sends a fire trip to the MSSB. Depending on the design, AHUs shut down or go into smoke control mode (stair pressurisation, smoke exhaust) to AS 1668.1, with a fire fan control panel for brigade override.

"Which standard covers routine testing?"

AS 1851, routine service of fire protection systems and equipment. Design and installation is AS 1670.1; EWIS is AS 1670.4. Always confirm the edition that applies.

Check yourself

A zone has a 4.7 kΩ EOL. A detector goes into alarm with a 680 Ω load. What resistance does the panel see (ignoring cable and standby currents)?

Parallel: 4,700 × 680 / (4,700 + 680) = 3,196,000 / 5,380 ≈ 594 Ω. Much lower than 4.7 kΩ, so the panel reads FIRE.

An installer fits the EOL resistor at the panel terminals instead of at the last detector. What goes wrong?

The panel always sees the EOL, so it reads normal even if the cable is broken further along. Detectors beyond the break are silently disconnected: exactly the failure supervision is meant to prevent.

Why does the panel need to remove power briefly to reset a conventional zone?

Conventional detectors latch in alarm, drawing alarm current until their supply is interrupted. Removing power clears the latch; if smoke is still present, the detector alarms again.

On an addressable loop with isolators, a short occurs between isolators 2 and 3. What does the panel report and what keeps working?

Isolators 2 and 3 open, cutting out only the section between them; the panel reports a short-circuit fault (and the devices in that section as missing). All other devices keep working, fed from both ends of the loop.

Why are heat detectors used in kitchens instead of photoelectric smoke detectors?

Cooking fumes and steam scatter light in a photoelectric chamber and cause false alarms. A heat detector responds to temperature, which normal cooking does not raise to the alarm point at ceiling level.

A 1.5 mm² zone cable is 500 m long. Roughly what loop resistance does it add, and does it matter?

About 24 Ω per km of loop, so about 12 Ω. Compared with a 4.7 kΩ EOL (0.26 %) or a ~600 Ω alarm, it is negligible.

You finish a test and the panel shows "1 isolation active". What do you do?

Find what is isolated (check your isolation list and the panel), confirm it should be restored, de-isolate it, reset, and confirm the panel shows normal with no isolations and no faults before telling the monitoring provider and leaving. If it must stay isolated (a known defect), it must be reported and recorded per the site procedure.
