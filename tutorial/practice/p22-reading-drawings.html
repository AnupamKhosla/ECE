Reading drawings: schematics, wiring diagrams and as-builts

On site, the drawing set tells you what to install, where it goes and where every wire lands. By the end of this lab you can read the main kinds of drawing, trace a wire from a schematic to a real terminal, find your way with cross-references, and mark up an as-built so the next technician can trust it.

This lab is mostly reading and marking up. You can do all of it on screen, but printing two or three pages and marking them by hand teaches the habit faster.

Bench tip

Print the drawing at the paper size written in the title block (for example A1), or remember that a smaller print changes the scale. A drawing marked 1:100 at A1 becomes 1:200 when printed on A3, because A3 is half the width of A1... no, A3 is a quarter of the width? Check: A1 is 841 mm wide and A3 is 420 mm wide, so A3 is half as wide, and the scale halves to 1:200.

The drawing set: who draws what, and why

A building project produces a drawing set: dozens or hundreds of sheets, each with a number and a revision. Different drawings answer different questions. The common beginner mistake is to look for the wrong answer on the wrong drawing, for example trying to find a terminal number on a floor plan.

Think of it like a map collection. A road atlas, a street directory and a house plan all show "where things are", but at different zoom levels and for different jobs.

In Australia

Most Australian electrical and electronic drawings use IEC-style symbols (from the IEC 60617 symbol library; older drawings may cite the old Australian symbols standard AS 1102). You will also meet American-style symbols on equipment made for the US market, such as zig-zag resistors and ladder-style control drawings. Security and communications floor plans use whatever symbols the consultant or company chooses. Drawings vary by company, so always read the legend first.

Title blocks, revisions and status

Before you read a single symbol, read the title block. It is the box in the bottom-right corner of every sheet. It tells you what the drawing is, which version it is, and whether you are allowed to build from it.

What to read in the title block, every time

  1. Drawing number and sheet. "SEC-L1-101, sheet 4 of 12". When you phone the office, quote this, not "the security plan".

  2. Revision. Letters (A, B, C) or numbers (0, 1, 2). Some companies use letters before construction and numbers after; others the reverse. The revision table shows the history. The latest row is the one that counts.

  3. Status. "Preliminary", "For tender", "For approval", "Issued for construction" (often written IFC), "As built". Never install from a drawing that is not issued for construction unless your supervisor tells you so in writing.

  4. Scale and paper size. "1:100 at A1" means 1 mm on an A1 print is 100 mm in the building. Many drawings also say "Do not scale": use written dimensions when they exist.

  5. Clouds and triangles. A revision cloud (the bumpy outline) marks what changed. The triangle next to it carries the revision letter. When a new revision arrives, look for the clouds first.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The drawing in the van is the drawing." Right: the drawing in the van might be two revisions old. The company keeps a drawing register (a list of every sheet and its current revision). Check your sheet's revision against the register at the start of each job. Installing from a superseded drawing is one of the most common and most expensive site mistakes.

One circuit, two drawings

Here is the key idea of this lab. A schematic and a wiring diagram can describe exactly the same circuit, but they are organised differently:

Schematic: organised by function

The supply rail runs along the top and 0 V along the bottom. Current "flows" down the page through each path. Parts are placed where they make the logic easy to follow. The relay coil and its contact can be far apart on the page, even on different sheets, linked only by the name K1.

Wiring diagram: organised by place

Parts are drawn where they physically sit: the power supply, fuse and relay on the DIN rail, the terminal strip at the edge of the panel, the field devices outside. Every terminal has its real number. You can wire the panel from it without understanding the circuit, which is exactly why mistakes hide in it.

The circuit: a 24 V DC power supply G1 feeds a fuse F1. A push-button S1 on the wall energises relay coil K1. The relay's normally-open contact switches a 24 V indicator lamp H1. A diode D1 across the coil absorbs the voltage spike when the coil switches off. You built this kind of circuit in P08. Everything here is extra-low voltage.

The rule for contact numbers is neat once you see it. The first digit is which contact set (1st, 2nd …). The second digit is its function: 1–2 is a normally-closed path, 3–4 is a normally-open path, and on a changeover contact 1 is common, 2 is the NC side, 4 is the NO side. So "21–24" means "second contact set, normally-open side". Letter codes vary between companies and between old and new standards; the legend sheet or the first sheet of the set will list the ones used.

Why it works

A wire number names a node: every point that is joined by copper, and so sits at the same voltage. That is Kirchhoff's idea that a connected conductor is one equipotential (ignoring the tiny drop along the wire). So if you measure 24 V on one end of wire 2, every other end of wire 2 must also read 24 V. If it does not, that piece of copper or one of its terminations is broken. D1 is there because the coil is an inductor: when current through it is cut, it tries to keep the current flowing and the voltage spikes. The diode gives that current a safe path.

Wire numbers, ferrules and terminal numbers

Three different numbers can sit on or near one wire end. Keep them apart in your head:

Terminal number

Belongs to the place: X1:3, K1:A1, S1:13. Printed on the terminal or on the device.

Wire number

Belongs to the wire: printed on a marker sleeve at both ends. Tells you which node the wire is part of.

Cable number

Belongs to the multi-core cable between two places: C1, L1-C07. Labelled on the sheath at both ends. Cores inside are identified by colour or number.

Two common numbering systems

Common misconception

"Ferrule" means two different things on Australian sites. A bootlace ferrule is the small metal tube you crimp on the end of a stranded wire so it clamps cleanly in a screw terminal (see P05). A ferrule marker (often just "ferrule") is the printed sleeve that slides over the wire to show its number. When a supervisor says "ferrule every wire", ask which one; usually it means both.

Bench tip

Fit the marker sleeves before you crimp the bootlace ferrule, and print them so they read from left to right as you look at the terminal (or from the bottom up for vertical wires). Most sites expect markers to be readable without twisting the wire. A label printer with heat-shrink sleeve tape saves hours on a big panel.

Cross-references: "see sheet 4, ref C7"

Large drawings do not fit on one sheet. A relay coil may be on sheet 3 and its contacts on sheets 5 and 9. A cable may leave one sheet and continue on another. Cross-references are the links between those places, like page references in a book.

Every sheet has a zone grid printed around its border: numbers along the top and bottom, letters down the sides (see Figure 1). A zone is a cell of that grid. The reference "sheet 4, ref C7" means: open sheet 4, go to row C, column 7. Software often writes the same thing in short form, for example /4.C7 or 4-C7, or only the column, /4.7, when each column holds one circuit.

The three cross-references you will meet most

  1. Continuation arrows. A wire runs off the edge of the drawing area into an arrow labelled "to 5/D2". On sheet 5, zone D2, a matching arrow says "from 4/C7". Same wire number at both arrows.

  2. Contact cross-reference under a coil. Under relay coil K1 you see a little table: "11–14 ▸ 5" means "its NO contact is drawn in column 5". Under the contact, "(coil: 3)" points back. In Figure 2 these are the small grey notes.

  3. Detail and section marks. On floor plans, a circle with "3 / SEC-501" means "detail 3, drawn on sheet SEC-501". This is how you find the reader mounting height or the back-box detail.

"to 5/D2" → open sheet 5 → row D, column 2 → find the same wire number If the wire number at the far arrow does not match, stop: either the drawing is wrong or you are on the wrong revision. Tell your supervisor before you wire anything.

System drawings: block and riser diagrams

A block diagram shows a system as boxes and lines: NVR, switches, cameras, access panel, the link to the building network. It has no wire detail. Read it first on a new job to get the "story" of the system.

A riser diagram is a block diagram stacked by floor. The vertical shaft that carries cables between floors is the riser. The diagram shows what equipment is on each floor, how many devices there are, and which backbone cable feeds each floor. It is not to scale and does not show where on the floor a device sits; that is the floor plan's job.

How to read a riser diagram in five questions

  1. Where is the head end? (Comms room, ground floor.) This is where recording, panels and the main switch live.
  2. What is the backbone to each floor? (Fibre for the network, RS-485 for access control.) Fibre is used when the run is longer than Ethernet's 100 m channel limit or between buildings; short risers sometimes use Cat6 instead.
  3. What is on each floor, and how many? Count devices: 4 + 3 + 3 = 10 cameras, 2 + 3 + 2 = 7 doors. Check the counts match the floor plans and the equipment order.
  4. Which outstation feeds which device? A level 1 camera goes back to SW-L1, not to the comms room. This tells you where to terminate and where to look when it fails.
  5. What powers what? Cameras get PoE from their floor switch. Door locks are usually powered from the door controller's power supply. Look for UPS and battery notes.
On the job

On a commissioning day the riser diagram is the checklist. You bring up the ground floor, then level 1, then level 2, ticking each device off the diagram as it appears on the NVR or access software. A camera that is on the floor plan but missing from the riser diagram is a design question to raise early, before the ceiling closes. See C05 · CCTV and IP camera installation and C06 · Access control and intruder alarms.

Floor plans, symbols and the legend

A floor plan is the building seen from above, drawn to scale, with device symbols placed where they are to be installed. Each symbol carries a tag (a unique name such as CAM-G-01), and the legend explains every symbol used on that drawing.

Reading a floor plan without getting lost

  • Find north and a fixed reference. A north arrow, a grid line (columns are named A, B, C … and 1, 2, 3 …) or the lift core. Grid lines on architectural plans are the most reliable way to set out a device.
  • Read the notes box. Mounting heights ("all readers 1100 mm AFFL", above finished floor level), cable types, and "refer to detail" marks live there.
  • Check the ceiling type. The reflected ceiling plan (RCP) shows ceiling tiles, plasterboard, bulkheads and lights. A camera on a solid plasterboard ceiling needs an access plan; a tile ceiling does not.
  • Measure, do not guess. At 1:100, 1 mm on the print is 100 mm in the room. If a written dimension exists, it beats your ruler.

Worked example: estimating a cable run from a plan

A data outlet on an A3 print of a drawing marked "1:100 at A1" is 85 mm (measured along the cable tray route) from the comms cupboard. How long is the cable?

Scale on A3 = 1:200 (A3 is half the width of A1)
Horizontal run = 85 mm × 200 = 17 000 mm = 17.0 m
+ drop into the rack 3 m + drop down the wall 3 m + service loops 2 × 1 m = 25 m Ceiling heights and loops are typical allowances; use the real ceiling height from the sections. 25 m is well under the 90 m limit for a Cat6 permanent link, so this outlet is fine. Always round up and order extra: a cable that is 1 m short is a new cable.

Points lists and I/O lists (BMS and PLC)

An I/O list is the hardware view: for each controller, what is wired to each input and output terminal. A points list is the wider BMS view: every point the system knows about, including hardware I/O, network points read from other equipment (such as a chiller over BACnet or Modbus), and software points such as setpoints, with alarm limits and trend settings. On small jobs the two are one spreadsheet. The terms AI, AO, DI, DO (analogue/digital input/output) are explained in C01 · BMS fundamentals.

On the job

BMS technicians live in points lists. During commissioning you go down the list one row at a time: force the output or simulate the input, check the right thing happens at the plant and on the screen, then tick the row and initial it. The signed points list becomes part of the handover. See C02 · BMS protocols and commissioning.

Single-line diagrams: look, don't touch

A single-line diagram (SLD) shows how mains power is distributed through a building: the main switchboard, the distribution boards on each floor, and the circuit breakers that feed each one. Three-phase feeders are drawn as one line to keep the picture simple, which is where the name comes from.

As an ELV technician you read SLDs; you do not work on what they show. You use them to answer questions such as:

  • Which distribution board and circuit feeds my security panel's power supply? (So you can ask the electrician to isolate the right one.)
  • Is the comms rack on an essential supply, a UPS or a generator-backed circuit?
  • Which board label should I quote in my job notes?
Safety

In Australia, work on mains (230/400 V) wiring and switchboards is legally restricted to licensed electricians. Do not open a switchboard, remove a breaker cover or "just check" a mains terminal because the SLD makes it look simple. Your work starts on the extra-low-voltage side of the power supply. If the 24 V supply needs isolating at the mains, the electrician (or the site's isolation procedure) does it. See T09 · Mains, earthing and RCDs and C13 · Your first day on site.

As-builts: marking up what really happened

No job is built exactly as drawn. A sprinkler pipe sits where a camera was meant to go. The client deletes an outlet. A cable takes a different route because a wall is fire-rated. The as-built drawings record what was actually installed. They are produced from the site markups: a set of drawings that the technicians mark by hand (or in a PDF tool) as the job goes on.

How to mark up as-builts properly

  1. Keep one site set. One clean, current set of drawings is the markup set, kept in the site office or one shared PDF. Not five copies in five vans.

  2. Mark it the same day. Memory fades fast. At the end of each day, mark every change made that day.

  3. Show the change, not just the result. Cross out the old position, draw the new one, add a dimension from a fixed point ("1.2 m east of grid line C").

  4. Say why and who. A short note, your initials, the date, and any reference (an RFI number: a "request for information" sent to the designer, or a client instruction).

  5. Update the schedules too. New cable length, new outlet numbers, test results. A floor plan that disagrees with the cable schedule is worse than either alone.

  6. Hand over. The drafter turns markups into a new revision with status "As built". You check that drawing before it goes to the client.

On the job

Why bother? Because the next technician might be you, two years later, at 2 am, looking for a dead camera in a hospital ceiling. Good as-builts tell them which ceiling tile to lift and which switch port to check. Bad as-builts send them along the wrong corridor. Maintenance companies quote higher, and clients complain louder, when the as-builts are wrong. Accurate markups are one of the quiet skills supervisors notice in a new technician.

In Australia

Handover requirements are set by the project specification and contract, and for some systems by the standards they are built to (for example, fire systems and telecommunications cabling have their own documentation and labelling requirements; see C04 and C07). Read the specification's "documentation" or "handover" section early in the job so you know what records you must keep while you work.

Worked example: from drawing set to a finished door

Your task sheet says: "Install and terminate reader CR-L1-03." Here is the order in which experienced technicians use the drawings.

  1. Register and title block. Confirm the floor plan, riser and door-wiring drawings are the current revision and issued for construction.

  2. Floor plan. Find CR-L1-03 on the level 1 security layout. It is beside door L1-03 near grid line C/4, on the corridor side. The notes say "readers 1100 mm AFFL, see detail 2/SEC-501".

  3. Detail sheet. Detail 2 on SEC-501 shows the back box, the mullion or wall mounting, and the cable entry.

  4. Riser diagram. Door L1-03 is served by door controller DC-L1 in the level 1 cupboard, not by the panel in the comms room.

  5. Cable schedule. L1-A03 (reader data, 6-core shielded, 22 m), L1-A04 (lock power, 2-core) and L1-A05 (REX and door contact, 4-core). Label them as you pull them.

  6. Door wiring diagram. The typical door diagram shows which reader core lands on which DC-L1 terminal (power, 0 V, data lines, LED, shield) and the lock and REX terminals.

  7. Test and record. Present a card, check the door event appears in the software, check the lock releases and the door contact reports. Tick the door on the commissioning sheet, mark any change on the site set.

Check yourself

You need to know which terminal the lamp feed lands on. Which drawing do you open, and why not the schematic?

The wiring (connection) diagram. The schematic shows how the circuit works (the relay contact switches the lamp) but not where things sit or their real terminal numbers. The wiring diagram shows K1:14 → X1:3 → H1 +.

A wiring diagram says a wire is marked "2" at both ends. Under node numbering, what does that tell you about the voltage at each end?

Both ends are on the same node, so they should be at the same voltage (here +24 V after the fuse). If you measure 24 V at one end and 0 V at the other, the wire or one of its terminations is open. That is a direct use of Kirchhoff's idea that connected copper is one equipotential.

What do the relay terminal numbers 11, 12 and 14 mean?

First digit 1 = the first contact set. Second digit: 1 = common, 2 = normally-closed side, 4 = normally-open side. So 11–14 closes when the coil is energised, and 11–12 opens. A second set would be 21, 22, 24. The coil is A1–A2.

A note says "cont. on 5/D2". What exactly do you do?

Open sheet 5, use the border grid to find row D and column 2, and look for the matching continuation arrow with the same wire or cable number. If the numbers do not match, stop and check the revision before wiring.

The drawing says "1:100 at A1" but you have an A3 print. You measure 60 mm. How far is that in the building?

A3 is half the width of A1, so the print is at 1:200. 60 mm × 200 = 12 000 mm = 12 m. Better still, look for a written dimension, because prints can also be "fit to page" and the scale is then unknown.

What is the difference between a riser diagram and a floor plan?

A riser diagram shows the system by floor: what equipment and how many devices are on each level and how each floor is fed (backbone cables up the riser). It is not to scale. A floor plan shows the exact position of each device on one floor, to scale, with tags and a legend.

What is the difference between a points list and an I/O list?

An I/O list covers the physical inputs and outputs of each controller: what is on each terminal. A points list covers every point the BMS knows about, including hardware I/O, network points from other equipment, and software points like setpoints, often with alarm and trend settings.

You moved a camera 1.2 m because of a sprinkler pipe. What goes on the as-built markup?

Cross out the old position, draw the new one with a dimension from a fixed reference (a grid line or wall), draw the new cable route, update the cable length and test result, and add a note with the reason, your initials, the date and any RFI or instruction number. Then update the cable schedule to match.

Skills checklist

  • I can name the main drawing types and say which question each one answers.
  • I read the title block first: drawing number, revision, status, scale and paper size.
  • I can trace a wire from a schematic to its real terminals on a wiring diagram.
  • I can explain relay terminal numbers (A1/A2, 11/12/14) and push-button numbers (13/14).
  • I know the difference between a terminal number, a wire number and a cable number, and between a bootlace ferrule and a ferrule marker.
  • I can follow a cross-reference like "sheet 4, ref C7" using the zone grid.
  • I can read a riser diagram and count devices per floor.
  • I read the legend before the plan, and I can estimate a cable length from a scaled plan.
  • I can read a cable schedule, an I/O terminal diagram and a points list together.
  • I mark up as-builts the same day, in the company's colours, with why, who and when.
