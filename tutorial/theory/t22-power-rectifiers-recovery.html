Power rectifiers: reverse recovery and real ratings

Extend the p–n junction picture into power repair: a forward test is only the beginning when a diode must carry large current and stop conducting quickly.

Study + exercises 60–90 minLevel After T11; pairs with W02Context India · family repair shop

One symbol, two very different jobs

The input bridge rectifies mains-frequency AC and feeds a capacitor bank. The output rectifier commutates transformer pulses at the inverter’s switching rate and carries welding current. Voltage stress, waveform, cooling and recovery requirements differ. A diode selected for one job is not automatically suitable for the other.

Why the forward drop is not always 0.7 V

Forward bias lowers the junction barrier and injects carriers. Current depends strongly on applied junction voltage and temperature; at high current, bulk and contact resistance also matter. A meter’s tiny current may give 0.55 V while the same diode loses much more at working current. The built-in equilibrium potential is not a fixed external turn-on voltage.

Pcond = average[vF(t) × iF(t)]A rough constant-drop estimate of 0.9 V at 100 A gives 90 W while that path conducts. Account for the actual duty and number of simultaneously conducting diodes. Small voltage drops matter at welding current.

Stored carriers and reverse recovery

A forward-conducting p–n diode contains excess carriers near its junction and in its bulk. When the circuit tries to reverse-bias it, those carriers must first be removed or recombine. For a short interval, reverse current flows before the diode resumes blocking. This is reverse recovery.

trr describes recovery time under a specified measurement definition; Qrr is recovered charge, the integral of reverse current over that interval. Temperature, forward current and imposed di/dt change them. Two diodes with similar trr can have different charge and softness. Recovery can increase the stress and loss of the transistor turning on against the diode.

A rough stress intuition is energy of order voltage × recovered charge, but this is not a universal loss equation for every converter. The measured waveform and device test conditions determine where energy is dissipated. Read ST’s ultrafast-rectifier overview for the forward-drop/recovery trade-off.

Schottky, ultrafast silicon and SiC

A Schottky junction uses a metal–semiconductor barrier. It avoids the same minority-carrier storage mechanism as a conducting p–n diode, so it has very little minority-carrier reverse recovery. It still has junction capacitance: changing its voltage moves charge and creates a transient current. “No reverse recovery” does not mean “no switching current”.

Conventional silicon Schottky devices trade forward drop, leakage and voltage capability differently from ultrafast p–n rectifiers. SiC Schottky devices extend voltage capability with their own characteristics. None is a universal substitute: topology, reverse stress, hot leakage, surge and mounting still matter.

Parasitic inductance and the snubber

Tracks, leads and transformer leakage all contribute inductance. Rapidly changing current creates voltage according to v = L × di/dt. Even 100 nH with a 100 A change in 100 ns gives an illustrative 100 V. That voltage adds stress during commutation; it is not predicted by the average output voltage.

A snubber or clamp manages stored energy, ringing and voltage stress. Its resistor, capacitor and diode values belong to the actual design. A missing snubber, a longer replacement lead or altered routing can change the switching stress even if the fitted semiconductor has the same headline rating. Do not invent replacement snubber values from a generic tutorial.

Read two actual datasheets

Open Vishay’s GBPC bridge family. Find the four-terminal circuit, current-vs-temperature conditions, surge rating and mounting details. The family includes several voltage/current variants; use the full code.

Then open ST’s STTH6003. Identify both diode paths, the common terminal and tab. Its headline 2 × 30 A rating is subject to the stated waveform and case temperature, not a promise that any mounting can carry 60 A continuously. The recovery figure also has specified test conditions. It is a fast-output example, not an approved replacement for every MMA welder.

  1. Sketch the actual pinout and mark all internally connected metal surfaces.

  2. Copy VRRM, relevant current ratings, forward-voltage conditions and the thermal path.

  3. For the fast diode, also copy trr/Qrr information if provided and its test conditions.

  4. Explain why the two parts cannot be judged interchangeable from voltage and current alone.

A diode has normal forward voltage at room temperature. What relevant faults can remain?

Excessive blocking leakage at working voltage or high temperature, breakdown weakness, excessive full-current loss, intermittent connections and unsuitable or degraded switching behaviour. The meter has only screened low-voltage static conduction.

Skills checklist

  • I can explain reverse recovery using stored carriers.
  • I can distinguish recovery charge from junction capacitance.
  • I can interpret the conditions behind a rectifier’s current and recovery ratings.
  • I can connect lead inductance and snubber faults to repeat failures.
