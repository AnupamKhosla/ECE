Op-amps: the golden rules

An op-amp is a cheap chip with enormous gain. On its own it is a comparator; wrapped in negative feedback it becomes a precise amplifier you can design with two simple rules. By the end you will scale a 0–10 V building signal for a 3.3 V microcontroller, read a 4–20 mA loop, and build a window alarm.

Reading 50–60 minLevel After T04, T12Practice P12, P14Next T16 (4–20 mA loops)

What an op-amp is

An operational amplifier is a differential amplifier with very high gain. It has two inputs, the non-inverting input (+) and the inverting input (−), one output, and two supply pins. It amplifies only the difference between its inputs:

Vout = AOL × (V+ − V−) AOL is the open-loop gain: typically 105 to 106 (100–120 dB) at DC. The output can never go beyond the supply rails. The inputs draw tiny currents: nanoamps for bipolar-input parts such as the LM358, picoamps for CMOS or JFET inputs.

Inside are a few dozen transistors: a differential input pair (two matched transistors sharing a current, from T12/T13), a high-gain middle stage and an output stage that can source and sink current. The name comes from 1940s analogue computers, where these amplifiers performed mathematical operations such as adding and integrating.

Open loop: a comparator

Without feedback, the gain is so large that the output is almost always pinned at one rail. If V+ > V− by more than a fraction of a millivolt, the output goes high; if less, it goes low. That is a comparator: a one-bit decision "is this voltage above that one?"

Example: over-temperature alarm. A thermistor divider gives 2.0 V at 25 °C and 2.6 V at 60 °C. Feed it to V+, and feed a 2.5 V reference to V−. Below about 55 °C the output sits low; above it, the output swings high and can drive a buzzer through a transistor (T12).

  • Chatter. Near the threshold, noise of a few millivolts makes the output flip back and forth. The fix is hysteresis: a resistor from the output back to the + input (positive feedback) so the threshold moves slightly once it trips. This is a Schmitt trigger.
  • Dedicated comparators (such as the LM393) are faster when coming out of saturation and usually have open-collector outputs: the output transistor only pulls down, and you add a pull-up resistor to whatever logic voltage you need. That makes them easy to interface to 3.3 V or 5 V logic, or to an alarm-panel input.

Negative feedback and the two golden rules

Now feed part of the output back to the inverting input. If the output rises too far, it raises V−, which shrinks (V+ − V−), which pulls the output back down. The output settles at the one value where everything is consistent. Let a fraction β of the output reach V−:

Vout = A(Vin − βVout) ⇒ Vout = Vin × A/(1 + Aβ) ≈ Vin/β With A = 105 and β = 0.1: gain = 105/(1 + 104) = 9.999. The result depends almost only on β, which is set by two resistors, not on the op-amp's messy, temperature-dependent A. The input difference needed is Vout/A: for a 5 V output, only 50 µV.

That tiny difference leads to the two rules that let you design almost any op-amp circuit in your head:

The golden rules (with negative feedback, output not at a rail)

Rule 1. The output does whatever it must to make the voltage difference between the inputs zero: V− = V+.

Rule 2. The inputs draw no current.

Rule 1 is a consequence of feedback, not a property of the chip. It only holds while the output is free to move: if the output is jammed against a rail, or there is no negative feedback, Rule 1 is false (that is exactly the comparator case). Rule 2 is a property of the chip and is always nearly true.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The two inputs are connected together inside (a 'virtual short'), so current flows between them." Right: no current flows between them at all (Rule 2). The output simply drives the feedback network until the inputs happen to sit at the same voltage. Think of a thermostat: it does not connect the room to the setpoint; it adjusts the heater until they match.

The three basic amplifiers

Voltage follower (buffer)

Connect the output straight to V− and the signal to V+. Rule 1: Vout = V− = V+ = Vin. Gain is exactly 1. Why bother? Rule 2: the input draws nothing, yet the output can supply milliamps. A follower lets a weak, high-resistance source (a sensor divider, a 100 kΩ potentiometer) drive a load (an ADC input, a long cable) without being dragged down.

Non-inverting amplifier: gain 1 + Rf/Rg

Signal into V+. The output feeds V− through a divider: Rf from output to V−, Rg from V− to ground.

Rule 2: the divider is unloaded, so V− = Vout × Rg/(Rf + Rg) Rule 1: V− = Vin ⇒ Vout = Vin × (1 + Rf/Rg) Rf = 9.1 kΩ, Rg = 1 kΩ: gain = 10.1. A 0.25 V sensor signal becomes 2.53 V. Input resistance is huge (the op-amp input), and the gain can never be less than 1.

Inverting amplifier: gain −Rf/Rin

V+ is grounded. The signal goes through Rin into V−, and Rf runs from V− to the output.

Rule 1: V− = V+ = 0 V (a "virtual ground") Current in Rin: I = Vin/Rin; Rule 2: all of it flows on through Rf Vout = 0 − I × Rf = −(Rf/Rin) × Vin Rin = 10 kΩ, Rf = 47 kΩ: gain −4.7. A +1 V input gives −4.7 V (needs a negative rail). The input resistance seen by the source is just Rin. Gains below 1 are possible: Rf = 3.3 kΩ, Rin = 10 kΩ gives −0.33.

The summing amplifier

Give the inverting amplifier several input resistors, all meeting at the virtual ground. Each input pushes its own current Vk/Rk into the node; because the node stays at 0 V, the inputs do not disturb each other. All the currents add and flow through Rf:

Vout = −Rf × ( V1/R1 + V2/R2 + V3/R3 + … ) All resistors 10 kΩ: Vout = −(V1 + V2 + V3). Example: 1.0 V + 0.5 V − 0.2 V gives −1.3 V. With R1 = 10 k, R2 = 20 k, R3 = 40 k and Rf = 10 k you get binary weights 1, ½, ¼: a tiny digital-to-analogue converter.

Audio mixers work this way, and the same "add an offset" trick is used to shift a signal: sum the signal with a fixed reference voltage, then invert again if needed.

Bench tip

Every op-amp needs a 100 nF decoupling capacitor from each supply pin to ground, right at the pins. Unused op-amps in a dual or quad package should be wired as followers with their + input tied to a mid-supply or ground voltage, so they do not oscillate and draw extra current.

Practical job 1: scaling a 0–10 V BMS signal for a 3.3 V microcontroller

Building controllers use 0–10 V analogue signals everywhere: valve and damper positions, VSD speed references, sensor outputs. A microcontroller ADC on a 3.3 V supply reads only 0–3.3 V, and more than about 3.6 V on its pin can damage it.

A non-inverting amplifier cannot have a gain below 1, and an inverting one flips the sign. The clean solution is a resistor divider followed by a rail-to-rail follower powered from 3.3 V.

  1. Divider. We want 10 V → just under 3.3 V, leaving headroom. R1 = 22 kΩ (top), R2 = 10 kΩ (bottom): 10 × 10/32 = 3.125 V at full scale. (20 k/10 k would give 3.33 V, slightly over the ADC's range.)
  2. Load on the BMS output. 10 V across 32 kΩ = 0.31 mA. Analogue outputs typically drive at least a few kΩ, so this is a light load.
  3. Why the follower. The divider's Thevenin resistance is 22k ∥ 10k ≈ 6.9 kΩ. An ADC input briefly draws current to charge its sampling capacitor, and 6.9 kΩ can make readings sag. The follower presents nearly infinite resistance to the divider and a low resistance to the ADC.
  4. Built-in protection. Powered from 3.3 V, a rail-to-rail op-amp's output physically cannot exceed about 3.3 V, even if someone wires 24 V to the input. In that fault the divider gives 7.5 V at the op-amp input; the op-amp's internal input clamp diodes conduct, and the 6.9 kΩ source resistance limits that current to about (7.5 − 3.6)/6.9k ≈ 0.6 mA. Check the datasheet's maximum input current, and add a series resistor if needed.
  5. Firmware. Vsignal = VADC × 3.2. At 12-bit (0–4095 over 3.3 V), one count = 0.806 mV at the ADC = 2.6 mV of the original signal.

Practical job 2: turning a 4–20 mA loop into a voltage

Industrial and building sensors often send their reading as a current from 4 mA (zero) to 20 mA (full scale). Current is the same at every point of a series loop, so cable resistance and long runs do not change the reading; and a broken wire gives 0 mA, which is clearly a fault, not a valid low reading. T16 goes through the physics.

To read it, put a precise resistor (a "shunt") in the loop and apply Ohm's law:

V = I × Rshunt 150 Ω: 4 mA → 0.60 V, 12 mA → 1.80 V, 20 mA → 3.00 V The classic choice is 250 Ω (giving 1–5 V) for 5 V systems. For a 3.3 V ADC, 150 Ω keeps full scale at 3.0 V with headroom. Use a 0.1 % resistor: its tolerance is your measurement error.
  • Buffer it with a rail-to-rail follower, as in job 1, so the ADC sees a low source resistance.
  • Firmware scaling. Percent = (V − 0.6)/(3.0 − 0.6) × 100. At 1.8 V: (1.2/2.4) × 100 = 50 %.
  • Fault detection. Below about 3.6 mA (0.54 V) or above about 21 mA (3.15 V) is commonly treated as a sensor or wiring fault (the NAMUR NE 43 recommendation). A reading near 0 V means an open loop.
  • Voltage budget. A 2-wire transmitter typically needs 10–12 V across itself to operate (check its datasheet). With a 24 V supply and 20 mA through 150 Ω (3 V), there is still about 9 V of margin for cable resistance.
  • Shunt power. (20 mA)² × 150 Ω = 60 mW. A 0.25 W resistor is fine.

To get the full 0–3.3 V range from 4–20 mA instead of 0.6–3.0 V, you can remove the 0.6 V offset with a difference amplifier (a subtractor built from an op-amp and four resistors). For most jobs the simple shunt plus software scaling is accurate enough and easier to troubleshoot.

Practical job 3: a window comparator for an alarm

Sometimes you need "alarm if the value is too low or too high", for example a 24 V supply that must stay between 22 V and 26 V, or a room temperature that must stay within a band. Use two comparators:

  • Divide the 24 V supply by 10 (for example 90 kΩ over 10 kΩ, or the nearest standard values): 24 V → 2.40 V; 22 V → 2.20 V; 26 V → 2.60 V.
  • Comparator A ("too low") gets the signal on its + input and the 2.20 V reference on its − input. Below 2.20 V, V+ < V−, so its output transistor turns on and pulls low.
  • Comparator B ("too high") gets the signal on its − input and the 2.60 V reference on its + input. Above 2.60 V, V+ < V−, so it pulls low.
  • Make the references with a divider from a stable reference (for example a 2.5 V shunt reference such as the TL431, or a 5 V regulated rail), not from the supply you are measuring.
  • With an LM393 (open-collector outputs), tie the two outputs together with one pull-up resistor. The output is high only when both comparators are happy, and either one pulling low signals an alarm. A low = fault output also leans towards fail-safe: if the pull-up shares the comparator's supply and the receiving input has a pull-down, a lost supply or a broken signal wire reads as an alarm instead of silently looking normal.
  • Add a little hysteresis to each comparator so a supply sitting right at 22.0 V does not make the alarm flicker.
On the job

BMS technicians constantly deal with 0–10 V and 4–20 mA points, and with "the reading on the front end doesn't match the sensor". Measure the actual signal at the controller terminals: volts for 0–10 V points, and for 4–20 mA either the voltage across a known shunt or the loop current with the meter in series (or a clamp meter made for milliamp loops). If the field value is right and the front end is wrong, the problem is scaling or configuration, not wiring.

In Australia

Control wiring at 24 V AC/DC and signal levels of 0–10 V and 4–20 mA is extra-low voltage. In practice the controllers sit in panels that also contain mains supplies, and any work on the mains parts must be done by a licensed electrician. Follow the site's isolation procedures and confirm with your state electrical regulator what an unlicensed technician may do in a mixed panel.

Safety

Build all of these circuits on the bench from plug-pack or bench supplies at 24 V DC or less. Never connect your test circuit to a live building panel until you are trained, authorised by the site, and working only on its ELV terminals.

Check yourself

An op-amp with AOL = 200,000 runs open-loop on ±12 V. V+ = 1.0001 V and V− = 1.0000 V. What is the output?

Ideal: 200,000 × 0.1 mV = 20 V, which is beyond the rail, so the output sits at its positive limit (about +10.5 V for a classic part). Open loop it is a comparator.

Why is Rule 1 (V− = V+) not a property of the chip?

It comes from negative feedback plus huge gain: the output moves until the inputs differ by only Vout/A, microvolts. Without feedback, or with the output stuck at a rail, the inputs can differ by volts.

Design a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of 11 using a 10 kΩ Rg.

1 + Rf/Rg = 11, so Rf = 100 kΩ.

An inverting amplifier has Rin = 4.7 kΩ and Rf = 47 kΩ. Input +0.5 V, ±15 V supply. Output? Current from the source?

Gain −10, output −5 V. The source sees Rin to a virtual ground: 0.5/4.7k ≈ 106 µA.

An LM358 (GBW about 1 MHz) is set to a gain of 101. What bandwidth do you expect?

About 1 MHz/101 ≈ 10 kHz. Fine for a temperature sensor, not for audio-grade work at that gain.

Why can't you scale 0–10 V down to 0–3.3 V with a single non-inverting amplifier?

Its gain is 1 + Rf/Rg, never less than 1. Use a divider (gain 0.3125 with 22 k/10 k) then a follower. An inverting stage could attenuate but flips the sign, which a single-supply ADC cannot read.

A 4–20 mA loop uses a 150 Ω shunt. The ADC reads 1.32 V. What percentage is the sensor reporting?

I = 1.32/150 = 8.8 mA. Percent = (8.8 − 4)/16 × 100 = 30 %. Equivalently (1.32 − 0.6)/2.4 = 0.30.

In the window comparator, why is "low = alarm" with open-collector outputs a good choice?

Either comparator can pull the shared line low on its own, so one pull-up and one wire report both faults. And with the pull-up on the comparator's supply and a pull-down at the receiving input, a lost supply or a cut signal wire also reads low, so common failures show up as an alarm instead of looking normal. With "high = alarm" those same failures would hide.
