Theory · Chapter T01

Charge, current and voltage

What actually moves inside a wire, how slowly it moves, why the lamp still lights the instant you close the switch, and what a volt really measures. By the end you will be able to picture a working circuit correctly, not just name its parts.

Reading 45 minLevel FoundationMaths Class 12 algebra, a little calculusPractice P02, P03, P10

How we found out

People knew about static electricity for more than two thousand years before anyone knew what an electron was. The Greek word for amber is elektron, because rubbed amber picks up straw and feathers. Around 1600 the English doctor William Gilbert separated this "electric" attraction from magnetism and gave it its name.

In the 1740s Benjamin Franklin in America decided that electricity was one kind of "fluid". A body with too much of it he called positive (+), and a body with too little he called negative (−). He had to guess which was which. He guessed wrong, as we found out 150 years later. That one guess is the reason for the "conventional current" you will meet below.

In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb used a very sensitive twisting balance to show that the force between two charges falls with the square of the distance, just like gravity. In 1800 Alessandro Volta stacked zinc and copper discs with salt-water cardboard between them. This "voltaic pile" was the first battery: the first source of a steady flow of electricity instead of a single spark. Within a few years, chemists used it to split water and discover new metals. In the 1820s André-Marie Ampère and Georg Ohm worked out how current behaves in wires (Ohm is the subject of T02).

Nobody knew what was flowing until 1897, when J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays are made of tiny negative particles, far lighter than any atom. We now call them electrons. Between 1909 and 1913 Robert Millikan timed tiny charged oil drops falling between two metal plates. Every drop carried a whole-number multiple of one smallest charge. That was the charge of a single electron. So Franklin's "fluid" turned out to be a crowd of countless negative particles, and in metal wires it is the negative particles that move. Franklin's sign choice was already in every textbook, so we kept it.

Charge: the thing that is counted

Electric charge is a property of some particles, like mass is. It comes in two kinds, which we call positive and negative. Like charges push each other away, and unlike charges pull together. A proton carries +e and an electron carries −e, where

e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C C is the coulomb, the SI unit of charge. One coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons (that is 1 / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹).

Two facts about charge matter for everything that follows.

  1. Charge is conserved. You cannot create or destroy net charge. You can only move it from one place to another. A battery does not "make" electrons, and a lamp does not "use them up".
  2. Ordinary matter is almost exactly neutral. A copper atom has 29 protons and 29 electrons. The electric force is so strong that even a tiny imbalance pushes back hard. Coulomb's law says F = k q₁q₂ / r², with k ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². Two charges of just 1 C each, 1 m apart, would push on each other with 9 × 10⁹ N. That is the weight of about a million tonnes. Real circuits move a lot of charge, but they keep the wires very close to neutral the whole time.

In a metal, each atom lets go of one or more of its outer electrons. These conduction electrons are not tied to any one atom. They wander through the whole crystal, while the positive copper ions stay fixed in a regular grid. Copper gives about one free electron per atom. That is about 8.5 × 10²⁸ free electrons in every cubic metre. Keep that number; we use it in a moment.

Common misconception

"A battery stores charge, and a flat battery has run out of electrons." A battery is neutral when full and neutral when flat. It stores chemical energy. For every electron that leaves its − terminal, one electron enters its + terminal. What runs out is the chemical reaction that pushes them round, not the electrons. The same is true of a "charged" phone: charging it moves charge around inside the cell and stores energy. It does not add electrons.

Current: charge per second

Electric current is the rate at which charge passes a point. Picture standing beside a wire and counting the charge that crosses an imaginary slice through it every second.

I = Q / t (for a steady current)
I = dQ/dt (in general) I is current in amperes (A). Q is charge in coulombs. t is time in seconds. dQ/dt just means "the charge passing in a very short time, divided by that short time". It lets us talk about a current that changes from moment to moment. 1 A = 1 C/s.

So 1 A means about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons pass a point each second. Some everyday currents:

If a current flows for a known time, charge = current × time. A phone battery rated 3000 mAh (milliamp-hours) can deliver 3 A for 1 hour, which is 3 × 3600 = 10,800 C of charge moved round the circuit. This is a measure of how much charge it can push through, not how much it "contains".

Conventional current and electron flow

Because of Franklin's guess, we draw current as if positive charge flows out of the + terminal, round the circuit, and back into the − terminal. This is conventional current. In a metal wire the real moving charges are electrons, and they go the opposite way: out of the − terminal and into the +. This is electron flow.

Both descriptions give the same answers for every circuit calculation. A positive charge moving right and a negative charge moving left both carry charge to the right in the same way. So in this course, as in every datasheet and schematic, "current" means conventional current unless we say otherwise. The arrow in a diode symbol points the way conventional current can flow.

Common misconception

"Current gets used up in the lamp, so less comes back to the battery." Measure it: put a meter in series before the lamp and after it, and the two readings are the same. Charge is conserved and it cannot pile up in a wire, so every coulomb that goes in comes out. What the lamp "uses" is energy, which the charges carry in and lose as they pass through. Current is the flow; energy is what the flow delivers.

How fast do the electrons move?

Most people guess "close to the speed of light". The real answer is surprising, and it changes how you picture a circuit.

First, the free electrons in copper are already moving very fast, even with no current. Because of quantum effects, they race around at about 1.6 × 10⁶ m/s (1,600 km per second) in random directions. Random motion in all directions carries no net charge anywhere, so it is not a current. When you connect a battery, an electric field adds a tiny steady push. On top of the fast random motion, the whole crowd slowly creeps in one direction. That slow average motion is the drift velocity, vd.

Deriving I = n A vd e

Take a wire with cross-section area A, containing n free electrons per cubic metre, all drifting at speed vd. In a time t, each electron moves a distance vdt. So every electron in a slab of length vdt, just behind a chosen face, crosses that face in time t.

The slab's volume is A × vdt. The number of electrons in it is n A vdt. Each carries charge e, so the charge crossing in time t is Q = n A vd t e. Divide by t:

I = n A vd e so vd = I / (n A e) n: free electrons per m³ (8.5 × 10²⁸ for copper). A: cross-section in m² (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²). e: 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Worked example: 1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire

n A e = 8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.36 × 10⁴ C per metre of wire. That is the free charge in each metre: about 13,600 coulombs, a huge amount.

vd = 1 / 13,600 ≈ 7.4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s = 0.074 mm/s.

That is slower than a snail. At this speed, an electron needs about 13,600 seconds, nearly 4 hours, to drift 1 metre. In a 2 m lamp cord it never gets from the switch to the lamp in an evening. The current is large not because the electrons are fast, but because there are so many of them moving together.

With alternating current (AC) at 50 Hz the picture is even stranger. The direction reverses 100 times a second, so each electron only jiggles back and forth. Its whole movement is less than a micrometre. Electrons from the power station never reach your house. Yet energy clearly does. The next section explains how.

Why the lamp lights instantly

If electrons crawl at a fraction of a millimetre per second, why does a lamp 10 m away light the moment you flip the switch? Because the lamp does not need electrons from the switch. The wire is already full of free electrons, including the ones inside the lamp filament. What has to travel is the push: the electric field that tells every electron in the circuit to start drifting.

When you close the switch, a few electrons shift very slightly near the switch. This changes the charge on the surface of the wires next to it, which changes the field a bit further along, and so on. This rearrangement of surface charge runs along the wire as an electromagnetic wave. In a typical cable it moves at about 60–90% of the speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s). Within a few nanoseconds, a thin layer of surface charge has formed along the whole circuit. Wires connected to the + side of the battery carry a slight positive surface charge, and wires connected to the − side carry a slight negative one. This surface charge shapes the field so that inside every part of the wire, even round bends, the field points along the wire. Then all the electrons everywhere start drifting at once.

This also answers "where is the energy?". It is not carried as the electrons' kinetic energy. An electron drifting at 0.074 mm/s has kinetic energy ½mv² ≈ ½ × 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ × (7.4 × 10⁻⁵)² ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻³⁹ J. Compare that with the energy each electron delivers in a 12 V lamp: 12 V × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. That is about 10²¹ times more. The energy flows from the battery to the lamp through the electric and magnetic fields in and around the wires, at the speed of the wave. The electrons are the "handle" the field uses to do work inside the lamp filament. In the 1960s Oleg Jefimenko made these fields around working circuits visible, using grass seeds in oil around circuits painted with conductive ink.

On the job

The speed of the signal in a cable matters every day in telecom work. A cable tester sends a pulse down a pair and times the echo from the far end, then uses the cable's nominal velocity of propagation (NVP), usually about 0.6–0.75 of light speed for twisted pair, to work out the length. If the NVP setting is wrong, the reported length is wrong. Fibre testers (OTDRs, see C09) do the same thing with light.

Voltage: energy per charge

Voltage (or potential difference) between two points is the energy that each coulomb of charge gains or loses in moving between them.

V = W / Q 1 V = 1 J/C W is energy (work) in joules. Q is the charge moved, in coulombs. A 9 V battery gives 9 J of energy to every coulomb it pushes round the circuit. When that coulomb comes back, it has delivered all 9 J to the parts of the circuit.

A tiny unit of energy follows from this. One electronvolt (eV) is the energy one electron gains in moving through 1 V: 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Chemists and semiconductor physicists use it all the time. The silicon band gap of about 1.12 eV, which you meet in the p–n junction deep dive, is in these units.

Potential: voltage at a point

It helps to give every point in a circuit a potential: its "electrical height". Only differences in potential have meaning, so we choose one point as 0 V and measure everything from there. In electronics that point is called ground, common or 0 V. When someone says "this pin is at 3.3 V", they mean 3.3 V above the chosen 0 V. A multimeter always measures a difference between its two probes, so you must always know where the black probe is.

Think of a closed circuit as a loop walk on a hillside. The battery is a lift that raises each coulomb up by its voltage. The resistors are slopes where the coulomb loses height (energy) as heat. Wires are nearly flat paths. After a full loop you are back at the same height. (This "you always come back to the same height" rule is Kirchhoff's voltage law, T04.)

Voltage and the electric field in a wire

The electric field E is how fast potential changes with distance, pointing "downhill": E = −dV/dx. For a uniform wire or resistor of length L with voltage V across it, the field inside is simply E = V / L (in volts per metre, which is the same as newtons per coulomb).

Numbers make this real. A 1 mm² copper wire carrying 1 A has about 0.017 Ω per metre (you will calculate this in T02). So the voltage across 1 m of it is about 17 mV, and the field inside is only 0.017 V/m. Inside a small resistor with 5 V across a resistive track a few millimetres long, the field is hundreds or thousands of volts per metre. This is the right picture: the wires need only a tiny field to keep current flowing, and nearly all of the "voltage drop" happens across the parts that resist.

Common misconception

"Voltage flows through the circuit." Voltage is always between two points, like a height difference. Current flows through things. So the correct phrases are "the voltage across the resistor" and "the current through the resistor". You measure voltage with the meter's probes on both sides of a part (in parallel), and current by breaking the circuit and putting the meter in the path (in series). P03 practises both.

Common misconception

"A bird on a power line survives because it is insulated." Its feet are on the same wire, a few centimetres apart. The potential difference between them is tiny (like the 17 mV per metre above, scaled up for the line current), so almost no current goes through the bird. The danger comes from touching two points at very different potentials, such as a live conductor and earth. This is theory only: mains and overhead lines are never something you touch or work on without a licence.

Batteries: chemical charge pumps

Outside the battery, conventional current flows "downhill" from + to −. Inside the battery, the same current must go "uphill", from − back to +, against the electric field. Something non-electrical must push it. In a battery that push is a chemical reaction.

In an alkaline AA cell, zinc at the − terminal reacts and gives up electrons to the external wire. Manganese dioxide at the + terminal takes electrons from the wire. Inside, ions (charged atoms) move through the electrolyte paste to complete the loop. The reaction only runs when there is an outside path for the electrons, which is why a battery can sit on a shelf for years. In a lithium-ion cell, lithium ions shuttle between a graphite electrode and a metal-oxide electrode. In a lead-acid battery, lead and lead dioxide plates react with sulfuric acid.

The energy the chemistry gives to each coulomb is the electromotive force, written ℰ (it is a voltage, not a force; the name is historical). Typical values per cell: alkaline 1.5–1.6 V, NiMH 1.2–1.4 V, lead-acid about 2.1 V when fully charged (six cells make a 12.6 V "12 V" battery), lithium-ion 3.6–3.7 V nominal and 4.2 V full.

EMF, terminal voltage and internal resistance

A real battery is not a perfect pump. The ions inside must push through electrolyte and the plates, and that path has resistance. We model a real battery as a perfect EMF ℰ in series with a small internal resistance r.

Vterminal = ℰ − I r With no current (open circuit) the meter reads ℰ. With current I flowing, the voltage I r is lost inside the battery as heat, and the terminals show less.

Worked examples

  • Fresh AA alkaline, ℰ = 1.55 V, r ≈ 0.2 Ω. Drawing 1 A: V = 1.55 − 1 × 0.2 = 1.35 V. Short-circuit current ≈ 1.55 / 0.2 ≈ 8 A (and the cell gets hot).
  • 9 V PP3 alkaline, r ≈ 1.5 Ω. A small motor drawing 0.5 A: V = 9 − 0.75 = 8.25 V. At 2 A it would collapse to 6 V. This is why a 9 V battery is a poor choice for motors.
  • 12 V sealed lead-acid (SLA) alarm battery, ℰ = 12.8 V, r ≈ 0.03 Ω. A short with a spanner: I ≈ 12.8 / 0.03 ≈ 400 A. That is enough to weld the spanner and spray molten metal.
  • Measuring r yourself: a battery reads 12.6 V open circuit and 11.9 V while supplying 7 A. Then r = (12.6 − 11.9) / 7 = 0.1 Ω.

Internal resistance rises as a battery ages, gets cold, or runs flat. An old battery can show a healthy voltage on a meter (almost no current) and then collapse the moment a real load is connected. These figures are indicative; real values depend on the cell's size, chemistry, age and temperature.

Safety

Extra-low voltage (ELV) does not mean low danger. A 12 V battery cannot push dangerous current through dry skin, but it can deliver hundreds of amps through a metal tool, ring or watch strap. Cover terminals, take off rings, and always fit a fuse close to the battery. Lithium cells that are shorted, crushed or overcharged can catch fire. Everything in this course stays at ELV; mains (230 V) work is legally limited to licensed electricians in Australia.

On the job

Alarm panels, access control power supplies, fire panels and many BMS controllers keep a 12 V or 24 V SLA battery on standby. Technicians test those batteries under load, not just with a meter, because a failing battery can read 12.8 V open circuit and still drop badly when the mains fails and the system needs it. The physics is exactly V = ℰ − I r with a large r.

Try this

Measure an AA cell's open-circuit voltage. Then connect a 10 Ω, 1 W resistor across it and measure again quickly. Current ≈ Vloaded / 10. Internal resistance r = (Vopen − Vloaded) / I. Compare a new cell and a nearly flat one. The flat one may read almost the same voltage open circuit but have several times the r.

The water analogy, and where it breaks

The common analogy compares a circuit to water in closed pipes. Used carefully, it is a useful first picture.

Electric circuitWater in closed pipesDoes it hold?
Charge (C)Amount of water (litres)Yes: both are conserved, and the pipe is always full
Current (A = C/s)Flow rate (L/s)Yes: same flow everywhere in a single loop
Voltage (V = J/C)Pressure differenceMostly: both are "energy per amount" between two points
BatteryPumpYes: both raise energy, neither creates the fluid
ResistorNarrow pipe or a pipe full of sandYes: flow ∝ pressure difference (for steady, slow flow)
Switch openValve closedYes: no flow, full pressure across the valve

Now the places it breaks. Each one is a real mistake people make.

  1. Cut the pipe and water pours out. Cut a wire and electrons stay put. An open circuit is not a leak. Charges cannot leave the metal at normal voltages, so current simply stops, and the full voltage appears across the gap. (This is why a blown fuse has the whole supply voltage across it.)
  2. Speed. In a pipe, the pressure change travels at the speed of sound in water, about 1,500 m/s. In a wire, the push travels at a large fraction of the speed of light. The water picture makes the delay far too long.
  3. Where the energy goes. In a pipe, the energy really is carried inside the water. In a circuit, it mostly travels through the fields around the wires (Figure 3). This matters for cable design, interference, and later for antennas (T10).
  4. What does the pushing. Water is pushed by the water behind it. Electrons are pushed by the field, which is shaped by surface charges along the whole wire. That is why current can follow a sharp bend with no "pile-up".
  5. Magnetism. Water has nothing like the magnetic field around a current, which gives us inductors, motors, transformers and relays (T06). Water's momentum is a rough copy of inductance, but only rough.
  6. Insulation is not a pipe wall. At high enough voltage, "insulators" break down and current jumps through air or plastic. Water pipes do not suddenly let water through their walls at a certain pressure in the same way.

Use the water picture to remember "current in a loop is the same everywhere" and "voltage is a difference between two points". Drop it as soon as you think about speed, energy flow, AC, or magnetism.

Go deeper (optional): why the drift is steady, not accelerating

A constant field puts a constant force eE on each electron, so why don't they speed up forever? Because they keep scattering off vibrating ions and impurities, roughly every 2 × 10⁻¹⁴ s in copper at room temperature. After each scattering event the electron's extra velocity is randomised and it starts again. The average extra velocity is then vd = eEτ/m, where τ is the average time between scatterings. This gives a current proportional to the field, which is Ohm's law. T02 builds this "collision" (Drude) model properly and shows where it is right and where quantum physics corrects it.

Check yourself

A wire carries 2 A. How many electrons pass a point each second?

2 C/s ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C per electron = 1.25 × 10¹⁹ electrons per second. The number is huge because each electron carries so little charge.

You keep the current at 1 A but change from a 1 mm² wire to a 2.5 mm² wire. What happens to the drift velocity?

vd = I/(nAe), so with 2.5 times the area the drift velocity falls to 1/2.5 of its value: from about 0.074 mm/s to about 0.03 mm/s. The same charge per second is shared across more electrons, so each one moves more slowly.

If electrons drift at less than a millimetre per second, why does a lamp 20 m away light within microseconds?

The wire and the lamp are already full of free electrons. What travels is the change in the electric field, carried by a rearrangement of surface charge that moves at a large fraction of the speed of light. Once the field is in place, the electrons already inside the lamp start drifting immediately. The energy also arrives through the fields, not on the electrons.

A battery reads 12.6 V with nothing connected and 11.9 V while supplying 7 A. What is its internal resistance, and what would it read at 14 A?

r = (12.6 − 11.9) / 7 = 0.1 Ω. At 14 A the loss inside is 14 × 0.1 = 1.4 V, so V = 12.6 − 1.4 = 11.2 V (assuming r stays the same).

True or false: "A battery supplies the electrons that flow in the circuit."

False. The electrons were already in the wires and parts. The battery supplies energy: it pushes charge from its − side back up to its + side inside, using chemical energy. It stays neutral the whole time. Every electron that leaves the − terminal is matched by one entering the + terminal.

An electron is accelerated through 5 V in a vacuum. How much energy does it gain, in eV and in joules?

5 eV, by the definition of the electronvolt. In joules: 5 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Energy = charge × voltage.

Your meter's black probe is on the 0 V rail. The red probe reads 5 V at point A and 3.3 V at point B. What is the voltage between A and B?

VAB = 5 − 3.3 = 1.7 V. Each reading is a potential measured from 0 V; the voltage between two points is the difference between their potentials.

Why is "the current gets used up by the lamp" wrong, and what does get used?

Charge is conserved and cannot build up in a steady circuit, so the current going into the lamp equals the current coming out. What the lamp takes is energy: each coulomb enters the lamp at a higher potential and leaves at a lower one, and the difference (in joules per coulomb) becomes heat and light.
