How a MOSFET works

A voltage on an insulated gate pulls a thin sheet of electrons to the silicon surface and joins two terminals together. You will see why that needs a threshold voltage, why "logic-level" matters for 3.3 V and 5 V drivers, what gate charge costs you, and why MOSFETs run nearly every power switch and every computer chip.

The field-effect idea

A BJT (T12) is controlled by injecting carriers through a forward-biased junction, so its input always draws some current. A field-effect transistor is controlled by an electric field through an insulator. No charge crosses the insulator, so in steady state the control terminal draws essentially zero current. It only needs charge to move on and off, like charging a capacitor.

The idea is old: Julius Lilienfeld patented field-effect devices in the 1920s, but nobody could make a clean enough surface for them to work. In 1959 Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs built the first working MOSFET (metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor), using thermally grown silicon dioxide as a near-perfect insulator on silicon. In 1963 Frank Wanlass at Fairchild showed CMOS, which pairs n- and p-channel MOSFETs so that logic draws almost no power when idle. Today MOSFETs are the most-manufactured device in history.

The MOS capacitor

Start with the simplest structure: a metal (or doped polysilicon) gate, a thin oxide (SiO2, an excellent insulator), on a p-type substrate (also called the body). It is a parallel-plate capacitor whose bottom "plate" is silicon. The capacitance per area is:

Cox = εox / tox εox = 3.9 ε0 For a 50 nm oxide (a typical power-MOSFET gate): Cox = 3.9 × 8.85×10−12 / 50×10−9 ≈ 6.9×10−4 F/m², or 0.69 fF per µm². Logic transistors use oxides only a few nanometres thick, so Cox is ten or more times larger.

Now raise the gate voltage VG (body grounded) and watch the silicon surface go through three stages.

  1. Accumulation (VG negative): the gate attracts holes, piling them against the oxide.
  2. Depletion (small positive VG): the gate repels holes, uncovering negative acceptor ions. Their charge balances the gate's positive charge. Same physics as the p side of a p–n junction.
  3. Inversion (VG above the threshold voltage Vth): the surface potential is now high enough that electrons collect at the surface. Extra gate charge from now on is balanced mostly by these mobile electrons, not by more ions. This electron sheet is the inversion channel.

Why a threshold, and how big? Recall from T11 that n·p = ni² and that each 60 mV of potential changes a carrier density 10×. In a body doped NA = 1017 cm−3, electrons are only ni²/NA = 103 per cm³. To make the surface as n-type as the bulk is p-type (1017 electrons/cm³), the surface potential must rise by 14 decades × 59.5 mV ≈ 0.83 V. That is the textbook "strong inversion" condition. The gate voltage needed is larger than this, because some voltage also drops across the oxide (to hold the depletion charge) and because of built-in contact and oxide-charge effects. Manufacturers tune Vth with doping; practical parts range from about 0.4 V (logic) to 2–4 V (standard power MOSFETs).

How much charge is in the channel? Above threshold, extra charge = Cox(VGS − Vth). With our 50 nm oxide and 1 V of overdrive: 6.9×10−4 C/m², which is 4.3×1011 electrons per cm². Squeezed into a layer a few nanometres thick, that is a local density around 1018 cm−3: a genuinely conducting sheet.

Where do the electrons come from?

In a bare MOS capacitor, the only supply is slow thermal generation, so the channel takes milliseconds to seconds to form. In a MOSFET the channel connects to heavily doped n+ source and drain regions, which are full of electrons and supply the channel in nanoseconds. This is why a MOSFET switches fast and a plain MOS capacitor does not.

The enhancement n-channel MOSFET

Add two heavily doped n+ regions on either side of the gate: the source (S) and the drain (D). The p-type body sits between them under the gate.

  • VGS below Vth: from source to drain the path is n+ → p → n+, two back-to-back p–n junctions with a wide p region between them. As you saw in T12, that is not a transistor: no current flows (apart from leakage). The switch is off.
  • VGS above Vth: the inversion channel forms an n-type bridge from source to drain. Electrons flow from source to drain (conventional current from drain to source). The switch is on.

This is called an enhancement-mode device: the channel does not exist until you "enhance" it with gate voltage, so it is normally off. (Depletion-mode MOSFETs, which are on at VGS = 0, exist but are rare in your work.) A p-channel MOSFET is the mirror image: n body, p+ source and drain, a hole channel, turned on by pulling the gate below the source.

Linear and saturation regions

Define the overdrive Vov = VGS − Vth: how far above threshold the gate is. The simple "square-law" model gives:

Linear (VDS < Vov): ID = k [ VovVDS − VDS²/2 ] Saturation (VDS ≥ Vov): ID = (k/2) Vov² k = μnCoxW/L: mobility × oxide capacitance × channel width ÷ channel length. Wide, short channels give big k. Power MOSFETs are thousands of tiny cells in parallel, so their k is several A/V² or more.
  • Linear (also "ohmic" or "triode") region. For small VDS, the channel is uniform and acts like a resistor whose value the gate sets: RDS ≈ 1/(k Vov). More gate voltage, more electrons, lower resistance. A MOSFET used as a switch should sit here, fully on, with a tiny VDS.
  • Saturation region. Once VDS ≥ Vov, the channel is pinched off at the drain end and current hardly increases with VDS. The MOSFET is now a voltage-controlled current source: this is where it amplifies.
Common misconception

The names are a trap. A MOSFET in "saturation" is in its flat, current-source region, which corresponds to a BJT's active region. A BJT in "saturation" is fully on, which corresponds to a MOSFET's linear/ohmic region. When someone says "saturate the MOSFET" for a switch, they usually mean "drive it hard into the ohmic region". Ask which they mean.

Worked example. A power MOSFET with Vth = 2 V and k = 4 A/V².

  • VGS = 10 V: Vov = 8 V, RDS ≈ 1/(4 × 8) ≈ 31 mΩ. Saturation current limit (k/2)Vov² = 128 A, far above any load you would connect. Fully on.
  • VGS = 3.3 V: Vov = 1.3 V, RDS ≈ 190 mΩ, and the current cannot exceed 2 × 1.3² ≈ 3.4 A. A 5 A load would push it out of the ohmic region: it would drop several volts and overheat.

The square law is a simplification. Real short-channel and power devices deviate from it, and below threshold a small "subthreshold" current still flows, falling about 10× for every 60–100 mV below Vth. For design, always use the datasheet curves.

RDS(on) and why logic-level MOSFETs matter

Datasheets give RDS(on), the on-resistance, at a stated VGS, usually 10 V for standard parts. They also give VGS(th), but read its definition: it is the gate voltage where the drain current just reaches a tiny test value, often 250 µA. At VGS(th) the MOSFET is barely on, not fully on.

A classic example: the IRF540N, a popular 100 V power MOSFET, has VGS(th) of 2–4 V and RDS(on) of 44 mΩ specified at VGS = 10 V. Driven from a 3.3 V microcontroller pin, a sample with a 3 V threshold has almost no overdrive and passes only a small current with a large voltage drop. People wire it up, the load barely runs and the MOSFET gets hot.

A logic-level MOSFET has a lower threshold and, crucially, an RDS(on) specified at 4.5 V, 2.5 V or even 1.8 V. Rule: only trust a MOSFET at a gate voltage where the datasheet gives an RDS(on) figure.

  • 5 V Arduino: choose a part with RDS(on) specified at 4.5 V (often marked "logic level", e.g. the IRLZ44N family).
  • 3.3 V ESP32 or Raspberry Pi: choose a part with RDS(on) specified at 2.5 V or 2.7 V (many small SOT-23 MOSFETs such as the AO3400 type are; check the datasheet), or use a gate driver.
  • Otherwise: add a gate driver or a small transistor stage that switches the gate to 10–12 V from your supply rail.

Gate charge and gate resistors

The gate is a capacitor, and a big one: a power MOSFET's input capacitance is often 1–5 nF. To switch it you must deliver a total gate charge Qg (datasheets list it, typically 10–100 nC). The switching time is roughly:

t ≈ Qg / Igate Example: Qg = 40 nC driven from a 5 V pin through a 220 Ω resistor (about 10–20 mA, average ~10 mA): t ≈ 40 nC / 10 mA = 4 µs. A dedicated gate-driver chip delivering 1–2 A does it in tens of nanoseconds.

Why does switching time matter? During the transition the MOSFET has both voltage across it and current through it, so it heats. Energy lost per transition ≈ ½ × V × I × t.

  • 12 V, 5 A load, t = 4 µs: ½ × 12 × 5 × 4×10−6 = 120 µJ per edge.
  • PWM at 1 kHz (2 edges per cycle): 0.24 W. Acceptable.
  • PWM at 20 kHz: 4.8 W. The MOSFET cooks, even though RDS(on) is tiny. Fast PWM needs a proper gate driver.

Partway through each transition the gate voltage pauses on a flat step, the Miller plateau. There, the drain voltage is swinging and the gate–drain capacitance soaks up almost all the gate current. Most of Qg and most of the switching loss happen on this plateau.

The two gate resistors you will see on almost every board:

  • Series gate resistor (10–220 Ω): limits the peak current a microcontroller pin must deliver into the gate capacitance, and damps ringing between the gate capacitance and the wiring inductance (which otherwise can oscillate at tens of MHz).
  • Gate pull-down (10–100 kΩ, gate to source): while the microcontroller boots, its pins float. A floating gate can be charged by stray coupling and leave the MOSFET half-on: high resistance, high current, smoke. The pull-down holds it firmly off.

The body diode

In a discrete power MOSFET the body is connected to the source inside the package. That leaves a p–n junction between the p body (now at the source) and the n drain: the body diode, anode at the source, cathode at the drain for an n-channel part. It is drawn in the symbol.

  • An n-channel MOSFET blocks only one direction: drain positive with respect to source. Reverse the voltage and the body diode conducts whatever the gate does.
  • In motor H-bridges the body diodes give the motor's inductive current somewhere to go during dead time.
  • To block both directions (an AC switch, or the charge/discharge switch in a lithium battery pack's protection board), use two MOSFETs back to back, sources joined.

Why MOSFETs dominate power switching

  • No steady gate current. Gate leakage is nanoamps. A BJT switching 10 A needs perhaps 0.5–1 A of base current, continuously.
  • Very low on-resistance. Milliohms in modern parts, so the on-state loss is tiny.
  • Fast. Only majority carriers flow in the channel, so there is no stored minority charge to remove at turn-off (unlike a saturated BJT). Switching speed is set by how fast you can move the gate charge.
  • Easy to parallel. RDS(on) rises with temperature (mobility falls as the lattice vibrates more). A hotter MOSFET takes less current, so parallel devices share fairly. A BJT does the opposite.
P = I² × RDS(on) 10 A through 10 mΩ: P = 100 × 0.01 = 1 W. At 5 A: 0.25 W. But RDS(on) often rises 1.5–2× at 100–125 °C, so design with the hot figure: 10 A × 10 A × 18 mΩ ≈ 1.8 W, which needs a heatsink or good copper area. Compare a BJT at 10 A with VCE(sat) = 0.3 V: 3 W in the collector, plus its base drive.

The CMOS inverter

Put a p-channel MOSFET on top (source to VDD) and an n-channel MOSFET below (source to ground). Join their gates as the input and their drains as the output. This is the CMOS inverter, the basic cell of every logic chip, microcontroller and CPU.

Why CMOS won. A gate that is idle draws essentially nothing. Power goes only into charging and discharging the wiring and gate capacitance each time a signal switches:

P ≈ Ceff × VDD² × f Example: 100 pF of effective switched capacitance at 3.3 V and 48 MHz: 10−10 × 10.9 × 4.8×107 ≈ 52 mW. Dropping the supply from 5 V to 3.3 V cuts this by (3.3/5)² ≈ 0.44, a 56 % saving. That is why chips moved from 5 V to 3.3 V and why processor cores run near 1 V.

It is also why a CMOS input must never be left floating: an unconnected gate drifts to around half supply, turning both transistors partly on, which wastes current and makes the output chatter. Tie unused inputs high or low (P11 covers pull-ups).

ESD: the thin oxide is fragile

The gate oxide is a superb insulator but very thin. Silicon dioxide breaks down at roughly 10 MV/cm, about 1 V per nanometre. A 50 nm power-MOSFET oxide fails somewhere around 50 V; that is why datasheets rate VGS at only ±20 V or so. A logic transistor's oxide of a couple of nanometres fails at a few volts. Once punctured, the damage is permanent: the gate leaks or shorts.

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) from your body easily reaches several kilovolts on a dry day. Worked: model your body as 100 pF charged to 3 kV (0.3 µC). Touch the gate of a MOSFET with 1 nF input capacitance and the charge shares: V ≈ 3000 × 100/(100 + 1000) ≈ 270 V on the gate, more than ten times the rating. You may not even feel a spark below about 3 kV.

  • Integrated CMOS chips have built-in protection diodes on their pins, which helps but does not make them immune.
  • Many discrete MOSFETs have no protection at all (some include a gate Zener).
  • Store parts in their antistatic bags or foam, use an earthed wrist strap and mat when you can, touch an earthed metal point before handling, and fit the gate pull-down resistor early in assembly.
Safety

An ESD wrist strap must connect through its built-in high-value resistor (typically 1 MΩ) to a proper earth point provided for it. Never clip it to anything connected to mains. All the circuits in this course stay at extra-low voltage (5–24 V DC); mains switching with MOSFETs belongs to licensed electricians and certified products.

On the job

MOSFETs are the switches inside PoE switches, LED drivers, electric lock controllers, 12/24 V power supplies, solar charge controllers and battery packs. A field technician rarely replaces one, but recognising the symptoms helps: a "failed short" MOSFET leaves a lock or load permanently energised; a damaged gate leaves it half-on and hot. In battery systems, "BMS" means battery management system, a protection board built around back-to-back MOSFETs, not the building management system of C01.

Check yourself

Why does a MOSFET's gate draw no steady current, while a BJT's base does?

The gate is insulated by oxide, so it is a capacitor: current flows only while its charge changes. A BJT's base–emitter is a forward-biased junction that continuously supplies holes lost to recombination and back-injection.

What is the "inversion" in an inversion channel?

The p-type surface under the gate becomes n-type: the gate's field raises the surface potential until electrons (normally the minority carrier) outnumber holes there. That electron sheet connects the n+ source and drain.

A datasheet says VGS(th) = 2–4 V and RDS(on) = 44 mΩ at VGS = 10 V. Is it suitable to switch 3 A from a 3.3 V pin?

No. VGS(th) is the barely-on point (often 250 µA). At 3.3 V, a sample with a 3–4 V threshold may be barely on or off. Only trust it at a VGS where RDS(on) is specified. Use a part specified at 2.5 V, or a gate driver.

A MOSFET has RDS(on) = 25 mΩ (hot). How much heat at 6 A?

P = I²R = 36 × 0.025 = 0.9 W. In a TO-220 package without a heatsink that is warm but usually acceptable; check the thermal resistance on the datasheet.

Why does fast PWM need a gate driver even if RDS(on) is tiny?

Each transition takes t ≈ Qg/Igate and dissipates about ½VIt. A weak microcontroller pin gives microsecond transitions; at tens of kHz the switching loss (watts) far exceeds the conduction loss. A driver delivering amps shrinks t to nanoseconds.

What is the gate pull-down resistor for?

To hold the gate at 0 V when the driving pin is floating (during boot, reset, or with a broken wire). A floating gate can charge from stray coupling and leave the MOSFET half-on and overheating.

Why can't one n-channel MOSFET switch AC or block a reversed battery?

Its body diode conducts from source to drain whenever the drain goes below the source, whatever the gate does. Blocking both directions needs two MOSFETs back to back (sources joined), or a P-channel "ideal diode" arranged so the body diode blocks the wrong polarity.

Why does a CMOS chip use less power at 3.3 V than at 5 V, at the same clock?

Dynamic power is C V² f. (3.3/5)² ≈ 0.44, so it uses about 44 % of the power, a 56 % saving.
