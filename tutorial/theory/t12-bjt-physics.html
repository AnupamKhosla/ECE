How a bipolar transistor really works

Two junctions and a very thin base. By the end you will know why a tiny base current controls a collector current a hundred times bigger, why two diodes glued together cannot do it, and how to bias a transistor as a switch or an amplifier with real numbers.

Reading 50–60 minLevel After T11Practice P08Needs diode equation, minority carriers

A short history

Before transistors, amplification meant vacuum tubes: hot, fragile, power-hungry glass bulbs. Bell Labs wanted a solid-state replacement for telephone networks. In December 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain pressed two gold contacts, very close together, onto a slab of germanium. A small current into one contact changed a larger current at the other. That was the point-contact transistor. It worked, but it was delicate and hard to make consistently, and nobody fully understood it.

Their group leader, William Shockley, worked out a cleaner idea in early 1948: a sandwich of three doped layers with two p–n junctions, the junction transistor. Its theory could be calculated with the same diffusion and drift physics as the diode. Working junction transistors followed around 1950–51, the first silicon transistors arrived in 1954, and the three men shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. The word "transistor" comes from "transfer resistor". Every BJT you buy today is Shockley's junction transistor; the point-contact device is history.

The structure of an NPN transistor

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three regions. "Bipolar" means both electrons and holes take part. In an NPN device:

  • Emitter (E), n-type, very heavily doped (around 1019–1020 cm−3). Its job is to inject electrons.
  • Base (B), p-type, very thin (well under a micrometre to a few micrometres in modern parts) and much more lightly doped than the emitter (around 1017 cm−3).
  • Collector (C), n-type, lightly doped (around 1015–1016 cm−3) and thick. Its job is to collect electrons and to stand off a high voltage.

So the device is not symmetrical. Swap emitter and collector and it still works a little, but with a gain of maybe 1–10 instead of 100–300. Each region's doping and size is chosen for its job.

How it works, step by step

Bias it the normal way, called forward-active: base–emitter junction forward-biased (base about 0.65 V above emitter) and collector–base junction reverse-biased (collector several volts above base).

  1. The emitter injects electrons into the base. The B–E junction is an ordinary forward-biased diode (T11). Its barrier is lowered, so electrons pour from emitter into base, exponentially more with VBE. Holes also go the other way, from base into emitter, but the emitter has about 100× more dopant than the base, so electron injection dominates by about 100× or more. (Remember: injected current from each side scales with that side's majority density.)
  2. In the base, the electrons are minority carriers. There is little field in the neutral base, so they move by diffusion: many electrons near the emitter edge, almost none at the collector edge, so they spread towards the collector.
  3. Most cross before they recombine. In T11 you saw that an electron in p-type silicon survives for a diffusion length L of tens of micrometres. A modern base is under a micrometre to a few micrometres wide. So only a small fraction meet a hole and recombine on the way.
  4. The collector junction sweeps them up. At the far edge of the base, the electron meets the reverse-biased C–B depletion layer. For a majority electron in the collector that is a barrier, but for a minority electron arriving from the base it is a downhill slide: the field throws it straight into the collector. The reverse bias does not stop these electrons; it collects them.
  5. The base current is small. The base terminal only has to replace the holes lost to recombination in the base and the holes injected back into the emitter. That is a fraction of a percent of the electron flow.
IE = IC + IB IC = α IE IC = β IB β = α / (1 − α) α (alpha) is the fraction of emitter current that reaches the collector, typically 0.99–0.997. β (beta, also written hFE on datasheets) is the current gain. α = 0.99 gives β = 99; α = 0.996 gives β = 249.

Illustrative numbers. Take base width WB = 1 µm and electron diffusion length in the base Ln = 20 µm. The fraction lost to recombination while crossing is roughly WB²/(2Ln²) = 1/800 ≈ 0.13 %. Back-injection of holes into an emitter with 100× more doping costs very roughly another 0.3 %. Total loss about 0.4 %, so α ≈ 0.996 and β ≈ 250. These are teaching estimates; real β comes from the datasheet and varies a lot between parts of the same type (for example a BC547 is sold in gain groups, and a 2N2222's datasheet gives a wide minimum-to-maximum range).

Common misconception

Wrong: "The base current is amplified: the transistor takes the base current and multiplies it." Right: VBE sets how many electrons the emitter injects. The base current is just the small "tax" that comes with that injection. Because the tax is a near-fixed fraction, IC ≈ β IB is true and useful, but the physical control is the base–emitter voltage through the exponential diode law. The energy for the big current comes from the collector supply, not from the base.

Why two diodes back to back do not make a transistor

Look at a transistor with a multimeter's diode test and it behaves like two diodes sharing the base: B→E reads about 0.65 V, B→C reads about 0.65 V, and C↔E reads open both ways. That is a useful field test. So why can't you solder two 1N4148s anode-to-anode and get gain?

Because the trick depends on electrons crossing the base as minority carriers before they recombine. In two separate diodes, the "base" is: the p region of diode 1, a metal wire, and the p region of diode 2. That path is millimetres long.

  • Electrons injected into diode 1's p region all recombine within a few diffusion lengths (tens of µm). None survive to the wire.
  • In the metal wire there are no minority carriers to deliver. Current in the wire is just the base current flowing out to the base terminal.
  • Diode 2's reverse-biased junction only ever sees the tiny equilibrium minority population of its own p region. It passes its normal leakage, nothing more.
fraction surviving ≈ e−W/L 2 diodes: W ≈ 1 mm, L ≈ 20 µm → e−50 ≈ 2×10−22 One transistor: W ≈ 1 µm, L ≈ 20 µm → about 99.9 % cross. The base must be thin compared with the diffusion length; that single geometric fact is the whole difference.

So with two diodes, all the emitter current comes out of the "base" terminal, and β ≈ 0. Shockley's insight was that the two junctions must share one continuous, thin piece of crystal so that the carriers injected by one junction are collected by the other.

Why this matters for history

This is also why the first transistors needed the point contacts to be very close together (tens of micrometres). Bardeen and Brattain had to get the collector within reach of the minority carriers injected by the emitter contact.

VBE ≈ 0.65 V and the exponential law

Because IC is set by the B–E diode's injection, it follows the diode equation:

IC ≈ IS eVBE/VT VT ≈ 26 mV Worked: IS = 10−14 A, IC = 1 mA → VBE = 0.026 × ln(10−3/10−14) = 0.026 × 25.3 ≈ 0.66 V. At 10 mA: +60 mV, 0.72 V. At 100 µA: 0.60 V.

That is why "VBE ≈ 0.6–0.7 V" works as a design number. Two consequences you will meet:

  • Never drive the base straight from a voltage source. Every extra 18 mV doubles IC. A 3.3 V pin straight on a base would try to push enormous current. Always use a base resistor, which turns the voltage into a defined base current.
  • Temperature. At fixed IC, VBE falls about 2 mV/°C. A transistor biased with fixed VBE heats up, conducts more, heats more: thermal runaway. The emitter resistor in the amplifier below prevents that.
  • Transconductance. The slope dIC/dVBE = IC/VT. At 1 mA that is 1/26 Ω ≈ 38 mA per volt. A 1 mV wiggle on the base changes IC by 38 µA. This steep slope is why BJTs make good amplifiers.

Cut-off, active and saturation

The output characteristics plot IC against VCE for several fixed base currents. Each curve rises steeply, then goes almost flat: once VCE is more than a few tenths of a volt, the collector collects everything the emitter sends, and more voltage hardly adds current. (The slight upward tilt is the Early effect: more C–B reverse voltage widens that depletion layer, which makes the neutral base slightly thinner, which loses fewer electrons.)

What saturation really means. The transistor can only pass what the load allows. If β IB asks for more than (VCC − VCE(sat))/RC, the collector voltage falls until the C–B junction becomes forward-biased too. Then the collector also injects into the base, IC stops following β IB, and the transistor sits at a low VCE. "Saturated" does not mean "full of current"; it means "the transistor is no longer the thing limiting the current". The extra charge stored in the base in saturation is why a saturated BJT takes some time (often hundreds of ns to µs) to turn off.

Worked example 1: a BJT as a switch

A 3.3 V microcontroller pin must switch a 12 V relay with a 400 Ω coil. A small NPN (BC547 or 2N2222 class) switches the low side.

  1. Load current. IC = 12 V / 400 Ω = 30 mA (ignoring the 0.2 V VCE(sat)).
  2. Base current. For a switch, do not design for the typical β. Use a forced β of about 10–20 so the transistor saturates hard even if this one has a low gain or is cold. With forced β = 10: IB = 3 mA.
  3. Base resistor. RB = (3.3 − 0.7)/3 mA ≈ 870 Ω. Choose 1 kΩ: IB = 2.6 mA, forced β ≈ 11.5. A 3.3 V GPIO can source 2.6 mA comfortably.
  4. Check power. P = VCE(sat) × IC ≈ 0.2 × 0.03 = 6 mW. Tiny. A switch is either fully off (no current) or fully on (almost no voltage), so it wastes little power.
  5. Flyback diode. Across the coil, cathode to +12 V. Without it, the coil's stored energy drives the collector to tens or hundreds of volts at turn-off.

Worked example 2: biasing a small amplifier

Goal: a common-emitter amplifier on 12 V with IC ≈ 1 mA and the collector sitting near half supply, so the output can swing both ways. The trick that makes it reliable is the emitter resistor RE.

  1. Pick RE = 1 kΩ. At 1 mA, VE ≈ 1 V. So VB ≈ 1 + 0.65 = 1.65 V.
  2. Pick RC = 5.6 kΩ. VC = 12 − 1 mA × 5.6 kΩ = 6.4 V. Roughly mid-way between VE and 12 V.
  3. Base divider. IB ≈ 1 mA/150 ≈ 7 µA. Make the divider current about 10× that so IB barely loads it: R1 = 100 kΩ (top), R2 = 18 kΩ (bottom).
  4. Check properly. Thevenin: VTH = 12 × 18/118 = 1.83 V, RTH = 100k ∥ 18k = 15.3 kΩ. Then IB = (1.83 − 0.65)/(15.3k + 151 × 1k) = 1.18/166k ≈ 7.1 µA, IC = 150 × 7.1 µA ≈ 1.07 mA, VC = 12 − 1.07 × 5.6 ≈ 6.0 V, VCE ≈ 6.0 − 1.07 ≈ 4.9 V. Active region.
  5. Swap in a transistor with β = 300. IB = 1.18/(15.3k + 301k) ≈ 3.7 µA, IC ≈ 1.12 mA. β doubled, IC moved 5 %. That is the point of RE: it is negative feedback. If IC tries to rise, VE rises, VBE shrinks, and IC is pulled back.
  6. Gain. A small input wiggle on the base appears on the emitter (it follows the base), so the emitter current changes by Δv/(RE + re), where re = VT/IC ≈ 26 Ω. The same change flows through RC. Voltage gain ≈ −RC/(RE + re) = −5600/1026 ≈ −5.5 (inverted). Bypassing RE with a capacitor raises the AC gain a lot, at the cost of distortion and predictability; op-amps (T14) usually do this job better today.

PNP: the mirror image

A PNP transistor swaps every doping type: p++ emitter, thin n base, p collector. Now holes are injected from the emitter, cross the base and are collected. Every voltage and current reverses:

  • The emitter sits at the most positive point (often the supply rail).
  • The base must be pulled about 0.65 V below the emitter to turn it on (VEB ≈ 0.65 V).
  • Current flows out of the base and out of the collector. In the symbol, the emitter arrow points in ("PNP: Pointing iN Proudly" is one memory aid).

PNPs are used as high-side switches: between the positive supply and the load, so the load's other end can stay on ground. Holes have lower mobility than electrons (about 480 against 1350 cm²/V·s in lightly doped silicon), so a PNP is a bit slower and has a bit less gain than a matching NPN, which is one reason NPN parts are more common.

Bench tip

A PNP high-side switch on a 12 V rail cannot be turned off by a 3.3 V microcontroller pin: 3.3 V on the base is still 8.7 V below the emitter, so it stays on. The usual fix is a small NPN that pulls the PNP's base down when the pin goes high, plus a resistor from the PNP's base to its emitter to hold it off. You will build exactly this kind of driver in P08.

See it on the bench

In P08 you drive a relay and a small motor from a microcontroller pin with an NPN, measure VBE and VCE(sat), and fit a flyback diode. Expected readings: VBE ≈ 0.65–0.8 V when switched on, VCE ≈ 0.05–0.3 V in saturation, and the full supply across C–E when off.

On the job

Small BJT and Darlington (two BJTs in one package, β in the thousands) stages are inside PLC and BMS controller digital outputs, relay driver boards, and the "open collector" outputs of alarm panels, smoke detectors and access-control readers. "Open collector" means the transistor's collector is the terminal: it can pull a line down to 0 V, but you supply the pull-up. Knowing that saves hours when an alarm input "doesn't see" a device.

Safety

Keep your transistor experiments at extra-low voltage (5, 12 or 24 V DC). The relay you switch on the bench should switch another ELV circuit, never mains.

Check yourself

Why must the base be thin compared with the diffusion length?

Electrons injected into the base are minority carriers that cross by diffusion. They survive a typical distance L (tens of µm). If the base is much thinner than L (around 1 µm), nearly all reach the collector junction. If it is many L wide (as in two separate diodes), they all recombine and the base current equals the emitter current: no gain.

α = 0.995. What is β? If IB = 20 µA, what is IC?

β = α/(1 − α) = 0.995/0.005 = 199. IC = 199 × 20 µA ≈ 4 mA, and IE = 4.02 mA.

Why does a reverse-biased collector junction pass a large current in a transistor, when a reverse-biased diode passes almost none?

A reverse-biased junction blocks majority carriers but sweeps across any minority carriers that reach its edge. In a lone diode there are very few. In a transistor the emitter floods the base with minority electrons, so the collector junction has a large supply to sweep across. The current is set by the emitter injection, not by the collector voltage.

VCC = 12 V, RC = 1 kΩ, β = 100, IB = 200 µA. Which region, and what is IC?

β IB = 20 mA, but the load only allows (12 − 0.2)/1 k ≈ 11.8 mA. So the transistor is saturated, IC ≈ 11.8 mA, VCE ≈ 0.2 V, forced β ≈ 59.

Why do we design switches with a forced β of 10–20 instead of the datasheet's typical 200?

β varies widely between parts, falls at low temperature and at high current. Designing for 10–20 guarantees deep saturation in the worst case, so VCE stays low and the transistor stays cool.

In the amplifier example, why did doubling β change IC by only 5 %?

The emitter resistor makes IC depend mainly on VB, VBE and RE: IC ≈ (VB − 0.65)/RE. Any rise in IC raises VE, which reduces VBE and pulls IC back. That is negative feedback.

VBE rises from 0.65 V to 0.71 V at the same temperature. What happens to IC?

60 mV ≈ one decade, so IC rises about 10×. This is why you set base current with a resistor rather than forcing VBE.

Your multimeter shows 0.66 V from base to emitter, 0.65 V from base to collector, and OL from collector to emitter both ways, with the red lead on the base. NPN or PNP, and is it healthy?

Red (positive) on the base gives forward readings to both other legs, so the base is p-type: NPN. Both junctions read normal and C–E is open, so it is probably healthy. A shorted transistor shows near 0 V on C–E.
