The 555 timer: blinkers and PWM

You will understand what is inside the 555 in plain words, build an LED flasher whose speed you can calculate before you power it, build a push-button "stay on for 10 seconds" timer like a door-release timer, and dim an LED with pulse-width modulation (PWM).

Safety

Everything runs at 5–12 V DC. The NE555 is rated to about 16 V supply (check your datasheet; CMOS versions are often lower). Its output can source or sink up to about 200 mA, enough to cook an LED with no resistor. If you drive a relay or motor from it, fit the flyback diode from P08.

1 · What is inside the 555, in plain words

The 555 appeared around 1971 and is still made in huge numbers because it does one job very well: it turns the slow, predictable charging of a capacitor (P07) into clean on/off timing. Inside are five simple blocks.

The divider

Three equal resistors from VCC to ground give two reference voltages: 2/3 VCC and 1/3 VCC. Pin 5 (CONTROL) is the 2/3 point, brought out so you can adjust it or steady it with 10 nF.

Comparator A (upper)

A comparator outputs "yes" when its + input is higher than its − input. This one says "yes" when THRESHOLD (pin 6) rises above 2/3 VCC, and that resets the flip-flop.

Comparator B (lower)

This one says "yes" when TRIGGER (pin 2) falls below 1/3 VCC, and that sets the flip-flop.

The flip-flop

A one-bit memory. Set: output HIGH. Reset: output LOW. It remembers its state between events, which is what gives the 555 its two clean levels.

Output stage

A buffer that drives pin 3 close to VCC or close to 0 V, and can source or sink about 200 mA (bipolar NE555).

Discharge transistor

When the output is LOW, this transistor turns on and shorts pin 7 to ground, emptying the timing capacitor. When the output is HIGH, it lets go.

So the whole chip follows two rules: voltage on pin 2 drops below 1/3 → output HIGH, discharge released. Voltage on pin 6 rises above 2/3 → output LOW, discharge shorts to ground. Pin 4 (RESET) forces the output LOW whenever it is pulled low, so normally you tie it to VCC.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The 555 has a clock inside that sets the frequency." Right: there is no clock. The timing comes entirely from your resistor and capacitor. The 555 just watches the capacitor voltage and flips at 1/3 and 2/3 of the supply. Because both thresholds scale with VCC, the timing hardly changes when the battery runs down. That is the clever part.

2 · Astable: an LED flasher you can predict

Astable means "no stable state": the circuit keeps flipping by itself. The capacitor charges through R1 + R2 until it reaches 2/3 VCC. The output goes LOW and pin 7 shorts, so the capacitor discharges through R2 only, until it falls to 1/3 VCC. The output goes HIGH, pin 7 lets go, and charging starts again. Pins 2 and 6 are joined so both comparators watch the same capacitor.

The timing formulas, and where 0.693 comes from

During each half of the cycle the capacitor moves between 1/3 and 2/3 of the supply. Charging, the gap to the target (VCC) starts at 2/3 and ends at 1/3: it halves. Discharging, the voltage halves from 2/3 to 1/3. An RC curve halves its gap in ln 2 × τ = 0.693 τ. So:

thigh = 0.693 × (R1 + R2) × C tlow = 0.693 × R2 × C thigh is the time the output is HIGH (charging through R1 and R2). tlow is the time it is LOW (discharging through R2 only, into pin 7).
f = 1 / (thigh + tlow) = 1.44 / ((R1 + 2·R2) × C) duty = (R1 + R2) / (R1 + 2·R2) 1.44 is just 1/0.693. Duty cycle is the fraction of each cycle the output is HIGH. In this basic circuit it is always above 50%, because charging goes through both resistors.

Worked example. R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 68 kΩ, C = 10 µF.

  • thigh = 0.693 × 78 000 × 0.00001 = 0.54 s
  • tlow = 0.693 × 68 000 × 0.00001 = 0.47 s
  • f = 1.44 / (146 000 × 0.00001) = 0.99 Hz, about one flash per second; duty = 78/146 = 53%

  1. Place the 555 across the centre channel of the breadboard, notch to the left. Pin 1 is bottom-left; pins count anticlockwise, so the top row reads 8, 7, 6, 5 from left to right (Figure 3).

  2. Power pins first. Pin 8 and pin 4 to +, pin 1 to −. Put 100 nF right across pins 8 and 1, and 100 µF across the rails where the battery connects.

  3. Timing parts. R1 from + to pin 7. R2 from pin 7 to pin 6. A jumper from pin 6 to pin 2. 10 µF from pin 2 to − (stripe to −). 10 nF from pin 5 to −.

  4. Output. 470 Ω from pin 3 to the LED's anode; LED cathode to −.

  5. Power up and time it. Count 10 flashes with a phone stopwatch. Expect about 10 s (between about 8 and 12 s with a ±20% capacitor).

  6. Measure. Meter on V DC at pin 2/6: it swings between about 3 V and 6 V on a 9 V supply (1/3 and 2/3). Pin 3 swings between near 0 V and about 1.5 V below the supply (the bipolar output stage loses a little).

Why it works

The 555 is a capacitor timer plus a memory with hysteresis: two thresholds with a gap between them. Without the gap, the circuit would chatter at one voltage. With it, the capacitor must travel all the way from 1/3 to 2/3 and back, and each trip takes a fixed fraction of the RC time constant. The comparators are the same idea as the op-amp comparator in T14, and the flip-flop is the memory cell from T15.

3 · Monostable: "stay on for 10 seconds"

Monostable means "one stable state". The output rests LOW. A short trigger pulse on pin 2 sets it HIGH for a fixed time, then it returns to LOW by itself. This is exactly a door-release timer: press the exit button, the lock releases for 10 seconds, then it locks again.

t = 1.1 × R × C = 1.1 × 91 000 × 0.0001 ≈ 10 s Charging from 0 to 2/3 of the supply leaves a gap of 1/3, so t = τ × ln 3 = 1.0986 τ, rounded to 1.1. With R = 100 kΩ you get 11 s. Electrolytic tolerance (±20%) means your result will be somewhere between about 8 and 12 s: trim with R if you need it exact.

  1. Build Figure 4. Same power wiring as before. Pins 6 and 7 are joined to the top of C; R from + to that node; 10 kΩ pull-up and button on pin 2.

  2. Press and time. A short press: the LED stays on for about 10 s, then goes out. Repeat three times; the times should agree within a few percent.

  3. Hold the button for 15 s. The LED stays on while you hold it and goes out after you release (once the time has passed). A trigger held low keeps the output HIGH.

  4. Drive a relay. Replace the LED with the P08 BC547 relay driver (or drive a small relay coil directly from pin 3, with the flyback diode). Now the timer switches a separate circuit: that is a door-release timer.

Bench tip: edge triggering

If a held button must not stretch the time, couple the button to pin 2 through a 10 nF capacitor, with the 10 kΩ pull-up still on pin 2. Now only the moment of pressing makes a short negative pulse; holding the button does nothing more.

On the job

The "door open time" or "unlock time" on an access controller, the hold time on a PIR-controlled light, the run-on timer for an exhaust fan, and the off-the-shelf "time delay relay" modules sold for security work all do what this circuit does. Modern controllers do it in software, and DIN-rail timer relays in plant rooms do it with a microcontroller, but the behaviour is the same: a trigger, a fixed time, and a relay that drops out. When a site complains that "the door relocks too fast", you will be looking for this setting, and when an old timer module drifts long on a cold morning, you will suspect its electrolytic capacitor.

4 · PWM: dimming an LED

Pulse-width modulation (PWM) controls average power by switching fully on and fully off very fast and changing the fraction of time spent on (the duty cycle). An LED switched at 1 kHz with 25% duty looks steadily lit at about a quarter of its full brightness. The switch is always either fully on or fully off, so almost no power is wasted in it. This is how LED strips, motor speed controllers and many power supplies work.

The basic astable cannot go below 50% duty, because charging goes through both resistors. Two diodes fix that by giving charge and discharge separate paths through a potentiometer.

thigh ≈ 0.693 × (1 kΩ + RA) × C tlow ≈ 0.693 × RB × C RA + RB = 100 kΩ Period ≈ 0.693 × 101 kΩ × 10 nF ≈ 0.7 ms, so about 1.4 kHz whatever the pot position. The diode drops shift the numbers a little, more so on a 5 V supply.

  1. Build Figure 5 on a fresh 555 (or rewire the astable). Output to LED + 470 Ω as before.

  2. Turn the pot from end to end. The LED goes from very dim to full, with no visible flicker (1.4 kHz is far above what eyes notice).

  3. Measure pin 3 on V DC. The meter averages the square wave, so it shows roughly duty × output-high voltage. At 9 V supply and ~7.5 V output-high, a 25% setting reads about 1.9 V. This is a neat way to "see" duty cycle with a meter.

  4. Film the LED with your phone's normal video: you may see rolling dark bands. That is the camera's line-by-line shutter catching the PWM, the same effect as the 50 Hz LED in P06.

  5. Scale it up. Power the 555 from 12 V and drive the P08 logic-level MOSFET gate from pin 3 (keep the 220 Ω gate resistor and 10 kΩ pull-down). Now the pot dims a 12 V LED strip. Fit the flyback diode if you use a motor instead.

Common misconception

Wrong: "PWM at 50% gives the LED half the voltage." Right: the LED always sees either full voltage or none. It is the time-average of the current that halves. That distinction matters for motors (which feel the average because of their inertia) and for meters (which may misread PWM). A cheap "dimmer" made with a resistor instead wastes the difference as heat; PWM does not.

Check yourself

What two voltages does the 555 compare against, and what happens at each?

1/3 VCC and 2/3 VCC, made by three equal internal resistors. When TRIGGER (pin 2) falls below 1/3, the flip-flop is set: output HIGH, discharge transistor off. When THRESHOLD (pin 6) rises above 2/3, it is reset: output LOW, discharge transistor on.

Calculate f and duty for R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF.

f = 1.44 / ((1 000 + 20 000) × 100 × 10−9) = 1.44 / 0.0021 ≈ 686 Hz. Duty = (1 + 10)/(1 + 20) = 52%. Making R1 small compared with R2 brings the duty close to 50%.

Why does the 555's timing stay almost the same when the battery voltage drops from 9 V to 7 V?

Both thresholds are fractions of the supply (1/3 and 2/3), and the capacitor charges toward the same supply. The fraction of the RC curve the capacitor must travel is the same at any voltage, so the time (0.693 RC per half-cycle) does not depend on VCC.

Where does the 1.1 in the monostable formula come from?

The capacitor starts empty and must charge to 2/3 VCC, leaving 1/3 of the gap. That takes τ × ln(1 / (1/3)) = τ × ln 3 = 1.0986 τ ≈ 1.1 RC.

You need a 5-second door-release time using a 47 µF capacitor. What resistor?

R = t / (1.1 × C) = 5 / (1.1 × 0.000047) ≈ 96.7 kΩ. Use 100 kΩ (about 5.2 s) or 91 kΩ (about 4.7 s), and remember the ±20% capacitor tolerance. Trim with a pot in series if it must be exact.

Why can the basic astable not produce a duty cycle below 50%, and how does the PWM circuit get around it?

Charging goes through R1 + R2 and discharging through R2 only, so the HIGH time is always longer than the LOW time. The PWM circuit uses two diodes so charge and discharge flow through different parts of the potentiometer; either part can be the larger one.

Your 555 flasher works on the bench but glitches when it switches a relay. Name two fixes.

Fit the flyback diode across the relay coil (band to +). Add 100 nF directly across pins 8 and 1 plus a 10–100 µF bulk capacitor on the supply, and a 10 nF on pin 5. Keep the relay's current path away from the timing parts.

What does a DC meter on pin 3 show for a 1.4 kHz PWM at 30% duty, and why?

About 30% of the output-high voltage, for example about 2.2 V if the output swings to 7.5 V. The meter averages over many cycles, so it reports the time-average of the square wave, which is duty × high level.
