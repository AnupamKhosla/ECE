Diodes on the bench: rectifiers and protection

By the end of this lab you will test a diode with your meter, watch an LED flicker at 50 Hz on a low-voltage AC plug-pack, turn AC into DC with one diode and then with a bridge, predict every reading before you take it, protect a circuit from reversed wires, and design a Zener regulator.

Safety: stay on the low-voltage side

The plug-pack is a factory-sealed, approved appliance. You plug it into the wall and work only on its thin low-voltage output lead. Never open a plug-pack, never wire a bare transformer to 230 V, and never measure inside anything mains-powered. In Australia, mains wiring is legally limited to licensed electricians. A 12 V AC plug-pack peaks near 17–20 V, which is well inside the extra-low-voltage (ELV) limit of 50 V AC.

In Australia

Plug-packs sold here must meet the Electrical Equipment Safety System and should carry the RCM mark (a tick inside a triangle). Cheap no-name AC adaptors from overseas marketplaces sometimes do not. Buy the AC plug-pack locally from Jaycar or Altronics. AC plug-packs are less common than they were, so ask for "AC output plugpack" or "AC adaptor". If your state regulator publishes recalls, check them; rules and enforcement differ by state, so confirm with your state's electrical safety regulator.

1 · Read a diode with your meter

A diode lets conventional current flow one way only: from the anode (A) to the cathode (K). On the part, the cathode end has a painted band. On the symbol, the cathode is the bar, and the triangle points the way current is allowed to flow.

  1. Set the meter to diode test (the symbol looks like the diode in Figure 1, often shared with continuity). Touch the probes together: it should read close to 0.000 and may beep.

  2. Test a 1N4007 forward. Red probe on the anode (plain end), black probe on the band. Expect about 0.55–0.65 V. Write your number down; you will use it later.

  3. Reverse it. Swap the probes. Expect OL. A good diode blocks.

  4. Test a 1N4148 and a 1N5819. The 1N4148 reads about the same as the 1N4007 (both silicon p–n junctions). The 1N5819 Schottky reads lower, about 0.15–0.30 V at this tiny current. That is why Schottkys are used where every tenth of a volt matters.

  5. Test the LEDs. Red probe on the long leg (anode). A red LED usually reads about 1.6–1.9 V and may glow faintly. Blue and white LEDs need 2.6–3 V. Some meters cannot push that much, so they show OL even for a good blue LED. That is a meter limit, not a dead LED.

  6. Find the bad-diode signatures. A shorted diode reads near 0 V both ways (and the meter beeps). An open diode reads OL both ways. A leaky diode reads something both ways. Shorted is the common failure in rectifiers after a surge.

Why it works

Joining p-type and n-type silicon creates a depletion region and an internal equilibrium potential that depends on doping and temperature. Forward bias lowers the carrier-injection barrier, so current rises continuously and steeply; there is no universal 0.7 V switch point. The measured forward drop depends on current and temperature and is not simply the built-in potential. Reverse bias usually permits only a small leakage current until breakdown. Schottky and LED materials/structures give different current–voltage behaviour; an LED’s band gap also helps set its colour.

2 · An LED on AC: see 50 Hz with your own eyes

Mains in Australia alternates at 50 Hz: 50 full cycles per second. The plug-pack's transformer steps it down, but the frequency stays at 50 Hz. If you drive an LED from that low-voltage AC, it can only light on the half-cycle that forward-biases it. So it switches on and off 50 times every second. Your eyes blur that into steady light, but a phone can catch it.

The circuit and the numbers

First measure your plug-pack with the meter on V AC. A "12 V AC" plug-pack often reads 13–15 V with no load, because a simple transformer's rated voltage is at full load. Use your measured number.

Vpeak = 1.414 × Vrms = 1.414 × 12 ≈ 17 V The meter's AC reading is the RMS value (the "heating-equivalent" value). A sine wave's peak is √2 ≈ 1.414 times bigger. The LED and resistor see the peak, so design for the peak.
Ipeak = (Vpeak − VLED) / R = (17 − 2) / 1000 = 15 mA 15 mA at the very top of each wave is safe for a 5 mm LED rated at 20 mA. The average current is much lower, about 4–5 mA, because the LED is off half the time and below peak most of the rest.

The resistor is in the circuit for both half-cycles, so it sees nearly the full 12 V RMS: P ≈ 11² / 1000 ≈ 0.12 W. A 0.25 W resistor survives but runs warm; a 0.5 W resistor or a 1.5 kΩ value runs cool.

There is one more problem. On the reverse half-cycle, the LED would see about 17 V backwards. Most LEDs are only rated for 5 V reverse. So we put an ordinary diode anti-parallel across the LED: same two points, pointing the opposite way. On the reverse half-cycle that diode conducts and holds the LED's reverse voltage to about 0.7 V.

  1. Build it unpowered. Plug-pack output to a screw-terminal adaptor, then into the breadboard rails. Resistor in series with the top wire. LED and guard diode across the bottom of the resistor and the other wire, pointing opposite ways.

  2. Power up and look. The LED lights, maybe a little dimmer than on DC with the same resistor (it is only on part of the time).

  3. Wave it. In a dim room, move the breadboard quickly from side to side, or flick your eyes past the LED. You see a dotted line instead of a smooth streak. Each dot is one 10 ms "on" pulse.

  4. Film it in slow motion. Set the phone to slow-mo (120 or 240 frames per second) and film the LED close up. At 240 fps you get about 4–5 frames per 20 ms cycle, so on playback the LED visibly blinks on and off. Even normal video often shows dark bands rolling across the LED: that is the camera's line-by-line (rolling) shutter catching the flicker.

  5. Measure. Meter on V AC across the plug-pack: about 12–15 V. Meter on V DC across the LED: a small, odd number. A DC meter averages, and the LED voltage is +2 V for half the cycle and −0.7 V for the other half, so you get something like 0.5–0.8 V. That number is not wrong; it is an average of a shape the meter was not designed for.

Try this: a two-colour AC indicator

Replace the guard diode with a second LED of a different colour, also anti-parallel. Now a red LED lights on one half-cycle and a green one on the other, each protecting the other. On DC only one colour lights, which way round depends on polarity. Film it in slow motion: the two colours alternate. This is how some "AC/DC present" indicator lamps work.

3 · Half-wave rectifier: one diode

A rectifier turns AC into DC that flows one way. The simplest version is a single diode in series with the load. It passes the positive half-cycles and blocks the negative ones.

Predict before you measure

Two ideas give you every number.

  • Peak: the output peak is the AC peak minus one diode drop. With 12 V AC: 17.0 − 0.7 ≈ 16.3 V.
  • What the DC meter shows: a DC meter shows the average voltage over time, not the peak. A half-wave output is a hump for 10 ms then zero for 10 ms. The average of one sine hump is 2/π of its peak, and it is "on" only half the time, so the average is 1/π ≈ 0.318 of the peak.
VDC meter ≈ Vpeak,out / π = 16.3 / 3.14 ≈ 5.2 V The load "feels" pulses up to 16 V, but the meter averages them to about 5 V. Neither number is the 12 V written on the plug-pack. Learn to ask "which 12 V?" whenever someone quotes an AC voltage.

4 · Full-wave bridge rectifier: four diodes

The half-wave rectifier wastes every second half-cycle. A bridge of four diodes steers both half-cycles through the load in the same direction. On each half-cycle, two diodes conduct and two block. Current always goes through two diodes in series, so you lose two diode drops (about 1.4 V) instead of one.

  1. Build the bridge from four 1N4007s exactly as Figure 4 (bands toward the + corner for D1 and D2, bands toward the AC corners for D3 and D4). Or use a bridge module: its pins are marked ~, ~, + and −.

  2. Check it unpowered. Diode test from − to each ~ pin, and from each ~ pin to +: four readings of about 0.6 V. Reverse each: OL.

  3. Add a 1 kΩ load between + and −, then power up.

  4. Measure V DC across the load. Compare with the table below.

  5. Swap the two AC wires. The DC reading does not change sign. That is the whole point of a bridge: the output polarity no longer depends on how the input is connected.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A 12 V AC supply gives 12 V DC after a rectifier." Right: the 12 V is an RMS value. The peak is about 17 V. After a bridge you get pulses that peak near 15.6 V and average about 10 V. Add a big capacitor and the DC sits near the peak, about 15–16 V with a light load. So a "12 V AC" plug-pack can easily give 16 V DC, which can damage a 12 V device that has no regulator. Always measure, and always ask which kind of voltage a number means: RMS, peak or average.

Why the meter reads low

On V DC, a meter measures the average voltage over many cycles. A rectified sine spends only a moment at its peak and lots of time lower down, so its average is 0.637 of the peak for full-wave and 0.318 for half-wave. On V AC, a cheap "average-responding" meter assumes a pure sine and scales its reading; on a rectified shape that guess is wrong. A "True RMS" meter calculates RMS from the actual shape. For pulsing waveforms, a scope (P15) is the honest tool.

On the job

Many access-control and security devices are labelled "12–24 V AC/DC". Inside is a bridge rectifier, so you can connect either polarity and either AC or DC, and you lose about 1.4 V in the bridge. Electric door strikes are often sold this way. In HVAC, many controllers and actuators run from 24 V AC and use a single half-wave diode inside, with one side of the 24 V AC tied to their signal common. If several such devices share one transformer and you swap the two 24 V wires on just one of them, you can short the transformer through their internal diodes. That is why manuals insist the "24 V common" (often labelled G0, COM or ⊥) goes to the same transformer leg on every device.

6 · Reverse-polarity protection

On site, someone will connect + and − the wrong way round. It happens with 12 V cameras, readers, sensors and the batteries in alarm panels. Electronics can be destroyed in under a second. A protection part sits at the power input and stops current flowing the wrong way. Each method has a cost: the voltage it drops, the heat it makes, and the parts it adds.

  1. Build (a) with a 1N4007, a 9 V battery and a 470 Ω resistor plus an LED as the "load". Measure the battery and then the voltage after the diode. Difference: about 0.7 V at this small current.

  2. Reverse the battery. The LED stays off and the meter after the diode reads 0 V. Nothing was damaged.

  3. Swap in a 1N5819 and measure again. Expect about 0.3 V lost instead of 0.7 V.

  4. Optional: build (b) with a P-channel MOSFET whose on-resistance is specified at the gate voltage available in this 9 V circuit. Check the full datasheet: drain–source rating, gate–source limit (including fresh-battery voltage), current and package dissipation. Threshold voltage alone does not establish full drive. Use a documented breakout and measure the actual voltage loss at this small load; see T13.

Bench tip

A "12 V" device fed through a series diode from a 12 V supply gets about 11.3 V. If the device also sits at the end of a long cable, that lost 0.7 V adds to the cable drop (P10). Many cameras stop working reliably below about 11 V. A Schottky or a MOSFET buys that margin back.

7 · A Zener shunt regulator

A Zener diode is made to break down at a known reverse voltage, such as 5.1 V. Below that voltage it blocks. At that voltage it conducts as much current as needed to stop the voltage rising further. Put a resistor in series to limit the current, and you have a simple shunt regulator: the Zener "shunts" (diverts) extra current to ground so the output stays near VZ.

Design it by hand

Design rule: at the lowest input voltage and the highest load current, the Zener must still get a few milliamps to stay in regulation. Then check the worst case for heat: highest input voltage with the load disconnected, when the Zener takes everything.

R = (Vin,min − VZ) / (IL,max + IZ,min) = (9 − 5.1) / (0.010 + 0.005) = 260 Ω → use 220 Ω Round down to a standard value so the current is slightly higher than needed (never round up, or the Zener may starve). With 220 Ω: IR = 3.9 / 220 = 17.7 mA.
PR = (Vin − VZ) × IR = 3.9 × 0.0177 ≈ 0.07 W PZ,worst = VZ × IR = 5.1 × 0.0177 ≈ 0.09 W Both are comfortable for 0.25 W resistors and a 0.5 W or 1 W Zener. Rule of thumb: run parts at no more than half their power rating.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Remove the load and the regulator relaxes and runs cooler." Right: in a shunt regulator, the resistor current hardly changes. Remove the load and the Zener must absorb all of it, so the Zener is hottest with no load. That is the opposite of a series regulator like the 7805, which runs coolest with no load.

On the job

You will rarely build a Zener regulator to power something on site. You will meet Zeners inside equipment as voltage references, as clamps on input terminals (stopping a spike from reaching a chip), and in "transient voltage suppressor" (TVS) form across power inputs and data lines. A TVS is a fast, rugged Zener-like diode made to absorb short surges. When a camera or controller input is "dead short" after a lightning storm, a failed TVS or protection diode across the input is a common cause.

8 · LED colours and forward voltages

An LED's forward voltage is not random. Light comes out when an electron drops across the band gap and gives its energy to one photon. Bigger gap → more energy per photon → shorter wavelength (bluer light) → higher forward voltage. A handy conversion: photon energy in electron-volts ≈ 1240 ÷ wavelength in nanometres.

Red, 630 nm: 1240 / 630 ≈ 1.97 eV → VF ≈ 2.0 V Blue, 470 nm: 1240 / 470 ≈ 2.64 eV → VF ≈ 3.0 V The forward voltage is a little above the photon energy (in volts) because of resistance in the chip and its layers. A white LED is a blue LED with a yellow phosphor coating, so it has a blue LED's voltage.

Check yourself

Your plug-pack reads 13.5 V AC. Predict the bridge output peak and the DC meter reading with a resistor load.

Peak AC = 1.414 × 13.5 ≈ 19.1 V. Two diode drops: 19.1 − 1.4 ≈ 17.7 V peak. The DC meter shows the average of a full-wave shape, 0.637 × 17.7 ≈ 11.3 V. With a large smoothing capacitor and a light load, the output would sit near 17 V.

Why does the LED on AC need a diode connected anti-parallel across it?

On the reverse half-cycle the LED would see the full AC peak backwards (about 17 V on a 12 V AC pack). Most LEDs are rated for only about 5 V reverse. The anti-parallel diode conducts on that half-cycle and limits the LED's reverse voltage to about 0.7 V. The resistor still limits the current in both directions.

Why does the ripple from a full-wave rectifier on Australian mains have a frequency of 100 Hz, not 50 Hz?

Mains is 50 Hz: one positive and one negative half-cycle every 20 ms. The bridge flips each negative half up, so there are two humps every 20 ms, one every 10 ms. That is 100 humps per second. A half-wave rectifier keeps only one hump per cycle, so its ripple is 50 Hz. This matters for smoothing capacitors in P07.

In diode-test mode a part reads 0.004 V in both directions and the meter beeps. What does that tell you?

The diode is shorted. A good diode reads about 0.6 V one way and OL the other. Shorted is the usual failure after a surge or overload. In a bridge, a shorted diode often blows the supply's fuse, because on one half-cycle it puts the AC source straight across another conducting diode.

A 12 V, 1 A camera is protected with a 1N4007 in series. How much voltage and power does that protection cost, and what would you use instead?

At 1 A a 1N4007 drops about 0.9–1 V, so the camera sees about 11 V and the diode dissipates about 1 W, which is hot for a small diode at its full rating. A 1N5819 Schottky halves the loss (about 0.45 V, 0.45 W). A P-channel MOSFET with 0.05 Ω on-resistance loses only 0.05 V and 0.05 W.

In the Zener regulator, when is the Zener hottest, and why?

At the highest input voltage with the load disconnected. The resistor current is set by (Vin − VZ)/R and barely depends on the load. Whatever the load does not take, the Zener must take. With no load, it takes all of it: P = VZ × IR.

Why must you not clip two oscilloscope ground leads onto a bridge rectifier, one on an AC terminal and one on DC −?

A mains-powered scope's ground clips are all joined together inside the scope and to mains earth. Two clips at two different points join those points with a wire. Joining an AC terminal to DC − shorts one of the bridge diodes. On the half-cycle when the opposite diode conducts, the AC source is shorted through a single diode, and large current flows. Use one ground clip, measure relative to it, or use a differential probe.

A blue LED will not light from your meter's diode test, but a red one glows faintly. Is the blue LED dead?

Probably not. Blue LEDs need about 2.8–3 V to conduct because their band gap is wider (blue photons carry more energy, about 2.6 eV). Many meters' diode-test supply cannot reach that, so they show OL. Test it with a 9 V battery and a 1 kΩ resistor instead.

Go deeper (optional): why a "12 V AC" plug-pack reads high with no load

A transformer has resistance in its copper windings. When current flows, some voltage is lost in that resistance, so the output falls as you load it. A small, unregulated plug-pack is labelled with its voltage at full rated current. With no load there is no loss, so you read the higher no-load voltage, often 10–25% above the label. The ratio of no-load to full-load voltage is called regulation. Small transformers have poor regulation; big ones are better. This is another reason to design for the voltage you measure, under the load you will actually draw.
