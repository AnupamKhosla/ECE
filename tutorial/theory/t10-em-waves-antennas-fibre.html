Electromagnetic waves, antennas and optical fibre

Radio, Wi-Fi, 4G and 5G, and the light in an NBN fibre are all the same thing: electric and magnetic fields that keep each other going as they travel at the speed of light. This chapter takes you from Maxwell's idea to antenna lengths, dBm and path loss, and then into glass, where total internal reflection traps light for tens of kilometres.

A short history: from equations to the NBN

By the 1860s, Faraday had shown that a changing magnetic field makes an electric field (T06). James Clerk Maxwell noticed that the equations were lopsided, and in 1865 he added the missing half: a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, even in empty space. Put the two together and something remarkable falls out of the maths: a disturbance in the fields can travel by itself, as a wave, at a speed fixed by two constants of nature. When Maxwell calculated that speed, he got the measured speed of light. Light, he concluded, is an electromagnetic wave.

Nobody had made such waves on purpose until Heinrich Hertz did it in 1887–88 with a spark gap and a loop of wire with a tiny gap across the room. A spark at the transmitter made a tiny spark at the receiver. He measured their wavelength, showed they reflect and refract like light, and remarked that they had no practical use. Within a decade Guglielmo Marconi was sending wireless telegraph messages, and in 1901 across the Atlantic. The SI unit of frequency, the hertz, honours Hertz.

Light in glass took longer. In 1966 Charles Kao and George Hockham argued that glass fibres could carry communications over long distances if the impurities that absorb light were removed, aiming for a loss below 20 dB per kilometre. In 1970 Corning made fibre that met that target. Modern fibre loses only about 0.2 dB per kilometre at 1550 nm. Kao received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009. Every NBN fibre connection rests on his idea.

In Australia

Australia has its own place in this story: research at CSIRO in the 1990s led to a patent covering techniques that made fast wireless LANs practical, and it became part of the Wi-Fi standards used worldwide. In Australia, the radio spectrum is managed by the ACMA (Australian Communications and Media Authority), and limits on human exposure to radio waves are set by ARPANSA (Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency).

Maxwell's idea: fields that sustain each other

Picture a charge that suddenly moves. Its electric field cannot change everywhere at once. Near the charge, the field changes; that changing E makes a magnetic field around it (Maxwell). That magnetic field is itself changing, so it makes an electric field a little further out (Faraday). That makes a magnetic field further out still. The disturbance leapfrogs outward, each field regenerating the other. No medium is needed: the fields are the wave.

  • E and B are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. The wave is transverse.
  • E and B rise and fall together (in phase) in a travelling wave in free space.
  • The direction of E is the wave's polarisation. A vertical antenna sends vertically polarised waves and receives them best.
c = 1 / √(μ₀ ε₀) = 1 / √(1.257 × 10⁻⁶ × 8.854 × 10⁻¹²) ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s μ₀ is the magnetic constant (T06) and ε₀ the electric constant (T05). Maxwell computed this speed from electrical measurements alone, with no light involved, and got the speed of light.

c = fλ: one equation, many scales

In one period T, the wave moves one wavelength λ. Speed = distance / time = λ / T = fλ.

Inside glass the wave travels slower: v = c/n, where n is the refractive index. For fibre, n ≈ 1.47, so v ≈ 2.04 × 10⁸ m/s. A useful field number: light in fibre takes about 5 µs per kilometre. A 1000 km fibre route adds about 5 ms of one-way delay before any equipment delay is added.

The spectrum

Common misconception

"Wi-Fi and 5G are radiation, like X-rays." The word "radiation" covers both, but the physics differs completely. Each photon carries energy E = hf. At 3.5 GHz, E ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.5 × 10⁹ ≈ 2.3 × 10⁻²⁴ J ≈ 0.000 014 eV. Breaking a chemical bond takes a few eV, roughly a hundred thousand to a million times more. Radio waves cannot ionise atoms no matter how many photons arrive; their established effect at high power is heating, and exposure limits (set in Australia by ARPANSA) are set well below levels that cause measurable heating. Being near a high-power antenna on a rooftop is still a real hazard, which is why such sites have signs and exclusion zones.

Antennas: turning current into waves

Any accelerating charge radiates. A steady DC current does not radiate; an AC current does, because its charges are constantly speeding up and slowing down. But a short wire radiates very weakly. An antenna is a conductor shaped so that the currents add up efficiently into a travelling wave. The simplest and most important is the half-wave dipole: two rods, end to end, fed at the centre, with a total length of about half a wavelength.

Why half a wavelength? At that length the antenna is resonant (T07): a wave of current travelling out to each end reflects and arrives back in step with the next push from the transmitter, so a large standing wave of current builds up, maximum at the centre and zero at the open ends. At resonance the antenna looks like a pure resistance of about 73 Ω to the cable, so power flows in without reflection.

L ≈ 0.95 × λ / 2 The factor of about 0.95 (the "end effect") accounts for the rod's thickness and the field at the ends; it varies a little with design.

Worked examples.

  • FM at 100 MHz: λ = 3 m, λ/2 = 1.5 m, L ≈ 0.95 × 1.5 ≈ 1.43 m tip to tip (about 71 cm per side).
  • Wi-Fi at 2.44 GHz: λ ≈ 12.3 cm, λ/2 ≈ 6.15 cm, L ≈ 5.8 cm.
  • Quarter-wave monopole: one rod of λ/4 over a metal ground plane, which acts as a mirror for the missing half. At 2.44 GHz: about 3 cm. This is what the stubby antenna on a router or the zig-zag trace on an ESP32 board is approximating.

Gain and dBi: antennas focus, they do not amplify

An antenna is passive; it cannot create power. What it can do is redistribute it, like the reflector in a torch. The dipole squeezes the energy that would go up and down into the horizontal directions, so in those directions it is stronger than an imaginary antenna that radiates equally in all directions (an isotropic radiator). That ratio, in decibels, is the gain in dBi (dB relative to isotropic).

Free-space path loss: the intuition

A transmitter's power spreads out over the surface of an ever-growing sphere, of area 4πd². Double the distance and the same power covers four times the area: the power density drops to a quarter, which is −6 dB. That is the inverse-square law, and it is the main reason signals weaken, even in perfect empty space. The standard formula, with d in metres and f in MHz:

FSPL (dB) = 20 log₁₀(d) + 20 log₁₀(f) − 27.55 Each doubling of distance adds 6 dB of loss; each tenfold increase adds 20 dB.

Worked example. 2.4 GHz at 10 m: 20 log(10) + 20 log(2400) − 27.55 = 20 + 67.6 − 27.55 ≈ 60 dB. At 5 GHz the same distance gives about 66.4 dB: about 6 dB more. Link budget: an access point transmits 20 dBm with a 3 dBi antenna; the client has a 2 dBi antenna. At 10 m in free space: 20 + 3 − 60 + 2 = −35 dBm, excellent. Add an internal brick wall and a concrete floor (very roughly 10–25 dB more, depending on construction and frequency) and you may be down near −60 dBm.

Common misconception

"Free space absorbs high frequencies more." Empty space absorbs nothing at any frequency. The frequency term in the formula appears because the formula assumes antennas of fixed gain; a fixed-gain antenna at a higher frequency is physically smaller and so collects less of the passing power (its effective area scales with λ²). Real buildings do absorb more at higher frequencies: walls, glass coatings, people and foliage all hurt 5 GHz and millimetre-wave more than 2.4 GHz or 700 MHz.

Wi-Fi: 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz (and 6 GHz)

2.4 GHz

Longer range and better through walls. Only three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (1, 6 and 11), shared with Bluetooth, many IoT devices and microwave ovens, so it is crowded. Slower top speeds. Most ESP32 boards and many cheap cameras and smart devices only support 2.4 GHz.

5 GHz

Many more channels and wider channels (40, 80, 160 MHz), so much faster and less crowded. Shorter range and weaker through walls and floors. Some channels require radar detection (DFS) and may move if radar is detected. Best for laptops, phones and dense offices.

Newer Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 equipment can also use the 6 GHz band, which Australia has opened in part for Wi-Fi; check ACMA for the current rules. In all these bands, Wi-Fi in Australia operates under ACMA's Low Interference Potential Devices (LIPD) class licence, which sets maximum transmit power. Using illegal high-power amplifiers or out-of-band settings can interfere with other services and is an offence.

On the job

CCTV and access control installers meet radio constantly: wireless bridges to reach a camera on a car-park light pole (often 5 GHz point-to-point with high-gain panel or dish antennas, carefully aligned), Wi-Fi cameras that drop out because they sit at −80 dBm, and IoT sensors in BMS retrofits. A site survey with a phone app or a proper survey tool, measuring dBm at the device location, beats guessing. For anything critical, run a cable.

Mobile networks in brief: 4G and 5G

A mobile network divides an area into cells, each served by a base station with sector antennas (usually three, each covering 120°). Neighbouring cells use frequencies and codes arranged so they do not interfere, and the same frequencies are reused further away. As you move, your phone is handed over from cell to cell.

  • 4G (LTE) uses OFDM: the channel is split into thousands of narrow sub-carriers, each carrying a slice of data, which copes well with reflections in cities.
  • 5G (NR) uses the same basic idea with more flexibility. Low bands (hundreds of MHz) travel far and through buildings: coverage. Mid bands (around 3.4–3.7 GHz in Australia) balance range and speed: most 5G capacity. Millimetre-wave (around 26 GHz) gives very high speeds over short distances with clear line of sight.
  • The physics trade-off is always the same: lower frequency, longer reach and better penetration; higher frequency, more bandwidth (more data) but shorter reach.
In Australia

Australia's 3G mobile networks were switched off during 2023–2024. Many older alarm communicators, lift emergency phones, gate openers and M2M modems used 3G and had to be replaced with 4G or 5G devices. Security and building technicians did much of that replacement work, and some older sites may still have dead 3G devices. NBN's fixed wireless service also uses LTE/5G-type radio technology from towers to an antenna on the customer's roof (C08).

Optical fibre: light trapped in glass

Refraction and Snell's law

Light slows down in a material, to v = c/n. When a wave crosses a boundary at an angle, the part of the wavefront that enters the slower material first slows first, so the wavefront swings round: the ray bends. It bends towards the normal (the line at right angles to the surface) when entering a slower (higher n) material, and away from the normal when entering a faster one. The rule is Snell's law:

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ θ₁ and θ₂ are measured from the normal. n = 1.00 for air, about 1.33 for water, about 1.5 for ordinary glass, and about 1.46–1.47 for the silica glass in fibre.

Total internal reflection and the critical angle

Going from a higher n to a lower n, the ray bends away from the normal. As θ₁ grows, θ₂ reaches 90°: the refracted ray skims along the surface. That θ₁ is the critical angle θc. Beyond it, Snell's law would need sin θ₂ > 1, which is impossible, so no light gets out: all of it is reflected. This is total internal reflection (TIR), and it is genuinely total, far better than any mirror.

sin θc = n₂ / n₁ (only when n₁ > n₂)

Worked example 1: glass to air. n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.00. sin θc = 0.667, θc ≈ 41.8°. This is why a glass prism can act as a perfect mirror at 45°.

Worked example 2: fibre core to cladding. In a typical single-mode fibre, the core is about 1.468 and the cladding about 1.463 (the core has a little germanium added to raise its index). sin θc = 1.463 / 1.468 ≈ 0.9966, θc ≈ 85.3°. So only rays travelling within about 4.7° of the fibre's axis are trapped. That small acceptance is described by the numerical aperture: NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) = √(1.468² − 1.463²) ≈ 0.12, giving an acceptance half-angle in air of about sin⁻¹(0.12) ≈ 7°.

Core, cladding and the two kinds of fibre

A fibre is a thin core of slightly higher-index glass surrounded by cladding of lower-index glass, both 125 µm across in total (about the thickness of a human hair), with a protective plastic coating to 250 µm (or a 900 µm buffer). Light launched along the core stays in by total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary. The cladding matters: the boundary is inside clean glass, so dirt and fingerprints on the outside cannot spoil the reflection.

A mode is one of the allowed patterns in which light can travel down the core. In ray language, each mode is a different zig-zag angle. A multimode core is wide enough for hundreds of modes; a single-mode core is so narrow (only a few wavelengths across) that only one mode can travel.

Attenuation: how much light is lost

Fibre loss is quoted in dB/km. Typical values for modern fibre (indicative; check the cable's datasheet):

Fibre and wavelengthTypical attenuation
Single-mode at 1310 nmabout 0.32–0.35 dB/km (cabled specs often allow up to about 0.4)
Single-mode at 1550 nmabout 0.18–0.22 dB/km (cabled specs often allow up to about 0.3)
Multimode at 850 nmabout 2.5–3.5 dB/km

Why is 1550 nm best? Two effects compete. Rayleigh scattering from tiny density fluctuations frozen into the glass falls as 1/λ⁴, so longer wavelengths scatter far less (the same physics makes the sky blue). But beyond about 1600 nm, the glass starts to absorb infrared as molecular vibrations. The minimum sits near 1550 nm. Older fibre also had a water absorption peak near 1383 nm; modern "low water peak" fibre has almost removed it.

Worked loss budget. A 20 km single-mode link at 1310 nm with 2 connectors and 6 fusion splices: fibre 20 × 0.35 = 7.0 dB; connectors 2 × 0.5 = 1.0 dB; splices 6 × 0.1 = 0.6 dB; total ≈ 8.6 dB. If the transmitter launches −3 dBm, the receiver gets about −11.6 dBm. Compare with the receiver's sensitivity, keep a few dB of margin for repairs and ageing, and the link is good. In an NBN-style passive optical network, a 1:32 optical splitter shares the light 32 ways: ideally 10 log₁₀(32) ≈ 15 dB, about 17 dB in practice. The detailed field version of this is in C09.

Dispersion: why pulses smear

Digital data is a stream of light pulses. If the pulses spread out as they travel, neighbours overlap and the receiver cannot tell 1s from 0s. That spreading is dispersion.

  • Modal dispersion (multimode only): different modes take different zig-zag paths, so some arrive late, like runners taking different routes through a park. "Graded-index" multimode fibre, whose index falls smoothly from centre to edge, speeds up the longer paths to reduce this, but it still limits multimode to hundreds of metres at high speed. Single-mode has only one path, so there is no modal dispersion.
  • Chromatic dispersion: the refractive index depends slightly on wavelength, and every laser pulse contains a small spread of wavelengths, so the colours in a pulse arrive at slightly different times. Standard single-mode fibre has almost zero chromatic dispersion near 1310 nm and about 17 ps per nm per km at 1550 nm. At 10 Gb/s and above over long links, this is managed with narrow-linewidth lasers, compensating fibre or electronics.

The design trade-off in one line: 1310 nm has less dispersion, 1550 nm has less loss. Long-haul systems use 1550 nm and manage the dispersion; erbium-doped fibre amplifiers, which work in the 1550 nm band, boost the light without converting it to electricity.

Safety

The light in telecom fibre is infrared and invisible, and amplified systems can be powerful. Never look into a fibre end, a connector or an optical port, even if you think it is dark; use a power meter or a video inspection scope. Fibre offcuts are tiny glass needles that work into skin and eyes: put them in a marked offcut container, keep food and drink away from the work area, and wear safety glasses when cleaving.

On the job

Most fibre faults are bends and dirt, both explained by this chapter. A tight bend makes rays hit the core boundary more steeply than the critical angle, so light leaks out ("macrobend loss"), worst at 1550 nm. A visual fault locator (a red laser pen for fibre) makes the leak glow visibly at the bend or break. A speck of dust on a 9 µm core blocks a big fraction of it, which is why technicians inspect and clean every connector before mating it. Bend-insensitive fibre is used in NBN drop cables and inside homes to tolerate tighter routing.

Check yourself

In Maxwell's picture, why can an electromagnetic wave travel through empty space with no medium?

Because each field generates the other: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field (Faraday) and a changing electric field creates a magnetic field (Maxwell). The pair regenerates itself as it moves, so it needs no material to carry it. The speed comes out as 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = c.

What is the wavelength at 50 Hz, 100 MHz and 2.4 GHz, and why does the 50 Hz answer matter for wiring?

6000 km, 3 m and 12.5 cm. Because building wiring is tiny compared with 6000 km, mains circuits do not radiate significantly and ordinary circuit theory applies. At GHz frequencies a few centimetres of track is a significant fraction of a wavelength, so PCB tracks act like antennas and transmission lines.

How long should a half-wave dipole for 2.44 GHz be, and a quarter-wave monopole?

λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 2.44 × 10⁹ ≈ 12.3 cm. Half-wave ≈ 6.15 cm × 0.95 ≈ 5.8 cm tip to tip. Quarter-wave monopole over a ground plane ≈ 3 cm.

An access point transmits 20 dBm. What is that in milliwatts, and what does a +3 dB antenna do to the power radiated in its best direction?

20 dBm = 100 mW. A 3 dB gain doubles the power density in the antenna's favoured direction (equivalent to about 200 mW from an isotropic antenna), but only by taking power away from other directions. The antenna does not add energy.

A camera works at 10 m from the access point. Roughly how much weaker is the signal at 40 m in free space?

40 m is two doublings of 10 m. Each doubling adds 6 dB of loss, so about 12 dB weaker (one-sixteenth of the power). Walls would add more.

Calculate the critical angle for glass (n = 1.5) to air, and for a fibre core of 1.468 to cladding of 1.463.

Glass–air: sin θc = 1/1.5, θc ≈ 41.8°. Core–cladding: sin θc = 1.463/1.468 ≈ 0.9966, θc ≈ 85.3°. Only rays within about 4.7° of the fibre axis are guided.

Why do long-distance fibre links use 1550 nm, and why do short multimode links use 850 nm?

Silica glass has its lowest loss near 1550 nm (about 0.2 dB/km), since Rayleigh scattering falls as 1/λ⁴ and infrared absorption only rises beyond about 1600 nm. Short multimode links use 850 nm because cheap VCSEL lasers work there; higher loss (about 3 dB/km) and modal dispersion do not matter over a few hundred metres.

A technician coils a patch lead very tightly and the link fails. Explain with total internal reflection.

In a tight bend, light travelling along the core meets the core–cladding boundary at an angle steeper than the critical angle (measured from the normal, the angle becomes smaller than θc), so it is no longer totally reflected and leaks into the cladding and out. Longer wavelengths (1550 nm) are affected most. Respect the cable's minimum bend radius.
