PLCs and industrial instrumentation

By the end you will understand how a PLC scans, wire a 3-wire sensor to the right kind of input, read and write a start/stop ladder rung, scale a 4–20 mA signal, read a basic P&ID, and know honestly which Australian instrument jobs you can reach with and without an electrical licence.

What a PLC is

A PLC (programmable logic controller) is a small, tough industrial computer that reads switches and sensors, runs a program, and turns motors, valves, lamps and alarms on and off. It replaced cabinets full of relays wired into logic. A PLC lives in a steel control panel, clipped to a DIN rail, runs from 24 V DC, survives heat, vibration and electrical noise, and is expected to run for years without a reboot.

A PLC system has a few parts:

  • CPU: runs the program and talks on networks.
  • Digital inputs (DI): on/off signals from pushbuttons, limit switches, proximity sensors, float switches, contactor auxiliary contacts.
  • Digital outputs (DO): switch relays, contactor coils, solenoid valves, beacons.
  • Analogue inputs (AI) and outputs (AO): usually 4–20 mA signals from transmitters and to control valves and variable speed drives (VSDs).
  • Communications: Ethernet-based industrial networks (EtherNet/IP, PROFINET, Modbus TCP) and older serial ones (Modbus RTU on RS-485). An HMI (operator touch screen) and SCADA (site-wide monitoring software) sit on top.

You will meet PLCs from Rockwell Automation (Allen-Bradley), Siemens, Schneider Electric, Omron, Mitsubishi, Beckhoff, ABB and others. Which one depends on the site and the industry; the ideas are the same everywhere. Large process plants (refineries, LNG, big mineral processing) often use a DCS (distributed control system) instead, which is the same idea built for thousands of analogue loops.

On the job

A new instrument tech rarely writes PLC code on day one. You wire and check I/O, do loop checks (prove that each field device reaches the right PLC input and shows correctly on the HMI), calibrate transmitters, and find faults. Being able to read ladder logic online to see why an output is not on is the skill that makes you useful fast.

The scan cycle

A PLC does not react to inputs the instant they change. It works in a loop called the scan:

Three practical results come from this:

  • Very short pulses can be missed. A 2 ms pulse from a fast sensor may fall between two reads if the scan takes 10 ms. Fast counting uses special high-speed counter inputs.
  • The last rung wins. If two rungs write the same output, only the last one's result reaches the terminal. This "double coil" mistake is a classic fault in other people's code.
  • Order matters. A rung that uses a result calculated further down sees last scan's value.

24 V DC I/O: sinking and sourcing

Industrial control in Australia mostly runs at 24 V DC. That is extra-low voltage: safe to touch and the level you can practise with. A digital input is ON when roughly 11–15 V or more appears across it and OFF below about 5 V (the exact thresholds depend on the input type in IEC 61131-2 and the datasheet). It draws only a few milliamps through an optocoupler (an LED shining on a phototransistor), which isolates the field wiring from the CPU.

The confusing part is which way the current flows. Two words describe it:

  • Sourcing: the device supplies current. It connects the signal wire to +24 V.
  • Sinking: the device gives current a path to 0 V.

A circuit needs one of each. A sourcing sensor must feed a sinking input, and a sinking sensor must feed a sourcing input. 3-wire DC sensors (proximity, photoelectric) come in two flavours named after their output transistor:

  • PNP sensor = sourcing output. When it detects, it switches its black wire to +24 V. Pair it with a sinking input card (input common to 0 V). This is the usual choice in Australia and Europe.
  • NPN sensor = sinking output. When it detects, it switches its black wire to 0 V. Pair it with a sourcing input card (input common to +24 V). Common in some Asian equipment.

Standard 3-wire sensor colours: brown = +24 V, blue = 0 V, black = switched output.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Sinking and sourcing are labels on the sensor, so match the same word on the card." Right: they must be opposite. A sourcing (PNP) sensor needs a sinking input. Some manufacturers also describe cards by what they connect to rather than by current direction, so always look at the wiring diagram in the datasheet and trace where the common goes.

Outputs come as transistor outputs (usually sourcing, switching +24 V to the load, often up to around 0.5 A each) or relay outputs (a dry contact that can switch other voltages). When a DC output drives an inductive load such as a relay coil or solenoid valve, a flyback diode or suppressor across the coil protects the output from the voltage spike when it turns off.

Ladder logic

Ladder logic is the most common PLC language. It is drawn like an electrician's relay drawing: two vertical rails (left = power, right = return) and horizontal rungs between them. "Power" flows left to right through contacts to coils. It is one of the five languages in the IEC 61131-3 standard (with function block diagram, structured text, sequential function chart and the old instruction list).

The start/stop latch (seal-in)

This is the "hello world" of PLCs and the first thing interviewers ask you to draw. A motor must start when Start is pressed, keep running after Start is released, and stop when Stop is pressed or the motor overload trips.

  • Start is a normally open pushbutton (closes when pressed).
  • Stop is a normally closed pushbutton (opens when pressed). In the field it holds the input ON all the time and drops it when pressed.
  • The motor's own output bit is used as a contact in parallel with Start. Once the motor is on, that contact "seals in" the rung.

Here is the key trick: because Stop is wired normally closed, its input bit is 1 when not pressed. So in the program you examine it with a normally open contact. That looks wrong to beginners, but it is right, and it is fail-safe: if the Stop wire breaks or falls off a terminal, the input goes to 0 and the motor stops (or will not start). A normally open Stop button with a broken wire would leave you with a Stop button that does nothing.

Timers and counters in plain words

  • TON (on-delay): the output turns on after its input has been on continuously for the preset time. If the input drops early, it resets. Used for "run the cooling fan 5 s after the motor starts", or "alarm only if low level lasts 10 s" (filtering out splashes).
  • TOF (off-delay): output turns on immediately and stays on for the preset time after the input goes off. Used for "keep the fan running 2 minutes after the heater stops".
  • RTO / retentive timer: keeps its accumulated time when the input goes off; needs a reset. Used for run-hour counting.
  • CTU (up counter): adds 1 each time its input goes from off to on. At the preset, "done" turns on. Used for "after 12 bottles, index the conveyor".
Bench tip

When an output is not doing what you expect, go online with the PLC and look at the rung. The software highlights which contacts are true. Work from the coil back to the left rail and find the first contact that is false. Then go to the field and check why that input is not on: sensor, wiring, fuse, or the 24 V supply. This "coil back to the rail" habit solves most faults.

Free ways to practise

  • OpenPLC: an open-source PLC editor and runtime that supports the IEC 61131-3 languages including ladder. The runtime can run on a PC or a Raspberry Pi, so you can build a real 24 V practice rig. See P17 for the Pi side; any 24 V input to a Pi must go through a proper isolated input module or optocoupler, never straight to the GPIO.
  • CODESYS: a widely used IEC 61131-3 development environment that is free to download and includes simulation. Many PLC brands are built on it. Licences for running it on real hardware are a separate matter.
  • Vendor software: some vendors offer free editions for their small controllers or time-limited trials of their full software. Check the vendor's website for the current offer.

Field instruments

An instrument measures a process variable (pressure, level, flow, temperature) and sends it to the control system. The sensor or element does the measuring; the transmitter turns that into a standard signal, usually 4–20 mA.

RTDs: resistance that rises with temperature

A PT100 is a platinum resistor of 100.0 Ω at 0 °C. Its resistance rises by about 0.385 Ω per °C (α = 0.00385 per °C). Close to room temperature, a straight line is good enough:

R(T) ≈ 100 Ω × (1 + 0.00385 × T) → at 25 °C: 109.6 Ω; at 100 °C: 138.5 Ω T in °C. The exact standard curve (IEC 60751, the Callendar–Van Dusen equation) adds small correction terms; your calibrator or table uses it.

Worked example: why 3-wire RTDs exist. A 2-wire PT100 sits 10 m from the transmitter on 0.5 mm² copper (about 0.036 Ω per metre). The current goes out and back, so 20 m of wire adds about 0.72 Ω. The transmitter reads 0.72 / 0.385 ≈ 1.9 °C too hot. A 3-wire connection adds a third wire so the transmitter can measure and subtract the lead resistance. 4-wire removes it completely and is used for lab-grade accuracy.

Thermocouples: a voltage from two metals

A thermocouple is two different metals joined at the hot end. It produces a small voltage that depends on the temperature difference between the hot junction and the cold end where it connects to copper. A Type K produces roughly 41 µV per °C. Because it measures a difference, the transmitter must measure the terminal temperature too: cold junction compensation. Extension cable must be the matching thermocouple type; ordinary copper in the middle creates new junctions and errors. Thermocouples go hotter than RTDs; RTDs are more accurate and stable at normal process temperatures.

4–20 mA loops, HART and loop calibrators

Most transmitters send their reading as a current between 4 and 20 mA. Current is used because it is the same everywhere in a series loop, so wire resistance and long cable runs do not change the reading, and noise has a harder time disturbing it. The full physics is in the Theory book.

Theory T16 · Signals: sampling, decibels, noise and 4–20 mA loops
Process value = LRV + (I − 4 mA) ÷ 16 mA × (URV − LRV) LRV and URV are the lower and upper range values. Example: a 0–10 bar transmitter sends 12 mA → 0 + (12 − 4)/16 × 10 = 5.0 bar. 4 mA is a "live zero": 0 mA means a broken wire, not zero pressure.

Worked example: voltage budget. Supply 24 V. At 20 mA the 250 Ω resistor drops 20 mA × 250 Ω = 5 V. The transmitter needs, say, 12 V minimum to work. That leaves 24 − 5 − 12 = 7 V for cable and anything else in the loop (a barrier, an indicator). 7 V ÷ 0.020 A = 350 Ω of extra loop resistance allowed. Add too many devices in series and the transmitter "browns out" near the top of the range: readings stop rising at, say, 17 mA.

Fault signalling. Many transmitters follow the NAMUR NE43 recommendation: a normal reading stays between about 3.8 and 20.5 mA, and a current below 3.6 mA or above 21 mA signals a fault (such as a failed sensor). The PLC should alarm on these values instead of showing them as a real measurement.

HART in brief

HART (Highway Addressable Remote Transducer) adds a small digital signal on top of the 4–20 mA current. It uses two tones, 1200 Hz for a 1 and 2200 Hz for a 0, at about ±0.5 mA. The tones average to zero, so the analogue reading is not disturbed. With a HART communicator or a HART-capable calibrator, you can read the instrument's tag, range, units and diagnostics, and re-range it without opening it. HART needs some resistance in the loop (around 250 Ω is typical) for the communicator to "see" the signal. That is one reason the 250 Ω resistor is so common.

Loop calibrators

A loop calibrator is the instrument tech's main tool. Brands you will see include Fluke and Beamex, among others. It can:

  • Measure mA in a live loop (in series, or with a clamp-on mA meter on some models).
  • Source an exact mA value into a PLC input to check scaling and alarms ("inject 12 mA, HMI should show 5.0 bar").
  • Simulate a 2-wire transmitter: the loop supplies power and the calibrator sets the current.
  • Power a transmitter on the bench with its built-in 24 V supply, so you can calibrate it against a pressure or temperature reference.

A calibration check is a 5-point test: 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 % of range, up and down, recording "as found" and "as left" readings. If the error is outside tolerance, adjust zero and span, then repeat.

Bench tip

You can practise all of this at home at 24 V DC: a 24 V plug-pack, a 250 Ω resistor, a multimeter on mA, and a cheap 2-wire 4–20 mA pressure or temperature transmitter from an online supplier. Measure the current at different readings and check it against the formula. Practise on the multimeter's mA range carefully (P03): connect it in series, never across the supply.

4–20 mA carries one value per pair of wires. When an instrument or drive has many values to share, sites use a fieldbus or Modbus instead. See P14 · UART, I²C, SPI, RS-485 and Modbus.

Reading a P&ID

A P&ID (piping and instrumentation diagram) shows the pipes, vessels, pumps, valves and every instrument in a process, with tag numbers. Instrument symbols and tags follow ISA-5.1 style conventions in most of the world, with local and company variations.

First letter (what is measured)Following letters (what it does)
P = pressure, T = temperature, L = level, F = flow, A = analysisT = transmitter, I = indicator, C = controller, E = element (sensor), S = switch, A = alarm, V = valve, H/L = high/low

So PT-101 is pressure transmitter 101; TIC-205 is a temperature indicating controller; LSHH is a level switch high-high (a trip); FV-301 is a flow control valve. The loop number (101, 205) ties every part of one control loop together across drawings.

On the job

During commissioning you get a loop folder: the P&ID, the loop diagram (every terminal from the instrument to the PLC card), the instrument datasheet and a test sheet. You prove each loop end to end: inject or apply a value at the instrument, check it reads correctly on the HMI at 0, 50 and 100 %, check alarms trigger at the right values, and sign the sheet. Hundreds of these make up a project, which is why commissioning is a common way into the industry.

Control panel work

Much instrument and controls work happens at the panel. The ELV (24 V DC) side is where an instrument trainee builds skills. Typical tasks:

  • DIN rail: 35 mm "top hat" rail that PLCs, power supplies, relays, circuit breakers and terminals clip onto.
  • Terminals: screw or spring/push-in terminal blocks (brands such as Phoenix Contact, Weidmüller, Wago), with fused terminals, disconnect terminals for testing, and earth terminals. Field cables land on terminals, never directly on the PLC card.
  • Ferrules: crimped bootlace ferrules on stranded wire ends so no strands escape. Use the right size and a proper ratchet crimper (see P05).
  • Wire numbering: every wire carries a printed marker at both ends matching the drawing, so anyone can trace it years later.
  • Drawings: schematics, panel layouts, terminal schedules, cable schedules and loop diagrams. If the panel and drawing disagree, mark up the drawing ("red-line" it) so the as-built record is correct.
  • Cable screens: instrument cables have an overall screen (shield). It is normally earthed at one end only, usually the panel end, to avoid earth loops. Follow the site standard.
Safety

Industrial panels usually contain 230 V or 400 V supplies as well as 24 V DC. In Australia, work on mains-voltage parts, including terminating a 230 V supply to a power supply or a VSD, is electrical work that only a licensed electrician may do. As a non-licensed worker, you work only on isolated, proven-dead panels under site isolation and permit rules, and only on the ELV circuits. Never open a panel because "it's only 24 V inside": check the drawing and ask. Process plants also have stored energy (pressure, heat, moving machinery, chemicals): instruments are removed only under a permit with the process isolated.

Instrumentation jobs in Australia

Instrument and control technicians work across mining and mineral processing, oil and gas (including LNG), water and wastewater utilities, manufacturing, food and beverage, power generation and pharmaceuticals. Many mining and oil and gas roles are FIFO (fly-in fly-out) on rosters, for example two weeks on, one week off.

The honest picture: dual trade (E&I)

A large share of Australian instrument job ads ask for "E&I" (electrical and instrumentation): a licensed electrician who is also trained in instrumentation. The reason is practical: instruments, analysers and panels are mains-powered or sit next to mains circuits, and an employer wants one person who can legally do the whole job. So the honest advice is:

  • An electrical licence opens more doors, especially in mining and oil and gas. In Australia that normally means an apprenticeship (Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician plus supervised work) and a state licence. Overseas-qualified electricians go through a skills assessment and gap training before getting an Australian licence. See C11.
  • Instrument-only and ELV roles do exist: calibration technicians (often in calibration labs, some NATA-accredited), instrument technicians in water utilities and manufacturing, SCADA and telemetry technicians, commissioning assistants doing loop checks, and controls or PLC technicians. Some employers take trainees and apprentices in instrumentation.
  • Qualifications: Certificate III in Instrumentation and Control, and Certificate IV level instrumentation qualifications, are offered by TAFEs and RTOs in the electrotechnology training package. Check current qualification names and codes on training.gov.au, because they are updated.
In Australia

Electrical licensing is run by each state and territory's regulator (for example Energy Safe Victoria, NSW Fair Trading, Electrical Safety Office in Queensland), and the rules about what an unlicensed person may do differ by state. Work in hazardous areas (places with flammable gas, vapour or dust, common in oil and gas, fuel and some mines) uses explosion-protected (Ex) equipment under the AS/NZS 60079 series and needs specific hazardous-areas competencies, often called "EEHA". High-voltage and mine sites add their own authorisations and inductions. Confirm the current rules with your state electrical regulator and the employer; this changes over time and differs between states.

On the job

A realistic first-year path for someone with an ECE background but no Australian licence: an instrument or calibration technician trainee role, or a commissioning assistant doing loop checks for a contractor, while you work out whether an electrical apprenticeship or licence pathway is right for you. Your strengths to sell: reading drawings, 4–20 mA and HART, multimeter and calibrator skills, PLC basics, Modbus, and tidy documentation.

Typical interview questions

"Draw a start/stop circuit in ladder."

Start (NO) in parallel with a seal-in contact of the motor output, in series with Stop and Overload, to the motor coil. Explain that Stop is a NC button examined with a NO contact so a broken wire stops the motor.

"What is the difference between PNP and NPN sensors?"

PNP switches the output to +24 V (sourcing) and needs a sinking input; NPN switches to 0 V (sinking) and needs a sourcing input. PNP is the usual choice in Australia. Brown +24 V, blue 0 V, black signal.

"Why 4–20 mA and not 0–20 mA or 0–10 V?"

Current is the same around a series loop, so cable resistance does not change the reading and noise pickup is lower. The 4 mA live zero tells you a broken wire (0 mA) from a real zero reading, and it powers 2-wire transmitters.

"A 0–100 °C transmitter reads 8 mA. What is the temperature?"

(8 − 4)/16 × 100 = 25 °C.

"The HMI shows a level stuck at the bottom of range. How do you fault-find?"

Check whether it shows a fault value. Measure loop current at the panel: 0 mA means an open loop or no supply (check fuse, 24 V, terminals); about 4 mA means the transmitter thinks the level is low (check the instrument, isolation valves, impulse lines, configuration). Compare with a local gauge or sight glass. Follow permits before touching the process.

"What does a 3-wire RTD connection fix?"

It lets the transmitter measure and cancel the lead resistance, which would otherwise read as extra temperature.

"Do you hold an electrical licence? What can you do without one?"

Be honest. Say what ELV and instrument work you can do, that you never work on mains, and what your plan is for licensing if relevant.

Check yourself

A part passes a sensor in 3 ms. The PLC scan is 12 ms. Why might the count be wrong, and what is the fix?

The input is only read once per scan, so a 3 ms pulse can begin and end between two reads and be missed. Use a high-speed counter input (which counts in hardware), slow the conveyor, or use a sensor setting that stretches the pulse.

You wire a PNP proximity sensor to an input card whose common is connected to +24 V. What happens?

Nothing turns on. The sensor switches its output to +24 V, and the input's other end is also at +24 V, so there is no voltage across the input and no current. A PNP (sourcing) sensor needs a sinking input with the common at 0 V.

In the latch rung, why is the Stop button examined with a normally open contact?

The Stop button is wired normally closed, so its input is 1 when not pressed. An NO contact passes power when the bit is 1, so the rung is true until Stop is pressed. If the wire breaks, the input goes to 0 and the motor stops: fail-safe.

A PT100 reads 119.25 Ω. Roughly what temperature is it?

(119.25 − 100) / 0.385 ≈ 50 °C. Using the linear formula is fine for a field check near normal temperatures.

A −1 to 9 bar transmitter sends 6 mA. What pressure is that?

Span is 10 bar. −1 + (6 − 4)/16 × 10 = −1 + 1.25 = 0.25 bar.

A loop has a 24 V supply, a 250 Ω input resistor and a transmitter needing 12 V minimum. How much more loop resistance can you add before it fails at 20 mA?

At 20 mA the resistor drops 5 V. 24 − 5 − 12 = 7 V spare. 7 V / 0.02 A = 350 Ω of extra resistance (cable, barriers, indicators combined).

The PLC shows 2.1 mA on an analogue input. Is that a reading of "below zero"?

No. Below about 3.6 mA is a fault indication (NE43-style): a failed transmitter, open circuit or lost power. The PLC should raise a fault alarm, not display a value.

What does TIC-205 on a P&ID mean, and where would you expect to find it?

Temperature Indicating Controller, loop 205. If its circle has a horizontal line through it, it is in the control room or control system (PLC/DCS), not in the field.

  • I can draw and explain the start/stop latch, including why Stop is wired NC.
  • I can use TON, TOF and CTU instructions in a small program in OpenPLC or CODESYS.
  • I can convert a PT100 resistance to temperature and explain 2-, 3- and 4-wire RTDs.
  • I can scale 4–20 mA to engineering units, do a loop voltage budget and recognise fault currents.
