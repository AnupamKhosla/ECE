BMS fundamentals: what a building management system does

A building management system (BMS) is the computer network that runs a building's plant: air conditioning, pumps, lights, meters and more. By the end of this chapter you will know what it controls, how it is built in layers, what the four point types are, and what a BMS technician actually does on a working day in Australia.

What a BMS is (and what it is not)

Walk into the plant room of an office tower in Sydney or Melbourne. You will see big fans, pumps, pipes and a grey steel cabinet full of small boxes with green terminal blocks. Those boxes are controllers. They read sensors, run control programs, and drive valves, dampers and motors. All of them talk over a network to a server, and a person can see the whole building on a screen. That whole arrangement is the BMS.

You will hear several names for the same thing:

  • BMS – building management system. The most common term in Australia.
  • BMCS – building management and control system. Used in many Australian specifications.
  • BAS – building automation system. Common in American documents.
  • DDC – direct digital control. Strictly, this means a microcontroller runs the control loop (instead of an old pneumatic or analogue controller). People say "DDC controller" or "DDC panel".

A BMS does three jobs. It controls (keeps a room at 22.5 °C, keeps duct pressure at 250 Pa). It monitors (records temperatures and energy, raises alarms). And it schedules (turns plant on before people arrive and off when they leave). A building can run without a BMS, but it will waste energy, and nobody will know when something breaks until a tenant complains.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A BMS is a big computer that switches the air conditioner on and off."
Right: the intelligence is spread out. Each field controller runs its own control loops, even if the server is turned off. The server (front end) is mainly a window: graphics, alarms, trends and schedules. If the network fails, a well-designed building keeps running on the controllers' local programs. This is why technicians care so much about what is in each controller, not just what the screen shows.

What a BMS controls

The biggest part of almost every BMS is HVAC (heating, ventilation and air conditioning), because it uses the most energy and has the most moving parts. But a modern BMS touches nearly every service in the building.

HVAC plant

Chillers, cooling towers, boilers or heat pumps, air handling units (AHUs), VAV boxes, fan coil units, exhaust fans, car park fans. Chapter C03 explains the plant itself.

Lighting

Time schedules for common areas, after-hours lighting requests, links to DALI or KNX lighting controllers. The BMS often just sends "on/off by zone" to a separate lighting system.

Metering

Electricity, gas, water and thermal (BTU) meters, usually over Modbus or pulse outputs. Energy data feeds NABERS ratings and tenant billing.

Hydraulics and pumps

Domestic water pumps, sump pumps and pit level alarms, hot water plant, rainwater tanks. Often "monitor only": the BMS watches run status and faults.

Lift and fire interfaces

The BMS does not control lifts or fire systems. It receives status signals (lift fault, fire alarm) and reacts, for example by shutting AHUs in fire mode. See C04.

Generators and power

Standby generator running/fault, fuel level, mains fail, UPS alarms, power quality from switchboard meters. Again mostly monitoring.

Bench tip

Learn the two words control and monitor. On drawings and points lists, a system marked "monitor only" means the BMS can see it but must never command it. Writing to a monitor-only point from the front end, even by mistake, is a serious error on site.

The architecture: four levels

Almost every BMS, whatever the brand, is built in the same layers. Learn this picture and you can walk into any plant room and find your way.

Level 1 · Field devices

These are the physical sensors and actuators: temperature sensors, pressure switches, valve and damper actuators, variable speed drives (VSDs), current switches, meters. They are wired with copper to a controller's input/output (I/O) terminals. Most run on extra-low voltage: 24 V AC for actuator power, and 0–10 V, 4–20 mA or a resistance for signals.

Level 2 · Field controllers (I/O and DDC)

A field controller has a microcontroller, a set of I/O terminals and a program. A plant controller for an AHU might have 20–40 points. A VAV controller or FCU controller is small (often 6–12 points) and mounted on the box in the ceiling. Some systems also use I/O modules: a box of terminals with no program of its own, which reports to a nearby controller.

Level 3 · Network (supervisory) controllers

These sit between the field bus and the IP network. They collect data from many field controllers, run building-wide logic (for example "which chiller should run"), store short trend logs, and translate between protocols. You will hear brand names for them, such as a JACE (Niagara), an NAE or SNE (Metasys), or a PXC (Desigo). Chapter C02 covers the protocols they speak.

Level 4 · Front end (head end) and workstations

The front end is server software that holds the graphics, the alarm database, long-term trend storage, user accounts and schedules. Operators see it on a workstation in the facility manager's office, or in a web browser. Remote access is usually through the owner's VPN, because a BMS that is reachable from the open internet is a known cyber-security risk.

On the job

On your first day you will be asked "which controller is that point on?" more than any other question. Get used to tracing a point from the graphic (level 4), to the network controller (level 3), to the field controller and its terminal number (level 2), to the device and cable in the ceiling (level 1). Good technicians move up and down these levels quickly.

Points: AI, AO, DI, DO

A point is one piece of information the BMS knows about. The physical ones are wired to a terminal. There are exactly four kinds, made from two questions: Does the signal come in or go out? and Is it a range of values or just on/off?

BACnet calls the digital ones "binary" (BI, BO). Some brands say UI ("universal input", which can be set as AI or DI in software) or UIO (universal input/output). The physics does not change.

AI example: a 10 kΩ NTC room sensor

The white plastic box on an office wall is often just a thermistor: a resistor whose resistance falls as it gets warmer. NTC means "negative temperature coefficient". "10 kΩ" means it reads 10 kΩ at 25 °C. The controller puts a small, known voltage across it (through a fixed resistor, as a voltage divider), measures the voltage with its ADC, works out the resistance, and converts that to °C with a curve stored in its firmware.

R(T) = R25 · exp[ β (1/T − 1/298.15) ] R25 = 10 kΩ, β is the thermistor's constant (about 3,900–4,000 K for common 10 kΩ types), T is in kelvin. With β = 3,950: at 22 °C (295.15 K) R ≈ 11.4 kΩ; at 18 °C R ≈ 13.8 kΩ; at 30 °C R ≈ 8.0 kΩ. So a 1 °C change is roughly a 4–5 % change in resistance: easy to measure with a meter.

There are several "10 kΩ" curves in the building industry (often called Type II and Type III), and other sensors such as 1 kΩ Ni or PT1000. If the controller is set to the wrong curve, it will read a few degrees wrong at the ends of the range even though it looks right near 25 °C. That is a classic commissioning fault.

AO example: a 0–10 V damper actuator

A damper actuator is a small geared motor with a position controller inside. It is powered by 24 V AC and takes a control signal: 0 V means 0 % (shut), 10 V means 100 % (open), 4.5 V means 45 %. Some actuators use 2–10 V so that 0 V can be detected as a broken wire, and some give back a 2–10 V feedback signal of their real position (wired to an AI). Valve actuators on chilled water and heating coils work the same way. A VSD speed reference is also an AO: 0–10 V for 0–50 Hz.

DI example: run status from a current switch or DP switch

A digital input is a contact that the controller sees as open or closed. The controller supplies a small wetting voltage (often 5–24 V DC, a few mA) and looks for current. Two common ways to prove a fan is really running:

  • Current switch: a ring (current transformer) that a motor cable passes through, with a contact that closes when the current is above a set threshold. It proves the motor is drawing current. If adjusted well, it can also catch a broken belt, because an unloaded motor draws less current.
  • Differential pressure (DP) switch: two small tubes measure the pressure across the fan. When the fan moves air, the pressure difference closes the contact. It proves airflow, not just a spinning motor.

DO example: start/stop via a relay output

The controller closes a small relay contact. That contact is wired into the control circuit of a motor starter or the "run" input of a VSD in the mechanical services switchboard (MSSB). The MSSB has a Hand-Off-Auto switch for each motor: only in "Auto" does the BMS have control.

Common misconception

Wrong: "If the BMS shows the fan command ON, the fan is running."
Right: a command is only a request. The status point tells you what really happened. The MSSB switch might be in Off, the drive might have tripped, the belt might be broken. Every good BMS compares command and status and raises a command fail (or "status mismatch") alarm after a delay, often 30–60 seconds, if they disagree.

Safety

Controller panels and the MSSB usually also contain 230 V or 400 V: the 230/24 V transformer, motor circuits and contactor coils. In Australia only a licensed electrician may work on those parts. As a learner, you work only on the ELV (24 V and signal) side, only with your employer's isolation procedure, and never open an MSSB. If a DO contact switches a 230 V coil, that circuit is mains work even though the controller side is ELV.

A real points list: one air handling unit

Every BMS job starts and ends with the points list (also called an I/O schedule). It is a spreadsheet: one row per point, with name, type, device, signal and purpose. The engineer uses it to choose controllers, the electrician uses it to wire, the programmer uses it to build the database, and you use it to commission. An engineer would choose a controller with spare capacity (usually 10–20 % spare is specified), for example one with 8 universal inputs and 6 outputs plus a few spare. On top of these, the controller holds software points (also called virtual or value points): the supply air setpoint, the occupancy schedule, the enable, alarm limits and calculated values. A large office building can have tens of thousands of points in total.

Bench tip

Point names follow a naming standard set by the project or the building owner, something like Site_Building_Level_Equipment_Point. Newer projects may also add semantic tags (for example Project Haystack or Brick tags) so analytics software can tell that a point is a "supply air temperature sensor" without reading the name. Always follow the site standard. Never invent your own names on a live site.

What the BMS does with points

Setpoints and control loops

A setpoint is the target value: 22.5 °C room temperature, 13 °C supply air, 250 Pa duct pressure. A control loop compares the measured value (an AI) with the setpoint and moves an output (an AO) to reduce the difference. Most loops are PI or PID loops, which C03 lets you tune in a simulation. Setpoints are often reset by other logic. For example, supply air setpoint might rise from 13 °C to 16 °C when all zones are cool, which saves chiller energy.

Schedules

A schedule is a weekly timetable plus a calendar of holidays: "Mon–Fri 07:00–18:00 occupied". Plant can start earlier with optimum start, which learns how long the building takes to reach temperature. Tenants can press an after-hours button (or use an app) to run their floor for an extra hour or two, which is often billed back to them.

Alarms

An alarm is a condition the system should tell a person about. Good alarms have a limit, a deadband (hysteresis) and a delay, so they do not chatter on and off. Example: "Room temp high: above 26 °C for 10 minutes; return to normal below 25 °C." Alarms have priorities (critical, urgent, maintenance) and are sent to the front end, email or SMS, and sometimes to a monitoring company. Too many unimportant alarms train people to ignore all alarms; this is called alarm fatigue, and cleaning up alarm settings is real, valued work.

Trend logs

A trend log records a point's value over time, either every fixed interval (say every 15 minutes) or on change of value (COV, for example every time it moves by 0.2 °C). Trends are how you diagnose faults after the fact.

Samples per year = (60 / 15) × 24 × 365 = 35,040 per point Worked example: 2,000 points logged every 15 minutes is about 70 million samples per year. At roughly 10–20 bytes per sample in a database that is around 1 GB per year: small for a server, but too much for a field controller, which is why controllers keep only a short buffer and the front end keeps the history.

In Australia

Many buildings are serviced not by the manufacturer but by independent systems integrators, who may work across several platforms. Big facility-management companies also employ BMS techs. When you read a job ad, notice whether it is installation and commissioning (project work, new buildings, often on construction sites, needs a White Card) or service (existing buildings, a van and a run of regular sites).

A day in the life of a BMS service technician

Here is a realistic day for a service tech in a capital city. Details differ, but the pattern is common.

  1. 07:00 · Sign in. Arrive at an office building. Sign in at the loading dock or with security, collect keys or an access card, and check the site's induction and permit system. Read the logbook for notes from the last visit.

  2. 07:20 · Alarm review. Log in to the front end. Sort the alarm list: what is new, what is repeating, what is long-standing. Note anything critical (chiller fault, pump failure, communication loss to a controller).

  3. 08:00 · Preventive maintenance. Work through the maintenance schedule: check a sample of sensors against a calibrated reference thermometer, stroke valves and dampers from 0 to 100 % and watch them move, check that schedules and holiday calendars are right, back up controller programs.

  4. 10:30 · A complaint. The facility manager says level 5 is "freezing". On the front end, you see the zone at 20.1 °C, setpoint 22.5 °C, VAV damper at minimum, reheat valve commanded 100 %. You go to the ceiling: the reheat valve actuator is not moving. Measure 24 V AC at its supply terminals: present. Measure the control signal: 9.8 V DC, so the controller is doing its job. The actuator has failed. You quote a replacement.

  5. 13:00 · Second site. A cool room alarm keeps clearing itself. The trend shows the temperature crossing the limit for 30 seconds every defrost cycle. You add a delay to the alarm so it only triggers if the condition lasts 15 minutes, with the client's agreement, and record the change.

  6. 15:30 · Paperwork. Write up each job in the company's service app or CMMS (computerised maintenance management system): what you found, what you did, parts used, recommendations. Good notes are part of the product the client pays for.

On the job

The 10:30 diagnosis is the core skill employers test: check the command, check the signal, check the power, check the device, in that order, with a multimeter. You learned exactly these measurements (AC volts, DC volts, resistance) in Practice P03. To check a 10 kΩ sensor, disconnect it from the controller first (the controller's own measuring current will confuse your meter), measure its resistance, and compare with the curve: about 11.4 kΩ at 22 °C for a β ≈ 3,950 type.

How people get into BMS work

Entry roles

  • BMS apprentice or trainee. Often an electrical apprenticeship with a controls company, so you finish as a licensed electrician who specialises in controls. Some companies run their own trainee programs.
  • Controls technician / BMS installer. Wiring field devices (ELV), terminating cables, labelling, point-to-point checks under supervision.
  • BMS service technician. The role described above. Usually requires some experience, a driver's licence and a clean record for site access.
  • Commissioning technician, BMS programmer, front-end/graphics engineer. Later steps. Programmers write the control logic; graphics engineers build the screens.

Employers value an electronics or instrumentation background, because the work is about signals, sensors, networks and fault-finding, not heavy power. Many ads also ask for networking basics (IP addresses, subnets, VLANs), so Practice P19 is directly useful.

In Australia

Things you will see in ads: a White Card (general construction induction, needed on construction sites), a driver's licence, sometimes a police check, and "electrical licence preferred" or "required". Some BMS roles hire people without an electrical licence for ELV wiring, programming and commissioning, with electricians doing the mains parts; others require the licence. Electrical licensing is run by each state (for example NSW Fair Trading, Energy Safe Victoria, and the Electrical Safety Office in Queensland). Rules change and differ by state, so confirm with the regulator. Vendor courses (for example Niagara certification) are usually paid for by the employer. Chapter C11 covers licences in detail.

Typical interview questions

"What are the four point types? Give an example of each."

AI: a 10 kΩ room sensor. AO: a 0–10 V valve actuator. DI: a DP switch proving a fan. DO: a relay contact starting a pump. Then mention that BACnet calls digital points binary.

"A fan shows command ON but status OFF. What do you check?"

Check the Hand-Off-Auto switch on the MSSB is in Auto, check the VSD for a fault or trip, check whether the fire trip or a safety interlock is active, then check the status device: is the DP switch tube blocked or the current switch threshold set too high? Check that the DO actually closes (measure across the relay contact with the circuit isolated, or watch the relay LED). Work from the command outwards.

"How would you check if a temperature sensor is reading correctly?"

Compare the BMS reading with a calibrated reference thermometer at the sensor. If it differs, disconnect the sensor and measure its resistance, compare with the sensor curve for the actual temperature, and check the controller is configured for the right sensor type. Check for a loose terminal or a long cable run adding resistance.

"What is the difference between 0–10 V and 4–20 mA?"

0–10 V is simple and cheap but voltage drops and noise on long cables shift the reading. 4–20 mA is a current loop: the same current flows everywhere in a series loop, so cable resistance does not change the reading, and 0 mA means a broken wire. See Theory T16.

"What does a network controller do?"

It connects field controllers on RS-485 trunks to the IP network, runs building-wide logic, stores trends and alarms locally, and translates protocols (for example Modbus meters into BACnet points).

"Why would an alarm keep coming and going, and how would you fix it?"

The value is sitting near the limit, or there is a short, normal event (defrost, start-up). Add a deadband and a time delay, or disable the alarm during known events, with the client's approval. Never just disable an alarm without recording why.

Try this

Build a four-point "mini BMS" on your bench: an Arduino reads a 10 kΩ NTC (AI) and a push-button (DI), dims an LED with PWM (AO), and switches a relay module (DO) when the temperature passes a setpoint with 1 °C of hysteresis. Print the values over serial every 5 seconds: that is your trend log. Add an "alarm" if the temperature stays above a limit for 30 seconds. Keep it all on USB power or a 12 V plug-pack. Labs: P11, P12, P13.

Check yourself

A CO₂ sensor outputs 0–10 V for 0–2,000 ppm. The BMS shows 3.6 V on the input. What is the CO₂ level, and what point type is it?

2,000 ppm / 10 V = 200 ppm per volt, so 3.6 V × 200 = 720 ppm. It is an AI: a continuous value coming in.

Why do engineers prove a supply fan with a DP switch instead of just trusting the start command?

The command only says what the BMS asked for. The DP switch proves air is actually moving. The fan could be off at the MSSB, tripped, or have a broken belt. Comparing command with status lets the BMS raise a command-fail alarm.

The front end server is switched off for an upgrade. Do the AHUs stop?

No, not in a well-designed system. Control loops and schedules run in the field and network controllers. You lose graphics, alarm notification and long-term trends while it is off, so the facility team must be told, but the plant keeps running.

You measure a disconnected 10 kΩ NTC room sensor and read 8.0 kΩ. The room feels warm. Is the sensor likely fine?

For a β ≈ 3,950 thermistor, 8.0 kΩ is about 30 °C. If a reference thermometer also reads about 30 °C, the sensor is fine and the room really is hot (look at the cooling). If the reference reads 22 °C, the sensor or its curve is wrong.

How many DOs does a 3-speed fan coil unit need for its fan, and why?

Three: one relay per speed (low, medium, high), with logic so only one is on at a time. Each speed is an on/off command going out.

An AHU points list has 6 AI, 4 AO, 3 DI and 1 DO. Why would the engineer not choose a controller with exactly 14 I/O?

Specifications usually require spare I/O (often 10–20 %) for future changes and for failed terminals. Also some inputs must be of a certain type, so the mix matters as well as the total.

What three settings stop a high-temperature alarm from chattering on and off?

The limit itself, a deadband (it must fall some way below the limit before returning to normal) and a time delay (the condition must last for a set time before the alarm is raised).
