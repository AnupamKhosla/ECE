The NBN and access networks

By the end you will be able to explain each NBN access technology from the exchange to the wall, say why a long copper line is slow, and know which field roles exist, what tickets they ask for, and what an interviewer will test you on.

Time 60 min readLevel Beginner to job-readyBuilds on T10, T16, C07Roles Installer, splicer, network tech

What the NBN is, in one page

The NBN (National Broadband Network) is Australia's wholesale broadband network. It is owned by a government business, NBN Co. NBN Co does not sell internet to homes. It sells wholesale access to RSPs (retail service providers, the internet companies a customer pays each month). The RSP buys capacity from NBN Co at a POI (point of interconnect), a handful of big sites around the country where the RSP's own network meets the NBN.

Between the POI and the customer is the access network. People also call it the "last mile". This is the part that differs from street to street. Your house might have fibre to the wall, or a copper phone line from a street cabinet, or a coaxial cable from the old pay-TV network, or an antenna pointed at a tower, or a dish pointed at a satellite. The access network is where most of the field work happens, so it is where most of the field jobs are.

Common misconception

Wrong: "NBN" is one technology, and "NBN speed" is a property of the network. Right: the NBN is a mix of six access technologies. The speed a customer can get depends on the technology at their address, the plan they buy, and, for copper, the length and condition of their line. Two houses on the same street can have different technologies.

FTTP: fibre to the premises (GPON)

FTTP means the fibre goes all the way into the building. Most NBN FTTP uses GPON (Gigabit Passive Optical Network). "Passive" is the key word. Between the local site and the house there is no powered equipment. There are only glass fibres, joints and passive optical splitters, which are small glass chips that divide light from one fibre into many.

The parts

  • OLT (optical line terminal). A rack of electronics at the local NBN site (you will hear it called a FAN site, fibre access node). One OLT port serves one PON.
  • Feeder and distribution fibre. Cables with many fibres running from the site out to the streets.
  • Splitter. Typically in a street cabinet or hub (you may hear "FDH", fibre distribution hub). One fibre in, many out, commonly up to 32 customers per PON.
  • Local fibre and drop. Through pits and conduits, or on poles, to the building. The drop ends at a small weatherproof box on the outside wall (often called the PCD, premises connection device).
  • Connection box (NTD). The ONT (optical network terminal) inside. It turns light into Ethernet. The customer's router plugs into a UNI-D port (an Ethernet port on the box).

How 32 houses share one fibre

Downstream (to the customer), GPON runs at about 2.5 Gb/s on 1490 nm light. The splitter sends a copy of everything to every house. Each ONT only keeps the frames addressed to it, and the traffic is encrypted, so neighbours cannot read each other's data. Upstream (from the customer), the line runs at about 1.25 Gb/s on 1310 nm. If two ONTs transmitted at the same moment their light would add together in the splitter and garble both. So the OLT gives each ONT tiny time slots to transmit in. This is TDMA (time division multiple access).

The price of a splitter is light loss. Splitting light 32 ways means each output gets about 1/32 of the power, which is 10·log10(32) ≈ 15 dB. Real splitters add excess loss, so a 1:32 splitter is commonly budgeted at around 17 dB. You will do this sum properly in the next chapter.

Ideal split loss = 10 · log10(N) = 10 · log10(32) ≈ 15.1 dB → budget ≈ 17 dB with excess loss N is the number of outputs. Each doubling of N costs about 3 dB. The extra ~2 dB covers real-world imperfections in the splitter and its joints.

Newer standards such as XGS-PON run at about 10 Gb/s and use different wavelengths, so they can share the same fibre and splitters with GPON. Network owners around the world, including in Australia, are upgrading PON networks this way. The field skills (clean, splice, test light levels) stay the same.

On the job

An FTTP install tech checks the light level at the premises before and after fitting the connection box. Your employer will give you the pass window they use. As a rough guide, GPON receivers work between about −8 dBm and −27 dBm. A reading of "no light" or a level far below the window usually means a dirty connector, a tight bend, a bad splice, or the wrong fibre in the pit. Cleaning and re-testing fixes a surprising share of these.

FTTC: fibre to the curb

FTTC takes fibre to a pit right outside the premises. In the pit sits a small sealed box called a DPU (distribution point unit). The DPU converts light to a VDSL2 signal and sends it over the existing short copper lead-in (often tens of metres) into the building. The copper is short, so the speed is much better than FTTN.

The clever part is power. A pit has no mains supply. So the DPU is reverse powered: the NCD (NBN connection device) inside the house, which is plugged into a power point, sends DC power back up the copper pair to run the DPU. If the customer unplugs the NCD and their neighbour on the same DPU does not, the DPU can still run; if nobody powers it, it is dark. This is why "the customer turned the box off" is a real FTTC fault cause.

Bench tip

Because the NCD puts DC on the pair, a phone-line tester or a meter on the line will show a voltage that a plain copper phone line would not have. Do not assume every voltage on a pair is a fault. Know which technology you are on before you measure. Practise safe meter use first in P03 Multimeter mastery.

FTTN: fibre to the node

FTTN takes fibre to a powered street node cabinet. The node holds VDSL2 line cards (a DSLAM). From there, the signal runs over the old telephone copper to each premises. That copper can be a few hundred metres or well over a kilometre long. The customer supplies a VDSL2 modem.

FTTN speed falls with distance. A house 200 m from the node and a house 1.2 km from the node on the same plan can see very different speeds. Copper joints in pits, corrosion, water ingress, old in-house wiring and extra phone sockets all make it worse. Much of FTTN field work is copper fault-finding: testing pairs, finding bad joints, and swapping to a better pair.

HFC: hybrid fibre-coaxial

HFC reuses the coaxial cable networks first built for pay TV. Fibre runs to an optical node serving a neighbourhood. From there, coaxial cable runs down the street, with amplifiers to make up for loss and taps where each house's drop cable connects. The data system is DOCSIS (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification). At the network end a CMTS (cable modem termination system) talks to the cable modem NTD in each home. DOCSIS 3.0 bonds several TV-sized channels together. DOCSIS 3.1 uses OFDM (many narrow sub-carriers, like VDSL2 and Wi-Fi) for higher speed.

All homes on one node share the same coax spectrum, like GPON shares a fibre. Coax is shielded, so speed barely depends on distance (amplifiers top the signal up). The classic HFC enemy is ingress: outside radio noise leaking in through a loose or corroded F-connector, a cheap splitter or a damaged drop. Noise that enters at one house travels upstream and can upset the whole node. Tightening, replacing and weatherproofing connectors is real, valued work.

Fixed Wireless and satellite

Fixed Wireless

Where houses are too spread out for cables, Fixed Wireless uses mobile-network radio technology (LTE, and 5G-based upgrades) from a tower to a fixed outdoor antenna on the house. "Fixed" means the antenna does not move and points at one particular tower. The installer mounts the antenna, runs a cable inside to the NTD, and aligns the antenna for the best signal readings. Signal falls with distance, trees and hills get in the way, and a busy tower is shared by all its users.

Satellite

For the most remote premises, the NBN uses geostationary satellites (the service is called Sky Muster). A geostationary satellite sits about 35,786 km above the equator and appears fixed in the sky, so a dish can be pointed once and left. The installer mounts the dish, points it accurately, and connects the NTD.

Minimum delay for a request and reply = 4 legs × 35,786 km ÷ 300,000 km/s ≈ 0.48 s Your request goes up to the satellite and down to the ground station (2 legs). The reply comes back up and down (2 more legs). That is physics, not a slow network. Real round-trip times are higher once processing is added. Low Earth orbit services, which fly much lower, have much lower delay for this reason.
On the job

Wireless and satellite installs are roof jobs. Expect ladders, roof edges, heat, and long drives in regional areas. Employers will ask about working-at-heights training, a clean driving record, and whether you are comfortable alone on rural sites.

Why copper gets slow with length

VDSL2 sends data on thousands of small radio-frequency carriers called tones, spaced 4.3125 kHz apart, up to about 17.7 MHz (the "17a" profile). Each tone carries a number of bits per symbol, from 0 up to 15. The modem measures each tone's signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) when the line trains, then loads more bits onto clean tones and none onto noisy ones. Line speed is the sum over all tones. Two physical effects decide that SNR.

1. Attenuation grows with length and with frequency

A twisted pair loses signal as heat in the copper and the insulation. Loss in decibels grows in proportion to length: twice the length means twice the dB. It also grows with frequency, roughly with √f, because at high frequency the current crowds to the outside skin of the wire (the skin effect), which raises the resistance. So a long line does not just lose a bit of everything. It loses the high tones completely. The high tones are where most of the bits live.

2. Crosstalk from neighbouring pairs

Phone cables hold dozens to hundreds of pairs packed together. Each pair's signal couples a little into its neighbours, like hearing the conversation through a thin wall. FEXT (far-end crosstalk) is the main VDSL2 problem: signals from other modems on the same cable leak into your pair and arrive at your receiver as noise. Crosstalk coupling grows with frequency, so it also hits the high tones hardest. Vectoring is a technique where the node knows what every pair is sending, works out the leakage, and pre-cancels it. It can remove most of the crosstalk, which is why vectored lines get much closer to their attenuation-only limit.

Why it works

Bits per tone follow Shannon's idea: capacity ≈ log2(1 + SNR). Every 3 dB more SNR buys about one extra bit on that tone. Attenuation lowers the signal; crosstalk raises the noise; both shrink the SNR. Decibels, SNR and noise are explained in the Theory book, and why loss grows with frequency in the waves chapter.

Who does the work

NBN Co owns the network, but most physical field work is done by contractors. NBN Co engages large delivery partners (big construction, telecom-services and engineering firms) to build and maintain areas of the network. Those partners often sub-contract to smaller companies and to individual technicians, some of whom work as sole traders with their own ABN (Australian Business Number) and vehicle. Some roles are direct employees with a company vehicle and tools; others are paid per job. Ask about this in every interview, because it changes your income, insurance and tax.

Installation technician

Connects new premises: hauls or fits the lead-in, mounts the outside box and the connection box or NCD, tests, activates and explains to the customer. Lots of customer contact.

Fibre splicer / jointer

Joins fibres in pits, cabinets and joint enclosures with a fusion splicer, then tests with a power meter or OTDR. Methodical, indoor-and-outdoor, high skill. See C09.

Cable hauler / civil crew

Pulls cable through conduits, rods blocked ducts, works with pits, poles and trenches. Physical. A common first job that leads to splicing.

Network / assurance technician

Fixes faults: no light, low sync, dropouts, noisy HFC. Uses testers to find where the problem is, then repairs or escalates.

Copper jointer

Finds and fixes faults on copper pairs for FTTN and FTTC: wet joints, corrosion, split pairs, using pair testers and TDRs.

Mobile network field tech

Works for or with the mobile carriers: base station equipment, antennas, fibre to sites, power and batteries, tower work for riggers. Also in-building coverage.

An FTTP install, step by step

This is a typical flow. Your employer's procedure, forms and apps come first.

  1. Read the work order. Address, technology, what is already built, any notes. Check the site for hazards before you unload: dogs, traffic, overhead power lines, asbestos warnings on older pits.

  2. Meet the customer and agree the box location. Show where the outside box and the inside connection box will go, and how the cable will run. Agree before drilling. Note any existing damage.

  3. Make the pit safe. Barricade, lift the lid with proper lifting keys and posture, check for water, gas and pests. Some pits may need a gas test or are treated as confined spaces; follow your employer's assessment.

  4. Haul or fit the lead-in. Rod the conduit to the house, pull the drop fibre (or fit a pre-connectorised drop). Respect the minimum bend radius. Do not kink it.

  5. Fit the outside box and run the fibre inside. Drill neatly, seal the hole, use proper clips. Inside, mount the connection box near a power point.

  6. Join and clean. Splice or connect as the job requires. Inspect every connector end-face with a scope and clean it before mating.

  7. Test. Measure received light with an optical power meter and record it. Power up, check the ONT lights, confirm the service activates.

  8. Hand over and tidy. Show the customer which port the router uses. Clean up, close the pit, photograph the work if required, close the job in the app.

Safety

Never look into a fibre or a connector, even if you think it is dark. Network lasers are infrared and invisible. Fibre glass offcuts can go into your skin and eyes; collect them in a marked container. The connection box power supply plugs into a normal power point, which is fine; but never open, extend or rewire mains wiring. Mains work in Australia is for licensed electricians only.

In Australia

Cabling registration is regulated by the ACMA (Australian Communications and Media Authority) under the cabling provider rules, and is issued through ACMA-accredited registrars. Work health and safety tickets (White Card, high-risk work licences such as larger boom EWPs) are regulated by your state's WHS regulator, for example SafeWork NSW or WorkSafe Victoria. Mobile repeaters (boosters) need the carrier's permission in Australia; unauthorised ones are illegal because they interfere with the network. These rules change and some differ by state: confirm the current requirements with the ACMA, your state regulator and the employer before you pay for any course.

Formal training: TAFEs and private RTOs (registered training organisations) run telecommunications cabling and network courses at Certificate II–IV level, and the cabling registration courses. Many delivery partners also train new starters in-house. A realistic entry route is: White Card + registration-ready cabling course + driver's licence, then a hauler or trainee installer role, then splicing and testing as you prove yourself. For the full licence picture see C11 Licences, tickets and pathways.

In-building mobile coverage (DAS)

Big buildings (shopping centres, hospitals, tunnels, stadiums, office towers) block mobile signals with concrete and coated glass. The fix is a DAS (distributed antenna system). A head end in a comms room takes signal from the carriers' base station equipment. It distributes that signal through the building to many small ceiling antennas, either over coaxial cable with passive splitters (passive DAS) or over fibre to powered remote units near the antennas (active DAS). Carriers must approve and connect their services, so this work is done by specialist integrators under carrier rules.

DAS work is a strong fit for someone with cabling skills: coax and fibre installation, connector termination, labelling, sweep and PIM (passive intermodulation) testing, and lots of ceiling space work. Your RF and decibel knowledge from T10 and T16 pays off here.

On the job

On a DAS project, you might spend a week pulling and terminating coax runs in ceilings, then a day with a test set measuring the loss of each run against the design. A run that measures 3 dB worse than design usually means a bad connector. Neat labelling matches the design drawing so the RF engineer can trust your results.

Typical interview questions

Practise saying these answers out loud. Short, clear and safety-first wins.

"What is the difference between FTTP and FTTN?"

FTTP runs fibre all the way to an NBN box in the premises; speed does not depend on distance and there is no powered equipment in the street. FTTN runs fibre to a powered street cabinet, then uses the old copper pair with VDSL2; speed drops with copper length and condition.

"An FTTP customer has no service. What do you check?"

Lights on the connection box first. Then measure light at the box with a power meter. If there is no or low light, inspect and clean connectors, check the fibre for tight bends with a VFL, then work back towards the pit and splitter. Record readings at each point. Escalate if it is a network-side fault.

"Why would a long copper line sync slower?"

Loss in dB grows with length and with frequency, so the high VDSL2 tones fade into the noise. Crosstalk from other pairs adds noise. Less SNR per tone means fewer bits per tone, so lower sync speed.

"How do you make a pit safe before you work in it?"

Set up barriers and signage for pedestrians and traffic, lift the lid with the right keys and technique, look for water, damage, spiders and snakes, test the air if the procedure says so, never enter a confined space without the ticket and permit, and keep the area tidy so nobody falls in.

"A customer wants the box somewhere you think is a bad idea. What do you do?"

Explain politely why (no power point, cable damage risk, rules), offer two good alternatives, and record what was agreed. Do not install somewhere unsafe or non-compliant just to please them.

"What does 1:32 mean and what does it cost?"

One fibre is split into 32. Ideal loss is 10·log(32) ≈ 15 dB, about 17 dB in practice. It uses a big chunk of the loss budget, so every dirty connector matters.

"Do you have your White Card, licence and registration?"

Answer honestly and say what you are working on and when it will be done. Bring copies of every card.

Check yourself

Why is GPON called "passive"?

Because there is no powered equipment between the OLT at the local site and the ONT at the premises. The splitters are glass components that need no power. This makes the street network cheap to run and reliable in power cuts (although the customer's box still needs power).

Two FTTP houses share a splitter. Why can't they both transmit upstream at any time they like?

Their light would combine in the splitter and arrive at the OLT on top of each other, corrupting both. The OLT assigns each ONT time slots (TDMA). Downstream does not have this problem because only the OLT transmits.

An FTTC service drops out every night at 10 pm. What simple cause should you ask about?

Whether the customer switches the NCD off at night. The NCD reverse-powers the DPU in the pit. If it is the only one powering that DPU, the DPU goes dark and the service drops. (Other causes exist, but this one costs nothing to check.)

Line A is 400 m and line B is 1.2 km, same cable. Roughly how does their loss at 8 MHz compare?

Loss in dB is proportional to length, so B has about 3 times the dB loss of A. With the indicative 0.4 mm figure of ~21·√8 ≈ 59 dB/km, A loses about 24 dB and B about 71 dB at 8 MHz. That 47 dB difference wipes out the upper tones on B.

Why does vectoring help short FTTN lines more than long ones?

On short lines the signal is strong, so crosstalk is the main noise; cancelling it raises SNR a lot. On long lines the signal is already below the background noise floor at high frequencies; removing crosstalk cannot bring back a signal that is not there.

Why does geostationary satellite internet feel laggy even on a fast plan?

The satellite is about 35,786 km up. A request and reply need four trips up or down, about 143,000 km in total, which takes about 0.48 s at the speed of light before any processing. Bandwidth (Mb/s) and latency (delay) are different things.

On HFC, why can one bad connector at one house affect the neighbours?

All homes on a node share the coax spectrum and the upstream path. Radio noise that leaks in (ingress) through a loose or damaged connector travels upstream and adds noise for everyone on that node.

What should you confirm before paying for a telecom course in Australia?

That it leads to the ACMA cabling registration and endorsements the job needs, that the RTO is genuine, and which other tickets (White Card, heights, EWP, confined space, first aid) the employers you are targeting actually require. Confirm current rules with the ACMA and your state WHS regulator.
