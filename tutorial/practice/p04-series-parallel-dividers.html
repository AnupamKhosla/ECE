Series, parallel and voltage dividers

You will prove Kirchhoff's two laws with your own meter readings, combine resistors in series and parallel, and build voltage dividers from fixed resistors, a potentiometer, a light sensor and a thermistor, the same way a BMS controller reads a room temperature sensor and an alarm panel supervises its zones.

The two rules: series and parallel

Two parts are in series when the same current must pass through one and then the other: there is only one path. Two parts are in parallel when they connect between the same two nodes: the current has a choice of paths, and both parts have the same voltage across them.

Series

Same current through each part. The voltages add up to the total. Resistances add: more resistors in a row make it harder for current to flow.

Rtotal = R1 + R2 + R3 + …

Parallel

Same voltage across each part. The currents add up to the total. Every extra path makes it easier for current to flow, so the total is smaller than the smallest resistor.

1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …

For exactly two resistors in parallel there is a handy shortcut, "product over sum": R = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2). Two equal resistors in parallel give half the value: 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ.

Worked examples. 1 kΩ + 2.2 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ in series = 7.9 kΩ. The same three in parallel: 1/R = 1/1 + 1/2.2 + 1/4.7 = 1 + 0.4545 + 0.2128 = 1.6673 per kΩ, so R = 0.600 kΩ = 600 Ω. Check: it is smaller than the smallest (1 kΩ), as it must be.

Common misconception

Wrong: "Adding a resistor always increases the resistance."
Right: Only in series. In parallel, adding a resistor adds a new path, so the total drops. Think of lanes on a road: adding a lane (parallel) lets more cars through; adding a toll gate in line (series) lets fewer through.

Lab: prove Kirchhoff's laws with a meter

Two laws, named after Gustav Kirchhoff, solve every DC circuit. They are just bookkeeping of charge and energy:

  • Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): around any closed loop, the voltage rises equal the voltage drops. Energy given to each coulomb by the battery is all spent by the time it returns.
  • Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): the current into a node equals the current out. Charge is not created, destroyed or stored in a wire junction.

Predict first

R2 ∥ R3 = (2.2 × 4.7) / (2.2 + 4.7) = 10.34 / 6.9 = 1.499 kΩ. Total = 1 + 1.499 = 2.499 kΩ. Battery current I₁ = 9 / 2.499 kΩ = 3.60 mA. V(R1) = 3.60 mA × 1 kΩ = 3.60 V. V(B) = 9 − 3.60 = 5.40 V. Then I₂ = 5.40 / 2.2 kΩ = 2.45 mA and I₃ = 5.40 / 4.7 kΩ = 1.15 mA. Notice I₂ + I₃ = 3.60 mA = I₁, and the smaller resistor takes the larger share.

  1. Build it on the breadboard from Figure 1. Measure each resistor first (out of circuit) and write down the real values.

  2. Measure the battery under load (a fresh 9 V often reads 9.3–9.6 V). Scale all predictions by Vbattery/9.

  3. Measure voltages (black probe on node C for V(B) and the battery; across R1 directly).

  4. Measure the three currents, each with the meter in series: I₁ in the wire between R1 and node B; I₂ in the leg of R2; I₃ in the leg of R3. Power off each time you rearrange.

  5. Check the laws: does V(R1) + V(B) equal the battery? Does I₂ + I₃ equal I₁?

Why it works

Voltage is energy per coulomb. A coulomb leaving the battery's + terminal carries 9.38 J; it spends 3.73 J in R1 and 5.65 J in R2 or R3, and arrives back with nothing left. That is KVL. Current is coulombs per second; charge cannot pile up in a junction of copper (it would build a huge voltage almost instantly), so what flows in must flow out. That is KCL. The small mismatches you see are resistor tolerance, meter accuracy and the burden voltage of the ammeter.

The voltage divider

Two resistors in series across a supply share the voltage in proportion to their resistance. The bigger resistor gets the bigger share. The junction between them gives you a smaller voltage, set by the ratio:

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2) R1 is the top resistor (from Vin), R2 the bottom one (to 0 V), and Vout is measured across R2. Example: 9 V with R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 4.7 kΩ gives 9 × 4.7 / 14.7 = 2.88 V. The same current flows through both, I = 9 / 14.7 kΩ = 0.61 mA.

The divider is everywhere: setting a transistor's bias, scaling 12 V down to a 3.3 V microcontroller input, reading sensors whose resistance changes, and inside every voltmeter range. Its weak point is the load.

The loading effect, measured

  1. Build a 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider on 9 V. Measure Vout: half the battery, about 4.5–4.7 V.

  2. Add a 10 kΩ load from Vout to 0 V. The bottom becomes 10 k ∥ 10 k = 5 kΩ. Predicted Vout = 9 × 5 / 15 = 3.0 V. Measure it. A third of your output has vanished.

  3. Swap the load for 100 kΩ. Bottom = 10 k ∥ 100 k = 9.09 kΩ, Vout = 9 × 9.09 / 19.09 = 4.29 V, about 5% low.

  4. Now make the meter the load. Build a 1 MΩ / 1 MΩ divider and measure Vout. You expect 4.5 V; the meter's own 10 MΩ input gives 1 M ∥ 10 M = 0.909 MΩ, so it shows about 4.29 V. Same 10:1 ratio, same 5% error.

The lesson: a divider only gives its ideal voltage when the load is much bigger than the divider resistors. A practical rule is load ≥ 10 × R2 for about 5% error, and ≥ 100 × R2 for under 1%. You cannot power anything hungry from a divider: a divider is for signals (inputs that draw microamps), not for supplying a motor or an LED. For power you need a regulator (P10).

Bench tip

If a voltage reads lower than it should only when your meter touches it, suspect loading: the circuit has a very high resistance (hundreds of kΩ or more). Try the measurement on a higher-impedance setting if your meter has one, or measure the resistors instead and calculate.

The potentiometer: an adjustable divider

A potentiometer ("pot") is a resistive track with a sliding contact called the wiper. The two end terminals see the full track resistance; the middle terminal is the wiper. Connect the ends across a supply and the wiper gives any voltage from 0 to Vin: it is a divider whose R1 and R2 change together, always adding up to the track value.

  1. Measure the pot with the ohms range: outer pins about 10 kΩ whatever the shaft position; wiper to one end changes from near 0 to near 10 kΩ as you turn.

  2. Wire it as a divider on the 3×AA pack: ends to + and −, meter on the wiper. Turn the shaft: Vout sweeps from 0 to about 4.7 V. At the midpoint it reads about 2.35 V.

  3. Load it with a 1 kΩ resistor from wiper to 0 V. Near mid-travel the reading sags badly (the source resistance at mid-travel is 5 k ∥ 5 k = 2.5 kΩ, larger than the load). Remove the load.

On the job

The setpoint knob on many wall-mounted room temperature sensors is a potentiometer. The BMS controller reads its wiper voltage (or resistance) and turns it into "±3 °C from the setpoint". Trim pots ("trimmers") on PCBs set things like a PIR sensor's sensitivity, a power supply's exact output voltage, or a camera's IR threshold. Adjust trimmers with an insulated trim tool, gently: they are only rated for a few hundred turns.

Sensor dividers: LDR and NTC thermistor

Many sensors are just resistors that change with the world. A circuit cannot measure resistance directly, but it can measure voltage. So we put the sensor into a divider with a fixed resistor, and read the junction voltage. This is the single most common sensor circuit in electronics.

The light-dependent resistor (LDR)

An LDR is a cadmium sulfide track whose resistance falls as light frees more charge carriers. A typical small LDR reads several hundred kΩ to over 1 MΩ in darkness, roughly 5–20 kΩ under room lighting, and under 1 kΩ in bright light (each type differs; measure yours).

  1. Measure the LDR on the ohms range: covered with your finger, in room light, and under a phone torch. Write the three values.

  2. Build a divider: LDR on top (from + 4.7 V), 10 kΩ fixed on the bottom. Vout across the 10 kΩ rises with light.

  3. Predict and measure. With the LDR at 10 kΩ: 4.7 × 10/20 = 2.35 V. At 500 kΩ (dark): 4.7 × 10/510 = 0.09 V. At 500 Ω (torch): 4.7 × 10/10.5 = 4.48 V.

  4. Swap the positions (10 kΩ on top, LDR on the bottom): now Vout falls with light. Which way round you wire it decides whether the signal means "light" or "dark".

Choose the fixed resistor near the sensor's resistance in the middle of the range you care about: that is where the divider is most sensitive.

The 10 kΩ NTC thermistor

An NTC (negative temperature coefficient) thermistor is a ceramic semiconductor bead. Warmer means more free charge carriers, so its resistance falls as temperature rises, by roughly 4% per °C near room temperature. "10 kΩ" means 10 kΩ at 25 °C. A common model for its curve is the beta equation:

R(T) = R25 × eB (1/T − 1/298.15) T is in kelvin (°C + 273.15), R25 = 10 kΩ, and B (the "beta" value) describes how steep the curve is; 3950 K is common for hobby parts. At 20 °C: 1/293.15 − 1/298.15 = 5.72 × 10⁻⁵, × 3950 = 0.226, e0.226 = 1.254, so R = 12.5 kΩ.

On the job

The plain wall-mounted room temperature sensor in an office is very often just a 10 kΩ NTC thermistor in a plastic box, wired back over two cores to an analogue input on the BMS controller. The controller contains the fixed resistor and does exactly this divider maths. Sensors are sold with different 10 kΩ curves (you will see names like "10K Type 2" and "10K Type 3"), and the controller input must be configured for the matching curve, or the reading will be off by a degree or more at the ends of the range. Check the sensor's datasheet.

A sensor reading −40 °C or +150 °C in the BMS is almost never the weather: it is a broken wire (open → very cold) or a short (→ very hot). Check the resistance at the controller end with the input disconnected, and compare it with the expected value for the room temperature.

Bench tip

The meter itself can warm a thermistor: on the ohms range it pushes a small current through it. The controller does too: with 5 V across 20 kΩ about 0.25 mA flows and the thermistor dissipates around 0.6 mW, enough to warm a tiny bead by a fraction of a degree in still air. That "self-heating" is why sensor circuits keep the current small.

End-of-line resistors: four states from one pair of wires

An alarm panel has to know more than "door open or closed". It must also know if someone has cut the cable, or shorted it to fool the panel. With a plain switch on two wires, a cut wire looks exactly like an open door, and a short looks exactly like a closed door. The fix is to put known resistors at the end of the line (EOL), at the device, so each state gives a different resistance.

Try the widget with "Single EOL" and "No EOL". With a single EOL in series with a normally-closed contact, the panel can detect a short, but "door open" and "cable cut" both read as open circuit. With a bare contact, it can tell nothing beyond open or closed. Double EOL gives all four states, which is why many intruder-alarm panels use it. Build it on the breadboard with a push-button (or reed switch) and two 2.2 kΩ resistors, and measure each state with the ohms range: 2.2 kΩ, 4.4 kΩ, OL, 0 Ω.

Fire circuits use the same idea differently. On a conventional fire zone, detectors are connected in parallel across the pair and an EOL device sits at the far end. In normal state a small supervisory current flows through the EOL. A detector in alarm draws a much larger, defined current. An open cable stops the supervisory current (fault), and a short draws more current than any detector would. The panel watches the current level to tell these apart; exactly how a short is treated depends on the panel and its standard.

In Australia

EOL values are set by the panel manufacturer (common values include 1 kΩ, 2.2 kΩ, 4.7 kΩ and 6.8 kΩ, but use exactly what the panel's installation manual specifies). Fire detection and alarm systems are designed and installed to AS 1670.1, and fire-protection work is licensed or accredited in some states. Security installation work generally needs a security licence from your state's licensing body. Requirements differ by state and change over time, so confirm with the relevant regulator (see C04, C06 and C11).

Check yourself

What is 4.7 kΩ ∥ 4.7 kΩ ∥ 4.7 kΩ? And 220 Ω + 330 Ω + 1 kΩ?

Three equal resistors in parallel give a third: 1.57 kΩ. In series they simply add: 220 + 330 + 1000 = 1550 Ω.

In the Kirchhoff lab, why does R2 (2.2 kΩ) carry more current than R3 (4.7 kΩ)?

They are in parallel, so they have the same voltage (5.40 V). By Ohm's law, I = V/R, the smaller resistance carries the larger current: 5.40/2.2 k = 2.45 mA vs 5.40/4.7 k = 1.15 mA. Current divides in inverse proportion to resistance.

Design a divider to turn 12 V into about 3 V for a microcontroller input.

You need R2/(R1 + R2) = 3/12 = 0.25, so R1 = 3 × R2. With E12 values: R1 = 33 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ gives 12 × 10/43 = 2.79 V (safe, a bit low), or R1 = 30 kΩ (E24) gives exactly 3.0 V. Use tens of kΩ: low enough that the input's leakage doesn't load it, high enough that the divider wastes only about 0.3 mA.

A 100 kΩ / 100 kΩ divider on 10 V is measured with a 10 MΩ meter. What does the meter show?

Bottom = 100 k ∥ 10 M = 99.0 kΩ. V = 10 × 99.0/199.0 = 4.975 V instead of 5.000 V: 0.5% low. The load is 100× R2, so the error is under 1%.

A BMS shows a room at −40 °C. The sensor is a 10 kΩ NTC. What is the most likely fault, and how do you check it?

An open circuit: a broken core, a loose terminal or a disconnected sensor. Open circuit means infinite resistance, which the controller reads as extremely cold. Disconnect the sensor wires at the controller and measure the resistance of the loop: OL confirms the break. A healthy loop at about 22 °C would read roughly 11 kΩ plus a few ohms of cable.

With double EOL (2.2 kΩ + 2.2 kΩ), what does the panel see in each state?

Normal: 2.2 kΩ (contact shorts the alarm resistor). Alarm: 4.4 kΩ (both in series). Cut cable: open circuit (∞). Shorted cable: about 0 Ω. Four different values, so the panel can tell all four apart.

An installer fits both EOL resistors in the panel instead of at the door sensor. Why is that wrong?

Then the resistors are at the panel end, and the cable run to the door is just a bare contact on two wires. A short across the cable at any point between panel and door would look like a closed door (normal), defeating the supervision. EOL resistors must be at the end of the line, at or inside the device.

Why can't you power a 100 mA module from a 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider on 12 V to get 6 V?

The module looks like a load of about 6 V / 0.1 A = 60 Ω, in parallel with the 10 kΩ bottom resistor. The bottom becomes about 60 Ω and Vout collapses to about 12 × 60/10 060 ≈ 0.07 V. Dividers are for signals into high-impedance inputs. To power something, use a regulator.
