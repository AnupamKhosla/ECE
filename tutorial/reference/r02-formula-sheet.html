On the job

The voltage drop, PoE budget and storage sums are what a CCTV or access-control quote is built on. A tech who can do them quickly, and explain them to a customer, gets trusted with design work.

Check yourself

A 24 V AC plug-pack feeds a bridge rectifier and a big capacitor. Roughly what DC voltage will you measure with no load?

About 32 V. The capacitor charges to the peak, not the RMS: 24 × 1.414 = 33.9 V, minus two diode drops (about 1.4 V) in the bridge. This is why "24 V AC" equipment needs parts rated well above 24 V.

A sensor sends 4–20 mA for 0–50 °C. The PLC sees 16 mA. What is the temperature?

(16 − 4)/16 = 0.75 of the span, so 0.75 × 50 = 37.5 °C.

Why does a −3 dB fibre connector loss mean you have lost half the light?

For power, dB = 10 log(P2/P1). 10 log(0.5) = −3.0 dB. So −3 dB is half the power, −10 dB a tenth, −20 dB a hundredth.

You double the length of a 12 V camera cable. What happens to the voltage drop?

It doubles. The drop is I × R and R is proportional to length. To keep the same drop you need double the cross-section (for example 0.75 mm² → 1.5 mm²), or a higher supply voltage, which needs less current for the same power.

An RC circuit has R = 47 kΩ and C = 22 µF. About how long until the capacitor is effectively charged?

τ = 47 000 × 22×10−6 = 1.03 s. Effectively full after 5τ ≈ 5.2 s.

Skills checklist

  • I convert prefixes (k, m, µ, n) to base units before I calculate.
  • I can size an LED resistor and check its power rating.
  • I can work out a DC cable voltage drop and the longest allowed run.
  • I can scale a 4–20 mA signal to engineering units and back.
  • I can turn a ratio into dB and dBm into milliwatts.
  • I can estimate CCTV storage, a PoE budget and battery standby capacity.
