Practice · P23

Build a DIN-rail control panel (12/24 V)

Build a small control panel the way a technician does: a DIN rail, a fused 24 V DC feed, numbered terminals, ferruled and labelled wires. It watches a door contact, sounds a buzzer, and holds the alarm on until you press reset. You finish with a test sheet, a marked-up drawing and a handover photo.

Time 6–8 h over two sessionsLevel IntermediateKit cost ~A$250–450Theory T03, T06Builds on P05, P08, P10, P22

Read this first: what you wire and what you never touch

A real control panel has two halves. The 230 V side brings mains power in and feeds a power supply. The 24 V DC side takes that supply's output and runs the relays, controllers and field devices. This lab builds only the second half.

Your panel runs from a 24 V DC plug-pack: a sealed supply with a moulded mains plug, built and tested by its maker. You never open it. You wire only its low-voltage DC lead. It stands in for the DIN-rail power supply you will meet on a real job.

Safety · no mains terminals, ever

A DIN-rail power supply has screw terminals for the 230 V input. Wiring those terminals is mains work, and in Australia it must be done by a licensed electrician. So on the bench you use a plug-pack that feeds the DC side, and you do not buy a bare DIN-rail supply for this lab. Never connect a mains cable to one "just to test". On a real job the electrician wires and tests the supply's mains side, and you start at its 24 V DC output terminals. Everything in this chapter stays at extra-low voltage (ELV): at most 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC.

In Australia

Buy a plug-pack with the RCM mark (Regulatory Compliance Mark) from a normal Australian supplier. Choose a regulated 24 V DC type rated 1 A or more. An unregulated "wall wart" can give 30 V or more with no load, which is outside the 24 V ±10 % that most relay coils and lamps are made for. Licensing rules differ by state, so confirm the current rule with your state's electrical safety regulator. The legal meaning of ELV, and why the licensing line sits where it does, is in T09.

Bench tip

Buy terminals, jumper bars, end stops, end plates and marker strips from one maker and one series. Parts from different makers often do not fit, and an almost-fitting jumper bar is a fault waiting to happen.

The panel you will build

The panel is called CP-01. It watches a door and raises an alarm that stays on until someone presses reset. It is a tiny version of the alarm and monitoring panels in security and building-management work.

What it must do

  1. Door shut: relay K1 is energised through the door contact. No alarm.
  2. Door opens: K1 drops out. Relay K2 pulls in, locks itself on, and switches the buzzer B1 and lamp H1.
  3. Door shuts again: the alarm stays on. It is latched.
  4. Reset button S2: clears the alarm only if the door is shut. If the door is still open, the alarm comes straight back.
  5. A cut door-contact wire counts as an open door.
  6. A power failure clears the latch (a relay has no memory without power). At power-up with the door open, the alarm starts again.

Layout planning: power at the top, field at the bottom

A DIN rail is a standard 35 mm steel top-hat strip that terminals, relays and power supplies clip onto, so parts from many makers fit the same rail. Plan on paper before you drill or snap anything onto it. A bad layout stays bad for twenty years, and everyone who opens the panel pays for it.

The five layout rules

  1. Power at the top. Supply, fuse and the 0 V and +24 V distribution come first. Mains (if any) enters at the top, and everything else is fed from there. It also matches the way you read a schematic: supply rail on top, 0 V at the bottom.

  2. Flow left to right. Inputs on the left, logic in the middle, outputs on the right, the same order as the ladder diagram in Figure 4. Anyone tracing a signal can follow it with their eyes.

  3. Field terminals at the bottom. Every cable from outside lands on a terminal first, never straight on a relay. Then you can unplug the field side to test the panel alone, replace a relay without touching a field cable, and half-split a fault at the terminal strip (P21).

  4. About 20 % spare. Leave at least a fifth of every rail and of the duct empty, and fit a few spare terminals. Jobs change. A full panel means a new panel.

  5. Room to work. Leave about 20 to 25 mm between a device row and the duct so a screwdriver can reach, and place labels where you can read them with the cover closed. Duct should be about half full, no more.

Worked example: how long a rail?

Typical widths: a 2.5 mm² feed-through terminal is about 5.2 mm, a fused terminal about 8.2 mm, a 2-pole relay with socket about 15.5 mm, a small timer 17.5 mm, an end stop about 9 mm and an end plate about 2 mm. Check your own data sheets.

Bench tip

Heat rises. In this cool little ELV panel it does not matter, but on a real job a power supply or drive at the top of a sealed box bakes the parts above and around it. The layout drawing and the data sheets decide the spacing, and a panel in a hot plant room or a roof space needs the derating figures read.

Terminal numbers and wire numbers

Three kinds of number are in play, and mixing them up is the commonest beginner error (P22 covers this in detail). The terminal number belongs to a place: X1:3 is strip X1, terminal 3. The wire number belongs to a node: every wire joined to the same point carries the same number. The cable number belongs to a multi-core cable between two places: C1, C2.

Numbering rules

  • One strip, one purpose. X1 is power, X2 inputs, X3 outputs. Never mix a 24 V supply terminal into an input strip.
  • Number from 1, left to right, no gaps and no repeats inside a strip. Print or write the number on the terminal's label strip.
  • Wire numbers are node numbers. Wire 2 is +24 V after the fuse, wire 0 is 0 V. If you find wire 1 anywhere except the plug-pack and F1, someone has bypassed the fuse.
  • Both ends of every wire carry a marker. Fit the sleeve before you crimp the ferrule, and print the number so it reads from left to right.
  • Name the spares. A spare terminal is numbered and labelled "spare". A blank, unlabelled terminal makes the next person wonder if it is a mistake.
  • Draw it. The drawing set for CP-01 is Figure 4 (schematic), Figure 2 (terminals) and the wire list below. If the wiring and the drawing ever disagree, one of them is wrong, and you fix it before you leave.

Common misconception

Wrong: "It is a red wire, so it is a safe 24 V wire." Right: a colour is a hint, not a test. Red and black are also used for mains conductors, old panels have been rewired by many hands, and colours fade. The wire number and a meter tell you what a wire really is. Measure before you touch anything you did not wire yourself.

Ferrules, tightness and segregation

Why ferrule every wire

A ferrule (also called a bootlace ferrule or cord end) is a small metal tube with a plastic collar, crimped on the end of a stranded wire. A screw terminal then clamps one neat block of copper instead of thirty loose strands. Without it, the screw squashes the strands sideways, a few escape to touch the next terminal, and the clamp slowly loosens as the strands relax. Spring and push-in terminals work best with a ferrule too. (Do not confuse it with a ferrule marker, the printed sleeve with the wire number: see P22.)

Wire size0.5 mm²0.75 mm²1.0 mm²1.5 mm²2.5 mm²
Common collar colourwhitegreyredblackblue

That colour code is common in Australia (it follows the DIN 46228 style), but brands differ, so check the pack. The size on the pack is what matters, not the colour.

  1. Pick the size from the wire, not from the terminal. A 0.5 mm² wire gets a 0.5 mm² ferrule, even though the terminal can take 2.5 mm².

  2. Strip to the sleeve length. Set the stripper to the wire and cut cleanly without nicking strands. Twist the strands lightly in the direction they already lie.

  3. Slide on the marker sleeve first, then the ferrule, until the insulation touches the collar and the copper is flush with the end of the sleeve or sticks out no more than about 1 mm.

  4. Crimp in the matching die. A ratchet crimper will not let go until the crimp is complete. The sleeve should be squared, not split.

  5. Inspect, then tug. No strands outside, no insulation inside the sleeve. Pull firmly and steadily by hand. If the wire moves, cut the ferrule off and do it again. A bad ferrule costs 50 cents; a call-back costs hours.

  6. Terminate. Open the screw fully, push the ferrule in until it touches the back of the clamp, tighten, and tug again.

How tight is tight

Terminal makers give a tightening torque in newton-metres (N·m). For small 2.5 mm² screw terminals it is typically around 0.5 to 0.8 N·m, but read the data sheet of your terminal: it also tells you the right blade width. Too loose, and the joint wobbles, heats, or opens at random. Too tight, and you strip the screw, crack the housing or crush the ferrule. A torque screwdriver (about A$40–150) gives repeatable results, and many tradies mark a finished screw with a dab of paint (a torque stripe) so that any later movement shows. Without one, tighten until firm, stop, and tug. Re-check every screw once after the panel has run for a while, because brass and copper relax a little as they warm and cool.

Why it works

A bad joint has some extra resistance, and the heat it makes is I²R. In this panel the relay coil takes about 20 mA. Put 0.5 Ω of bad joint in that circuit and the loss is 0.02² × 0.5 = 0.0002 W: harmless, but the contact is also unstable, so the relay can drop out at random. That is the nightmare "intermittent" fault. Now put the same 0.5 Ω in a 10 A power circuit: 10² × 0.5 = 50 W of heat in a terminal smaller than your thumbnail, and that is how fires start. The habit is the same; the stakes are not. Learn it at 24 V.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A neatly twisted bare wire in a screw terminal is just as good as a ferrule." Right: it holds on day one. Strands splay, the screw cuts into them, and under vibration or temperature swings the joint loosens. Many specifications require ferrules on every stranded wire, and spring and push-in terminals work best with them. It costs 50 cents and ten seconds.

Keep ELV away from mains

Extra-low voltage wiring and mains wiring (low voltage, or LV: above ELV and up to 1000 V AC in Australian rules) must not share a wire duct, a cable or a terminal strip. In a real panel they are separated by distance, by a barrier or by separate compartments, and any mains terminals are shrouded and labelled. Why:

  • One fault can undo ELV. If a damaged mains conductor touches an "ELV" wire, that wire is suddenly at 230 V, and a person or a device expecting 24 V meets mains.
  • Noise. A mains cable running alongside a signal wire couples 50 Hz hum into it, and a sensitive input can trigger falsely.
  • Expectation. A technician working on an ELV loom does not expect a live mains wire hidden among the cores. A wire that is only "probably" safe is not safe.
  • Rules. AS/NZS 3000 and the assembly standards (AS/NZS 61439 for switchboards and control gear) set separation requirements. Your bench panel is not a certified assembly, but the habits are the same. Confirm the current requirements and the project spec for any real job.

In CP-01 there is no mains inside the box at all, which is exactly the point of using a plug-pack.

Size the feed: load, fuse and cable

Before you power the panel, work out three things: how much current it draws, which fuse protects it, and how much voltage is lost on the way to the far device.

The fuse protects the wire, not the devices. It must be bigger than the normal load (or it nuisance-blows) and smaller than the wire and the supply can safely carry. For this lab the working rule is:

fuse ≥ 1.5 × total running current supply ≥ 1.25 × total current Take the next standard size up. The 1.25 gives the supply about 25 % headroom, so it runs at 80 % of its rating or less. Keep the fuse well below the supply's rating so that a short circuit really does blow it. In this lab: 0.5 A fuse, 1 A plug-pack.

A polyfuse (a resettable PTC fuse, see P10) is chosen by its hold current: the current it carries without tripping. Aim for a hold current at least 1.25 times the load, because heat in a warm box lowers the true hold current. It trips at roughly twice the hold current and is slow, so it is a weaker protector than a fuse, but it resets itself.

Worked example: the door strike at the end of a long run

A 24 V door strike draws 250 mA. It is 60 m from the panel on 0.5 mm² two-core cable. Loop resistance = 2 × 60 × 0.0344 = 4.13 Ω. The drop is 0.25 × 4.13 = 1.03 V, which is 4.3 % of 24 V. That works. Now run the same job at 12 V: the same power needs 0.5 A, the drop is 0.5 × 4.13 = 2.06 V, and that is 17 % of 12 V. A 12 V strike at the end of that cable fails to open the door on a cold morning.

Why it works

For the same power, doubling the voltage halves the current. The drop in the cable is I × R, so it halves too, and as a share of the (now doubled) supply voltage it falls to a quarter. That is why 24 V is common for long control runs, and why PoE uses a much higher voltage still.

Build the circuit in stages

Figure 4 is the schematic of the control logic, drawn as a ladder diagram: two vertical power rails and horizontal rungs, each rung one circuit. +24 V (wire 2) is on the left, 0 V (wire 0) on the right. Read each rung from left to right. This is the diagram electricians and PLC programmers use for relay logic.

Why it works

The self-hold is memory made from feedback. Once K2 pulls in, its own contact 11–14 closes and powers its own coil, so the trigger can disappear and K2 stays on. Only breaking the supply (the reset button) lets it go. Relays also have hysteresis: they usually pull in at roughly 70–80 % of rated coil voltage and drop out far lower, so the latch is firm. It is the relay version of the set–reset flip-flop in logic.

Stage 1 · Mount, bridge and label (no power)

  1. Mount the rails. Cut the DIN rail to length, file off the burrs, and fix it with M4 screws about every 100 mm. Fit the wire duct between the rows as in Figure 1.

  2. Clip on the relay sockets and snap on the terminals in order. Sockets go on rail B. X1: F1, then 2–5, then 6–11, then PE. X2 and X3 each get six. Fit end stops at both ends of each group, and an end plate where a group ends.

  3. Fit the jumper bars. Red bar: F1 out to X1:5. Black bar: X1:6 to X1:11. X2: bridge 1–3. X3: bridge 1–2, and bridge 4–6. One bar, one wire number.

  4. Print the labels. Number each terminal, tag each device, and label each strip X1, X2, X3. Fit the relays into their sockets.

Stage 2 · Power and fuse (first power-up, no loads)

  1. Find the polarity first. With the adapter not yet wired to anything, plug the plug-pack into the wall and measure across the adapter's screw terminals with the meter on DC volts (P03). A positive reading with the red probe on a terminal means that terminal is +. Mark it, then unplug the plug-pack.

  2. Wire G1 to X1. Adapter + to X1:1 (wire 1, red), − to X1:6 (wire 0, black).

  3. Fuse out, plug in. Leave the F1 lever open. Plug the plug-pack in. Measure X1:1 to X1:6 on the volts range. You should read +24 V, within about ±5 % (22.8 to 25.2 V), with the red probe on X1:1.

  4. Close F1 and measure X1:2 to X1:6. It should match to within 0.1 V. Unplug the plug-pack before doing any more wiring.

Stage 3 · Door loop, K1 and a buzzer (not yet latched)

  1. Wire the loop. X1:2 to X2:1 (wire 2). Cable C1 from X2:1 and X2:4 to the reed switch S1. Wire 3 from X2:4 to K1:A1. Wire 0 from X1:7 to K1:A2.

  2. Wire the buzzer test. Wire 2 from X1:4 to K1:21. Wire 6 from K1:22 to X3:1. Wire 0 from X1:9 to X3:4. Cable C3 from X3:1 and X3:4 to B1 (+ to X3:1).

  3. Run the pre-power checks (see the "Test before power" section) for what you have wired so far, then power up. With the magnet at the reed, K1's LED lights and the buzzer is silent. Pull the magnet away: K1 drops out, and K1's normally closed contact 21–22 closes and the buzzer sounds.

Stage 4 · The latch: K2 and reset

  1. Add the reset button. Cable C2 from X2:2 and X2:5 to S2 (11 and 12). Wire 4 from X2:5 to K1:11 and to K2:11 (a twin ferrule at X2:5).

  2. Add K2. Wire 5 from K1:12 to K2:A1, and from K2:14 to K2:A1 (twin ferrule at A1). Wire 0 from X1:8 to K2:A2. Wire 2 from X1:3 to K2:21.

  3. Move the output. Unplug the Stage 3 test wires from K1:21 and K1:22 (the wires from X1:4 and to X3:1). Wire 6 from K2:24 to X3:1. Add C4 for the lamp H1 (X3:2 and X3:5).

  4. Test every row of the test sheet below. Press reset with the door open and watch the alarm return: that is the circuit working, not failing.

Safety · polarity on relays and lamps

Relays and lamps with a built-in LED or coil diode are polarised. Put 24 V on one the wrong way and the diode conducts like a short circuit. At best the fuse blows. At worst the part is damaged. Check A1 = +, A2 = −, and the + mark on lamps and the buzzer, before you power up. That is also why F1 is there.

Common misconception

Wrong: "A relay marked 10 A can switch any 10 A load." Right: contact ratings depend on the supply. AC current passes through zero 100 times a second, which snuffs out the arc as contacts open. DC never does, so the same contact is rated for much less at DC, and a coil or solenoid load is worse again. Read the DC rating on the data sheet and fit a diode across any inductive load. At 24 V and 20 mA you are safe here, but the habit matters on a 2 A siren.

Bench tip

Always do a wiggle test before power-up: grip each wire a few centimetres from its terminal and gently wiggle it. A good joint does not move. A bad one shows itself now instead of at 2 am on site. Build the wiring list in order and tick as you go.

Options: timer relay and a fail-safe lock demo

Option A · Door-held-open delay with a timer relay

A DIN timer relay delays a contact after its coil is energised. Used as an on-delay, it lets the door stay open for, say, 10 seconds before the alarm latches. This is the idea behind "entry delay" on alarm panels. On many timers the contacts are numbered 15 (common), 16 (normally closed) and 18 (normally open); read the label on yours.

  1. Remove the wire from K1:12 to K2:A1.
  2. Wire 2 again: X1:4 to K1:21. Wire 9: K1:22 to KT:A1, so the timer is powered while the door is open (K1 off, its normally closed contact 21–22 closed). Wire 0: KT:A2 to X1:11.
  3. Wire 10: K1:12 to KT:15. Wire 5: KT:18 to K2:A1.
  4. Set the delay to about 5 to 10 s. Open the door: nothing happens until the delay is up, then the alarm latches. Close the door before it finishes: no alarm.

Option B · Fail-safe versus fail-secure

Electric door locks come in two kinds. A fail-safe lock (a magnetic lock is always one) is locked while it has power and unlocks when power is lost. A fail-secure lock (an electric strike) is locked with no power and stays locked when power is lost; it unlocks when energised. Lamp H2 stands in for the lock coil: on means the coil has power.

  1. Add K3: wire 2 from X1:5 to K3:11, wire 0 from X1:10 to K3:A2. Button S3 on C5: X2:3 to S3:13, X2:6 to S3:14; wire 7 from X2:6 to K3:A1. Lamp H2 on C6: X3:3 (+) and X3:6 (−); wire 8 from K3:12 to X3:3.
  2. Fail-safe test: H2 is on (locked). Press S3: H2 goes out (unlocked). Release S3: H2 comes back on. Now lift the F1 lever: H2 goes out. The lock releases in a power cut.
  3. Fail-secure test: move wire 8 from K3:12 to K3:14. H2 is off. Press S3: H2 lights (unlocked). Lift F1: H2 off, still "locked".
On the job

Which lock type goes on a door is a design decision, not a preference. Doors that are part of an exit path must always open from the inside, and the National Construction Code (NCC) sets the rules. Fail-safe maglocks are usually wired through a fire-alarm release relay as well. Real systems also have a battery, so a "power cut" holds the door locked until the battery runs down (P10). As-built marking and the handover photo

No build matches its drawing exactly. You moved a wire, swapped a terminal, or added a jumper. The as-built drawing records what you really did, so the next person can trust it. Marking-up rules are in P22; here they are applied.

  1. Redline the drawings. Print Figure 4, Figure 2 and the wire list. Cross out each wire that changed and write the new one beside it, with the date and your initials. If there is no company colour scheme, use red for additions and changes and green for deletions.

Check yourself

Why can't you buy a DIN-rail power supply and wire its mains terminals for this lab? What do you use instead?

Those terminals take 230 V fixed wiring. In Australia that is licensed electricians' work, and a mistake can kill. The bench version uses a sealed 24 V DC plug-pack with a moulded plug, and you wire only its low-voltage DC lead. On a real job the electrician installs and tests the mains side, and you start at the 24 V DC output terminals.

The widest row of devices needs 98 mm. How long should the rail be, and why add the margin?

98 × 1.2 ≈ 118 mm, so a 150 mm or 200 mm rail. The margin is for new terminals or relays later, for finger and screwdriver room, and so you do not need a new panel for the first change.

Why is a door contact wired as a closed loop (closed when the door is shut)?

So that faults look like alarms. An opened door, a cut cable, a loose terminal or a broken reed all open the loop, and the panel sees "door open". If the contact closed when the door opened, a cut cable would look like a shut door and the failure would be silent.

The alarm is latched and the door is still open. You press reset. The alarm stops while you hold the button and comes back when you let go. Is the panel faulty?

No, it works as designed. Holding S2 breaks the supply to K2, so K2 drops out. When you release S2, the supply comes back and K1's normally closed contact (the door is still open) energises K2, which latches again. An alarm should not clear while the fault is present. Close the door first.

You crimp a 0.75 mm² wire in a ferrule with an 8 mm sleeve. What strip length do you use, and what do you inspect afterwards?

Strip about 8 mm, the length of the metal sleeve. After crimping, check that the insulation reaches the collar, the copper is flush with the end of the sleeve (not more than about 1 mm out), and no strand is outside. Then tug it. If it moves, cut it off and redo it.

Why should you never connect a megger to this panel? What do you do instead?

A megger applies 250 V to 1000 V DC, far more than the 24 V electronics (LEDs, diodes, timer, relay base electronics) can take, so it would destroy them. Use the ohms range of a multimeter (test voltage about 0.5–3 V) to look for unintended low-resistance paths: expect OL or above 1 MΩ between the rails, the circuits and the DIN rail, and between cable cores. It is a check for stray strands and damaged cable, not a proper insulation-resistance test.

A 24 V door strike draws 250 mA, 60 m away on 0.5 mm² two-core cable. What is the voltage drop? What happens at 12 V?

Loop resistance = 2 × 60 × 0.0344 = 4.13 Ω. Drop = 0.25 × 4.13 = 1.03 V, which is 4.3 % of 24 V: fine. At 12 V the same power needs 0.5 A, so the drop is 2.06 V, or 17 % of 12 V: it will probably fail. Double the voltage, half the current, a quarter of the percentage drop.

What does a fail-safe lock do when power is lost? What does a fail-secure lock do? Which suits a doorway on an exit path?

Fail-safe: unlocks (locked while powered). Fail-secure: stays locked (unlocked while powered). A door on an exit path must open from the inside, and the National Construction Code and the fire design decide how, so a fail-safe lock or a fail-secure lock with free mechanical exit is used. Never decide it yourself: follow the design and the spec (see C06).

Go deeper (optional): why panels have a 0 V bus and not a single "ground"

In a DC control panel, 0 V is the reference every voltage is measured from. A bus bar or bridged terminals carry it to every device, each with its own short wire, so one loose joint cannot disconnect all of them. Whether 0 V is tied to earth at one point (and where) is a design decision in the spec, and for mains-powered systems it belongs with the electrician. On the bench the plug-pack floats, so 0 V is only a reference, not earth.
