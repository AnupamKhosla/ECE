Security platforms: how Gallagher, Genetec, Integriti and Milestone think

Enterprise security systems look different on screen but share one mental model. Learn that model once (cardholders, access levels, schedules, doors, events, recording servers, retention) and you can walk into a job on any of the big platforms and learn its buttons in days, not months.

Time 60 min readLevel Beginner to job-readyBuilds on C05, C06, P19Roles Security tech, commissioning tech, integrator

Why platforms matter for your job search

In a sample of 2,764 Australian SEEK job ads captured for this course on 1–2 November 2026, 99 were security or CCTV roles. Of those, 24 mentioned Gallagher, 20 Genetec, 18 Milestone and 13 Inner Range (whose platform is Integriti). Employers name these products because their technicians spend much of the day inside the software: adding doors, enrolling cards, fixing cameras that went offline, exporting footage for police.

The products are, in plain terms:

  • Gallagher Command Centre: an access control and intruder alarm platform with its own controllers and readers.
  • Genetec Security Center: a "unified" platform that combines video management, access control and other systems in one client.
  • Inner Range Integriti: an access control and intruder alarm platform with its own controllers and expansion modules.
  • Milestone XProtect: a video management system (VMS) that works with cameras from many manufacturers.

This chapter does not teach any one product's menus. It teaches the shared model under all of them. Every platform uses its own words (one calls it an "access group", another an "access rule", another a "permission"), but the ideas map one to one. Learn the ideas, then learn each product's words on the job.

Common misconception

Wrong: "I need to know Gallagher (or Genetec, or…) before anyone will hire me." Right: employers expect to train you on their platform, often through the vendor's certification course. What they test at interview is whether you understand doors, readers, locks, networks, cameras and the logic of access decisions. A candidate who can explain why a door shows "forced open" is more useful than one who has memorised menus.

The big picture: architecture

An enterprise security system has three layers: servers (software and databases), edge devices (controllers and cameras on the network) and field devices (readers, locks, sensors, buttons). Operators use client software on workstations or a browser to watch and manage it.

The access control model

Every enterprise access system stores the same kinds of objects in its database. Learn these and you can read any of them.

Bench tip

Never give a person access to individual doors one by one. Always use access levels built from roles ("cleaner", "IT", "level 2 tenant"). When the cleaning contractor changes, you change one level, not 200 cardholders. Auditors and good supervisors check for this.

The door as an object

A "door" in the software is really a bundle of field devices, each wired to a reader module or controller. You learned the hardware in C06; here is how the software sees it.

Door modes

Each door has a mode, often changed automatically by a schedule:

  • Card only: a valid card unlocks the door for a few seconds.
  • Card + PIN: two factors, common on comms rooms and after hours.
  • Free access / unlocked: the lock stays released, for example the front door during business hours.
  • Locked / secure: nobody except special override levels gets in.
  • Lockdown: an emergency mode triggered by an operator or button.

Typical timers: unlock time (how long the lock releases after a grant, often around 5 s) and held-open time (how long the door may stay open before an alarm, often 30 s or more for busy doors). Many systems extend these for people who need more time.

Edge decisions: the controller decides

When a card is presented, the decision is usually made on the controller, not on the server. The server downloads cardholders, credentials, access levels and schedules to each controller. The controller checks them in milliseconds and stores events until the server is reachable again. This is called offline or edge operation. It is why doors keep working during a server upgrade or network outage. It also means a change you make on the server takes effect only once it has downloaded to the controller; if a controller is offline, your change waits.

    Commissioning a door, step by step

    This is the order most technicians follow on any platform. The software screens differ; the logic does not.

    1. Check the hardware first. Controller powered, battery connected, network cable tested, IP address set per the IP schedule. Reader module addressed on the RS-485 bus and terminated correctly at the ends (see P14).

    2. Add the controller in the software by IP or serial number. Wait for it to come online and download. Check its firmware version against the project standard.

    3. Add the reader module(s) and confirm each shows online with no tamper.

    4. Create the door. Assign: reader in (and out, if any), REX input, DPS input, lock output. Set the lock type (fail-safe maglock or fail-secure strike), unlock time and held-open time. Name it exactly as on the door schedule drawing.

    5. Test every state and watch the event log for each: valid card → granted, unlock, relock. Invalid card → denied. REX → unlock (or just shunt the alarm, depending on design). Open without a grant → forced open alarm. Hold open → held-open alarm. Cut or short the supervised inputs → tamper alarm. Fire trip → door releases as designed.

    6. Link cameras if the design calls for it, and check that an alarm on this door pops up the right camera.

    7. Record and hand over. Fill in the test sheet, mark up drawings, back up the database, and label everything.

    Safety

    Doors on escape paths must let people out in an emergency. Fail-safe locks and fire-alarm release are designed under the National Construction Code and Australian standards, and the fire interface is usually provided by the fire contractor as a dry contact. Never bypass a fire release or change a door from fail-safe to fail-secure without the design and approvals. Lock power supplies are 12/24 V DC; their mains input is connected by a licensed electrician.

    The video management system (VMS) model

    A VMS records, manages and shows video from many cameras. Its objects are just as consistent across products:

    PartWhat it does
    Management serverHolds the configuration: cameras, users, rules, licences.
    Recording serversPull streams from cameras and write them to storage. Big sites have many, each handling a few dozen to hundreds of cameras.
    StorageDisks or arrays holding days or weeks of video. Some systems move older video to a cheaper "archive" tier.
    Device driversHow the VMS talks to a camera: a generic ONVIF profile, or a native driver for that camera brand that unlocks more features.
    Recording rulesContinuous recording, recording on motion, or on event (door alarm, analytics), with pre- and post-event buffers. Often a low-resolution stream for live view and a high one for recording.
    RetentionHow long video is kept before it is overwritten, often set by the client's policy (for example 30 days).
    ClientsLive view, playback with timeline search, maps, bookmarks, video wall control.
    User rightsWho can see which cameras, play back, export, or control PTZ cameras.
    Evidence exportSaving a clip for police or insurers in a native format with a player, a watermark, and a digital signature or hash so anyone can prove it was not altered.

    Worked example: storage. 40 cameras recording continuously at 4 Mb/s each, kept 30 days. One camera: 4 Mb/s ÷ 8 = 0.5 MB/s × 86,400 s = 43.2 GB per day. × 30 days = 1.3 TB. × 40 cameras ≈ 52 TB, before RAID overhead. Switching to motion recording that captures about a third of the day cuts that to around 17 TB. The storage maths is in C05.

    On the job

    A very common service call: "camera 23 is offline". The VMS only knows it lost the stream. Work up the chain: is the camera's switch port up and delivering PoE? Can you ping the camera? Did its IP change (DHCP instead of static)? Did someone change its password, so the VMS cannot log in? Did a firmware update break the driver? Network skills from P19 solve most of these.

    Integration: making systems talk

    The value of an enterprise platform is that events in one system drive actions in another. The most common example: an access alarm brings up the right video.

    Other common integrations:

    • Intruder alarm panels: arm and disarm areas from the access system; a valid card at the entry disarms the area.
    • Intercoms: a call from the gate pops up the gate camera and lets the operator unlock the gate.
    • Lifts: the card decides which floor buttons work (destination control), through a lift interface.
    • BMS: occupancy from access events can switch lighting or air conditioning zones (see C01).
    • HR and visitor systems: new staff appear as cardholders automatically, and leavers are disabled the day they leave. This is one of the best security controls of all.
    • Number plate recognition: a plate read becomes a credential for a car park boom gate.

    Licensing, in general terms

    Enterprise platforms are licensed, and the licence often limits what you can add on site. Common patterns (each vendor differs; read the licence summary for the project):

    • A base server licence, plus licences per camera (or per video channel), per door or reader, and sometimes per client workstation.
    • Feature licences for integrations, analytics, mobile credentials or redundancy.
    • An annual maintenance or support agreement that covers software upgrades. If it lapses, the site may not be able to upgrade.
    • Licences tied to a specific server (activation). Moving to new hardware needs a licence transfer.
    Bench tip

    Before adding "just one more camera" or door on a service visit, check that a spare licence exists. Running out of licences is a common reason a new device will not come online, and it is a sales conversation, not a technical one. Tell your supervisor.

    Vendor certification and how you get trained

    Each major vendor runs its own certification program for integrators (the companies that sell, install and service their systems). The general pattern:

    • Courses at more than one level: an installer or technician level (hardware, wiring, adding devices), then an engineer or administrator level (design, servers, integrations, upgrades).
    • Delivered online, in a classroom, or both, often with a practical assessment.
    • Often available only to staff of an authorised integrator or partner, and usually paid for by the employer as part of onboarding.
    • Certifications usually expire and need renewing when major versions change.
    • Some vendors publish free introductory online learning. Check each vendor's website for what is currently open to the public.

    So the practical route is: get hired by an integrator for your general skills, then get certified on their platform. In interviews, saying "I understand the access and VMS model and I am ready to do your vendor certification" is a strong answer.

    In Australia

    In most states, installing, maintaining or advising on security equipment requires a state security licence of the right class, issued by the state regulator (for example the NSW Police Security Licensing & Enforcement Directorate, or Victoria Police Licensing & Regulation Division). Cabling for these systems also needs ACMA cabling registration (see C07). CCTV recording, and especially audio recording, is covered by state surveillance devices laws and privacy law. Rules differ between states and change over time: confirm with your state's security licensing regulator before applying for jobs. See C11.

    Typical interview questions

    "A user says their card doesn't work on one door. How do you approach it?"

    Check the event log first: what reason did the controller log? Unknown card, disabled, outside schedule, no access level for that door, wrong PIN? If there is no event at all, it is likely hardware: reader, wiring, reader module offline, or the controller offline.

    "What causes false 'door forced open' alarms?"

    A faulty or misaligned DPS, a REX that does not register, a mechanical lock that people open with a key, a door that bounces open after closing, or wrong input wiring/EOL values.

    "What happens to the doors if the server goes down?"

    The controllers keep making decisions from their downloaded database and buffer events. New changes on the server do not reach them until it returns.

    "How would you add a camera to a VMS?"

    Set a static IP and strong password on the camera, update firmware per the project standard, set the time source, add it to the VMS by ONVIF or native driver, set streams, recording rules and retention, check the licence, name it per the schedule, and test live and playback.

    "Police ask for footage. What do you do?"

    Follow the client's policy and get authorisation. Export in the native or signed format with a player, include the watermark or hash, note the time, camera and who exported it, and record the handover.

    "Which platforms have you used?"

    Be honest. Explain the concepts you understand, any hands-on experience (even a trial or demo), and that you are ready to complete their vendor certification.

    Check yourself

    Why are cardholders given access levels instead of individual doors?

    Levels model roles. When a role's doors or hours change, you edit one level instead of hundreds of cardholders, and audits can see who has what by role. It reduces mistakes.

    A door shows "forced open" every time someone leaves using the handle. What is the likely fault?

    The REX is not being registered (faulty REX sensor, wiring, or not assigned to the door in the software). The system sees the DPS open without a grant or REX, so it calls it forced. Mechanical exit with no REX on the handle causes the same.

    You remove a leaver's access on the server but their card still works at a remote building. Why?

    The decision is made on the controller from its cached database. If that controller is offline or the download has not finished, it still has the old data. Check the controller's status and force a download.

    Work out storage: 10 cameras at 6 Mb/s, continuous, 14 days.

    6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 MB/s × 86,400 = 64.8 GB/day per camera. × 14 = 907 GB. × 10 ≈ 9.1 TB, plus RAID and spare space.

    Why does an exported clip include a hash or digital signature?

    So anyone can check that the file has not been changed since export. Any edit changes the hash. That supports its use as evidence.

    The alarm pop-up shows video from 40 seconds after the event. What should you check?

    Time synchronisation. The controller, servers and cameras should all use the same NTP source. Also check pre-event buffer settings in the recording rule.

    What is the difference between ONVIF and a native driver?

    ONVIF is a common standard that lets a VMS talk to many brands with core features. A native driver is written for that brand and model and usually exposes more features (analytics events, special stream settings, I/O).
