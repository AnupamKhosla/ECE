Your first day on an Australian site

What actually happens when you turn up: sign-in and induction, the safety paperwork (SWMS, JSAs and Take-5s), PPE, permits, lock-out and danger tags, asbestos, ladders, occupied buildings, site manners, your tool bag, the van and your job notes. Read it before your first day so nothing on site surprises you.

In Australia

Work health and safety (WHS) law is made by each state and territory. Most use the national model WHS laws with local differences; Victoria has its own Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) Act and Regulations. The regulators who enforce them include SafeWork NSW, WorkSafe Victoria, WorkSafe Queensland (Workplace Health and Safety Queensland), SafeWork SA, WorkSafe WA, WorkSafe Tasmania, NT WorkSafe and WorkSafe ACT. Safe Work Australia writes the national model laws and guidance but does not enforce them. On top of the law, every site and every employer adds its own rules. This chapter describes common practice; always follow your site's induction and confirm details with your state regulator, because rules change and differ by state.

Before you arrive

Most first-day problems are made the night before. The site will not wait for you to find a car park.

  • Ask your supervisor five things: the site address and which entrance, the start time, who to report to (name and phone number), what PPE the site requires, and where to park. Sites in cities often have no parking; you may need public transport or a paid car park.
  • Bring your cards: your White Card (general construction induction), driver's licence, and any other tickets you hold (working at heights, EWP, first aid, ACMA cabling registration, security licence if the job needs it). See C11 · Licences, tickets and pathways.
  • Pack food and water for the whole day. Many sites have no shop nearby, and lunch breaks are short.
  • Charge everything: phone, drill batteries, laptop, torch.
  • Plan to arrive 15–20 minutes early. Construction sites commonly start around 7 am; sign-in and induction happen before that.

Sign-in and the site induction

Every site keeps a record of who is on site. On a building site this is usually a sign-in book or an app at the site office. In an occupied building it is often the security desk or loading dock. Sign in every time you arrive and sign out every time you leave. If there is a fire, the sign-in list is how wardens know who might still be inside.

On your first visit to a construction site you do the site-specific induction, run by the principal contractor (the company in charge of the whole site). Expect 20–60 minutes covering:

Your White Card

They check it, and often copy it. No White Card usually means no entry to a construction site. It shows you completed general construction induction training.

Emergencies

Evacuation alarm, assembly point, first aiders, fire wardens, first-aid room, the emergency number (000) and the site address to give.

Site rules

Working hours, PPE, restricted areas, traffic routes for trucks and forklifts, smoking areas, drugs and alcohol policy, and where you may eat.

Paperwork

Which SWMS you must sign onto, which permits exist, and who issues them. You may get a sticker for your hard hat to show you were inducted.

In occupied buildings (offices, hospitals, schools, shopping centres) the induction comes from the building or facilities manager instead. It covers access cards, keys, which lifts and loading docks contractors may use, noise and dust rules, and how to raise a fire-system isolation if your work could set off detectors.

Tip

Take a photo of the emergency plan and the assembly point on your first day, and save the site supervisor's phone number in your phone under the site name. You will need both when you least expect it.

When a SWMS is needed

Under the WHS laws, a SWMS is required before high-risk construction work starts. The list of high-risk categories includes several that ELV technicians meet regularly:

  • work where a person could fall more than 2 metres (ladders to high ceilings, roofs, risers, EWPs);
  • work on or near energised electrical installations or services (for example, cabling in a ceiling next to live mains cables, or in a switch room);
  • work on a telecommunication tower;
  • work likely to involve disturbing asbestos;
  • work in or near a confined space;
  • work in an area with movement of powered mobile plant (forklifts, EWPs, trucks), or next to a road or railway in use;
  • work in an area with artificial extremes of temperature (cool rooms, some plant rooms).

Your employer normally has SWMS templates for routine tasks such as "installing cabling in ceiling spaces" or "working from ladders and platforms". Before you start, read the SWMS for your task, ask about anything you do not understand, and sign it. If the job changes so that the SWMS no longer fits (for example, the ceiling turns out to be 4 m, not 2.7 m), stop and tell your supervisor so the SWMS can be reviewed.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The SWMS is paperwork for the office; signing it is the safety part." Right: the SWMS is the plan for how you will do the job safely. If you do the work differently from the SWMS, you and your employer can both be in breach of the law, and more importantly the controls that were meant to protect you are gone. If the plan is wrong for the real job, the plan gets changed, not ignored.

The hierarchy of controls

Every SWMS, JSA and Take-5 uses the same idea: some controls are much stronger than others. You try the strongest first and use the weaker ones only for what is left.

A Take-5 takes two or three minutes. It is not a test you pass; it is a habit that makes you notice the thing that would have hurt you. Redo it whenever the job changes: you move to a different room, the weather changes, someone starts grinding nearby, or the task turns out to be different.

Safety: the sun

Australia has some of the highest UV levels and skin-cancer rates in the world. UV, not temperature, causes sunburn, so a cool, cloudy day can still burn you. Cancer Council's Slip, Slop, Slap, Seek, Slide is the national habit: slip on sun-protective clothing, slop on SPF 50+ sunscreen (reapply every two hours), slap on a hat, seek shade, slide on sunglasses. Check the UV index on a weather app each morning.

Permits and the person in charge

A permit to work is written permission for a specific risky task, at a specific place, for a limited time, with conditions. It is issued by someone with authority on that site (the site manager, building manager or a nominated permit officer). You keep it at the work area and hand it back when you finish.

Learn who is in charge on each site: the principal contractor's site manager or foreman on a building site, the facilities or building manager in an occupied building, and your own supervisor for your work. Also find out who the first aiders and health and safety representatives (HSRs) are. If you are not sure whether you need a permit, you probably do: ask before you start, not after.

Safety: never drill a slab without permission

Many Australian commercial buildings use post-tensioned concrete slabs: steel cables under very high tension run through the slab. Drilling into one can damage the structure and is dangerous. Never drill or core into a slab, beam or column unless the site has approved it and the location has been scanned. Ask your supervisor first, every time.

Isolation, lock-out and danger tags

Isolation means disconnecting equipment from every energy source (electrical, mechanical, pressure, stored energy) and making sure it cannot be turned back on while someone works on it. Lock-out/tag-out (LOTO) is how that is enforced: a lock that physically stops the switch moving, and a tag that says who is working and why.

Safety: the rules you never break

You never isolate or restore mains (230/400 V) supplies yourself. In Australia that is licensed electrical work. If your 24 V power supply, door controller or camera rack needs its mains supply isolated, your supervisor arranges it with an electrician or through the site's isolation procedure. Never remove, move or ignore someone else's lock or danger tag, even if you are sure the work is finished and even if a supervisor asks you to "just pull it off". Removing another person's tag is a serious offence on any site and can get you removed from it permanently, or someone killed. If a tag seems to have been left behind, report it; there is a formal procedure for that.

For extra-low-voltage work you will still isolate things: you unplug a camera's PoE port, switch off a door controller's 12 V output, or remove a fuse on a 24 V circuit before you work on it. Treat these with the same discipline: know what you switched off, tell the people affected (security staff lose a door, BMS operators lose a plant item), and label it if you leave it. See P01 · The ELV safety line and T09 · Mains, earthing and RCDs.

Asbestos: stop if you suspect it

Asbestos was used widely in Australian buildings until it was banned nationally from 31 December 2003. Buildings built before about 1990 are the most likely to contain it. Its fibres, once released by drilling, cutting or breaking, can cause fatal lung diseases decades later. Undisturbed and intact, it is much less of a risk, so the danger for you is disturbing it without knowing.

Places an ELV technician may find it: fibre-cement sheets ("fibro") in walls, eaves and ceilings; old switchboard and meter-board backing panels; vinyl floor tiles; pipe lagging in plant rooms; textured ceiling coatings; old cable pits and conduits.

  1. Ask for the asbestos register before drilling or cutting in an older building. Workplaces in buildings constructed before 31 December 2003 must have one under the WHS laws.

  2. If you find a material you suspect, stop. Do not drill, cut, break or sweep it. Keep people away.

  3. Tell your supervisor and the person in charge of the site. They arrange testing by a competent person. Work resumes only when it is confirmed safe or removed by a licensed asbestos removalist.

In Australia

Many employers require an asbestos awareness course for technicians working in older buildings. It is short, cheap and worth having on your CV. Removal work needs specific licences; awareness training does not let you remove anything. Check your state regulator's asbestos pages for current rules.

Ladders and working at height

Falls from height are one of the biggest killers in Australian construction, and many of those falls are from low heights, including ladders. ELV work involves a lot of ladder time, so this matters to you every day.

  • Three points of contact while climbing: two hands and a foot, or two feet and a hand. That means you do not carry tools or boxes in your hands up a ladder.
  • Use the right ladder. Industrial-rated (marked with its load rating), not the household ladder from home. Non-conductive (fibreglass) ladders are standard for electrical and ELV work.
  • Inspect it first: feet, rungs, locks and labels. A damaged ladder gets tagged out of service, not used "carefully".
  • Know your site's rule. Some sites require a platform ladder for any ladder work, some limit ladder work to short-duration tasks, and some require a SWMS for any work where you could fall more than 2 m. Over about 2 m your employer may send a mobile scaffold or an EWP instead.
  • Barricade below you in busy areas, so no one walks into the ladder or under falling tools.

The step ladder is usually supplied by your employer or kept in the van. Never borrow another trade's ladder without asking, and never stand on a chair, a bin or a desk "just for a second".

Working in occupied buildings

A lot of ELV work happens in buildings that are open and in use: offices, hospitals, schools, shopping centres, aged care, data centres. The people there did not choose to have you in their space. Your job is to be safe, invisible and tidy.

Hospitals and aged care

Infection control comes first: ceiling access permits, dust barriers, HEPA vacuums, sealed tiles. Quiet near patients. Never block corridors used by beds and trolleys. Ask staff before entering any patient area.

Schools

Sign in at the office every time. Many schools require a Working With Children Check (the rules depend on the state and the work). Noisy or ladder work in corridors is best done during class time, not during breaks. Never leave tools or ladders unattended.

Offices

Work out of hours if the work is noisy or disrupts staff. Protect carpets and furniture with drop sheets. Do not read screens or papers on desks, and do not plug into the client's network unless you are asked to.

Retail and public spaces

Barricades and signs around every ladder. Expect members of the public, including children, to walk right up to your work.

In any occupied building:

  • Ceiling tiles: lift them with clean gloves, put them back in the same place the same way up, and replace any you crack (tell the client). A missing or broken tile looks unprofessional and can affect fire and smoke behaviour.
  • Dust: vacuum as you drill, and clean the floor and desks under your work before you leave.
  • Smoke detectors: dust and drilling can set them off. A false alarm can evacuate a building and fire brigades may charge the owner for attending. Arrange an isolation through the proper channel first; never cover a detector yourself without permission.
  • Security: do not prop open secure doors, and return keys and access cards at the end of every day.

Site etiquette and talking to people

Technical skill gets you hired. Behaviour on site is what gets you asked back. Supervisors notice quickly who is reliable.

Do

  • Arrive early, ready to start at start time.
  • Listen at the toolbox talk or pre-start meeting. Ask a question if something affects your work.
  • Ask before moving another trade's materials, ladders or leads.
  • Keep your area tidy and your leads off walkways.
  • Clean up every day: offcuts, cable boxes, ceiling dust.
  • Say "I don't know, I'll find out" rather than guessing.
  • Tell your supervisor early about problems: a clash, a missing part, a drawing that doesn't match the site.

Don't

  • Use another trade's tools or materials without asking.
  • Cut, move or "borrow" cable, containment or fixings that belong to someone else.
  • Argue with the client about the design: note it, and let your supervisor raise it.
  • Make promises to the client about dates or extra work.
  • Use your phone in the middle of a walkway or on a ladder.
  • Leave the site without signing out and telling your supervisor.

Talking to the supervisor and the client

When you report a problem, use a simple structure: what I found, where, what it affects, what I suggest. For example: "The camera position on level 1, near grid C7, clashes with a sprinkler main. It would need to move about 1.2 m east. I've taken photos. Do you want me to move it and mark it up, or wait for the designer?" That message takes 20 seconds and saves an hour of confusion. It also makes your as-built markup easy (see P22 · Reading drawings).

With the client or building staff, be polite and brief. Tell them what you are doing and roughly how long you will be, especially if a door, camera, lift or air-conditioning zone will be out of action. Pass any request for changes or extra work to your supervisor.

On the job

Apprentices and new technicians are often judged in their first two weeks on four things: being on time, staying off the phone, cleaning up without being asked, and asking good questions. None of these need technical knowledge, and all of them are noticed.

The van, stock and paperwork

The van

If you drive a company van, it is your mobile workshop and a big responsibility.

  • Walk around it before driving: tyres, lights, loads secured. Heavy cable drums and ladders must be tied down; in a crash, loose items become projectiles.
  • Lock it every time and do not leave tools, laptops or meters visible. Tool theft from vans is common.
  • Stock: learn what the van should carry (cable, connectors, ties, fixings, common spare parts). Tell the office when you use the last of something. Record parts used on each job so they can be billed.
  • Fines and logbooks: follow the company's rules for vehicle use, fuel cards and logbooks.

Your timesheet is how the company bills the client and how you get paid correctly. Your job notes are the record of what you did. Both are part of the job, not extras.

Heat, hydration and fatigue

Australian summers make roofs, ceiling spaces and plant rooms dangerously hot. A ceiling space under a metal roof can be far hotter than the outside air. Heat illness builds up gradually, and the first thing it damages is your judgement, which is why you must plan for it before you feel it.

Heat exhaustion

Heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps, tiredness. Action: stop work, move to shade or air-conditioning, drink water, cool down, tell someone. Do not go back into the heat that day without checking with your supervisor.

Heat stroke (medical emergency)

Confusion, slurred speech, collapse, fitting, very high body temperature; the skin may be hot and dry or still sweating. Action: call 000, cool the person with water and air flow, and stay with them.

  • Drink before you are thirsty: small amounts often through the day. Pale urine is a good sign.
  • Schedule the hot work early. Roof and ceiling work in the morning; inside or shaded work in the afternoon.
  • Take breaks in the cool, and keep an eye on each other. People with heat illness often do not notice it in themselves.
  • Fatigue: early starts, long drives and overtime add up. Tired technicians make wiring mistakes and fall off ladders. If you are too tired to drive or work safely, say so.
In Australia

Safe Work Australia and the state regulators publish guidance on working in heat; some sites and agreements have heat policies that change work hours on very hot days. Ask about your site's heat policy in the induction. Noise is regulated too: the workplace exposure standard is an average of 85 dB(A) over 8 hours or a peak of 140 dB(C), and your employer must manage exposure above it. Confirm current details with your state regulator.

    Check yourself

    What is the difference between a SWMS and a Take-5?

    A SWMS is a written plan required by WHS laws for high-risk construction work (such as work where you could fall more than 2 m, or near energised electrical services). The employer prepares it with the workers, and the work must follow it. A Take-5 is a short personal pre-start check that you do yourself before each task and whenever conditions change. It is a company tool, not a legal form, and it does not replace the SWMS.

    Your 24 V door controller needs its mains supply isolated. Who does it, and what is your part?

    A licensed electrician (or the site's authorised isolation procedure) isolates mains. You ask your supervisor to arrange it. If the site uses group isolation, you fit your own personal lock and danger tag to the hasp, and remove only your own when your work is finished. You never operate the mains isolator yourself.

    At the end of the day you notice another worker's danger tag still on an isolator, and the work looks finished. What do you do?

    Leave it exactly where it is and report it to your supervisor or the site manager. Only the person named on the tag removes it, or the site follows its formal procedure for an abandoned tag. Removing someone else's tag could restore power to a person still working on that equipment.

    You are about to drill into fibre-cement sheeting in a 1975 building. What should you do first?

    Ask for the asbestos register. Buildings from before 1990 commonly contain asbestos, and workplaces built before 31 December 2003 must have a register. If the material is not identified as safe, stop, keep people away, and tell your supervisor so it can be tested. Never drill or cut a suspected asbestos material.

    An extension ladder must reach a gutter 4 m above the ground. Where do you put its foot, and how high should it extend?

    About 1 m out from the wall (the 4:1 ratio), extending about 1 m above the landing so you have something to hold as you step off, and secured at the top. Climb with three points of contact, tools in a belt or hauled up.

    Why is PPE called the "last line of defence"?

    Because it only protects the person wearing it, only if it fits and is worn correctly, and it does nothing to remove the hazard. The hierarchy of controls says to eliminate the hazard first, then substitute, isolate or engineer it out, then use administrative controls, and only then rely on PPE for what remains.

    You need to drill near smoke detectors in an occupied office. What do you arrange first, and why?

    A fire-system (detector) isolation through the building or facilities manager or the fire contractor, following the site's procedure. Drilling dust can trigger detectors, which can evacuate the building and bring the fire brigade, and the owner may be charged for the call-out. You never cover or disable a detector yourself without permission, and the isolation must be restored when you finish.

    It is 2 pm, 37 °C, and your workmate in the ceiling space becomes confused and slurs his words. What do you do?

    Treat it as possible heat stroke, a medical emergency. Get him out of the heat if it is safe, call 000, cool him with water and air flow, and stay with him. Tell the site first aider and supervisor. Confusion means his body is losing control of its temperature; do not let him "rest and carry on".
