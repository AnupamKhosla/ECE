Maths toolkit: the school maths electronics uses

You did all of this maths in Class 12, but that was a long time ago. This chapter brings back only the parts a technician uses every day: prefixes, powers of ten, rearranging formulas, percentages, graphs, exponentials, logs and decibels, sine waves and square roots. Every idea comes with a real circuit and real numbers, so you can see why it matters.

SI units and prefixes

Intuition

Electronics covers a huge range of sizes. A capacitor can be 10 pF (ten million-millionths of a farad). A power station makes about 1 GW (one thousand million watts). Writing all those zeros is slow and easy to get wrong. So we use prefixes: a letter in front of the unit that means "multiply by this power of ten".

The SI system (Système International, the metric system) gives each quantity one base unit: volt (V), ampere (A), ohm (Ω), farad (F), henry (H), watt (W), hertz (Hz), second (s). The prefixes we use most go up and down in steps of 1000.

The rule

Two details catch people. First, case matters: m is milli (one thousandth), M is mega (one million). A 1 mΩ resistance and a 1 MΩ resistance differ by a factor of a thousand million. Second, µ (the Greek letter mu) is often typed as u when the keyboard has no µ. So "100uF" on a parts list means 100 µF.

Component markings also hide the decimal point inside the prefix. 4k7 means 4.7 kΩ. 2R2 means 2.2 Ω (R stands for the decimal point). 4n7 means 4.7 nF. This stops a tiny printed dot from being lost.

Circuit example

A schematic asks for a 4700 pF capacitor. The shop lists 4.7 nF. The old datasheet says 0.0047 µF. Are they the same part?

  • 4700 pF → one step up the ladder (÷1000) → 4.7 nF.
  • 4.7 nF → one more step up (÷1000) → 0.0047 µF.

Yes, all three are the same value. Small ceramic capacitors print a three-digit code: the first two digits, then the number of zeros, in picofarads. So 4700 pF is printed 472 (47 followed by 2 zeros). A 100 nF capacitor is 100 000 pF, printed 104.

Scientific and engineering notation

Intuition

Prefixes are words. Powers of ten are the same idea written as maths. 10³ means 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000. 10⁻³ means 1 ÷ 1000 = 0.001. The small number up high (the exponent) counts how many places the decimal point moves: positive to the right, negative to the left.

The rule

  • Scientific notation: one digit before the decimal point, then × 10 to some power. 4700 = 4.7 × 10³. 0.000 022 = 2.2 × 10⁻⁵.
  • Engineering notation: the exponent must be a multiple of 3, so it matches a prefix. 0.000 022 = 22 × 10⁻⁶ = 22 µ. This is the one electronics people use, because you can read the prefix straight off it.
  • Multiplying: multiply the front numbers, add the exponents. (2 × 10⁻³) × (4.7 × 10³) = 9.4 × 10⁰ = 9.4. That is 2 mA × 4.7 kΩ = 9.4 V. Notice that milli and kilo cancel. This is why "mA × kΩ = V" works.
  • Dividing: divide the front numbers, subtract the exponents. 5 V ÷ (10 × 10³ Ω) = 0.5 × 10⁻³ A = 0.5 mA.

Typing it on a calculator

A scientific calculator has a key marked ×10ˣ (on older models EXP or EE). It means "× 10 to the power of", in one key. To enter 4.7 nF (4.7 × 10⁻⁹):

4.7 [×10ˣ] [(−)] 9 Use the (−) negative key for the exponent, not the minus (subtract) key. The screen may show 4.7E-9 or 4.7×10⁻⁹. Both mean the same thing.

Now the trap. To enter 1 kΩ (1 × 10³) you type 1 ×10ˣ 3. Many people type 10 ×10ˣ 3, because they think "ten to the three". That is 10 × 10³ = 10 000, which is ten times too big. The key already contains the "× 10".

Most Casio-style calculators also have an ENG key. Press it after an answer and the display shifts to engineering notation. An answer of 0.0047 becomes 4.7×10⁻³, which you read straight off as 4.7 m (milli). Press it again (or SHIFT + ENG) to move to the next prefix.

Circuit example

An RC timing circuit uses R = 47 kΩ and C = 220 nF. The time constant is τ = R × C (you will meet it properly below).

τ = 47 × 10³ × 220 × 10⁻⁹ = (47 × 220) × 10³⁻⁹ = 10 340 × 10⁻⁶ s ≈ 10.3 ms On the calculator: 47 ×10ˣ 3 × 220 ×10ˣ (−)9 = 0.01034. Press ENG: 10.34×10⁻³, which is 10.34 milliseconds.

Significant figures and sensible rounding

Intuition

A calculator happily gives you 7.575757576 mA. But your resistor is only accurate to 5%, and your meter to about 1%. Most of those digits are noise. Writing them down pretends you know something you do not. Significant figures are the digits that carry real information.

The rule

  • Count from the first non-zero digit. 4.7 has 2 significant figures. 0.0047 also has 2 (leading zeros only place the decimal point). 4.70 has 3 (the trailing zero says "I measured this place too").
  • Keep all the digits during a calculation. Round only the final answer.
  • Give the answer about as many significant figures as your least precise input. For bench work, 2 or 3 significant figures is almost always right.
  • When you choose a real part, round to the nearest standard value. Resistors come in series such as E12: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82 (then ×10, ×100 and so on). E24 adds values in between. For an LED resistor, round up: a bit less current is always safe.

Circuit example: how good is a meter reading?

Your multimeter shows 12.37 V across a 12 V battery. The manual says DC volts accuracy is ±(0.5% + 2 counts). A "count" is one step of the last digit, here 0.01 V.

0.5% of 12.37 = 0.062 V; 2 counts = 0.02 V; total ≈ ±0.08 V The real voltage is somewhere between about 12.29 V and 12.45 V. So 12.37 V and 12.40 V from two different meters do not disagree. Do not read meaning into the last digit.

Rearranging formulas

Intuition

A formula is a balance. Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other, and it stays true. To "make R the subject" you undo the operations around R one at a time, in reverse order, doing the same to both sides.

Ohm's law three ways

Ohm's law says V = I × R: the voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance. Divide both sides by R and you get I = V / R. Divide both sides of the original by I and you get R = V / I.

Common misconception

"I just need the triangle." The triangle only works for three quantities linked by one multiplication. The very next formula you meet, the LED resistor, has a subtraction in it, and the triangle cannot rearrange it. Algebra (do the same thing to both sides) works for every formula. Use the triangle as a quick check, and use algebra to actually rearrange.

Harder example 1: the LED resistor

An LED in series with a resistor sits across a supply. The LED takes a nearly fixed voltage VF (its forward voltage). The resistor takes the rest. So the resistor's voltage is VS − VF, and Ohm's law on the resistor gives:

I = (VS − VF) / R → multiply both sides by R: I × R = VS − VF → divide by I: R = (VS − VF) / I VS = supply voltage, VF = LED forward voltage (about 2.0 V for red, about 3.0 V for blue or white), I = the LED current you want.

Numbers. A red indicator LED on a 12 V panel, at 10 mA: R = (12 − 2.0) / 0.010 = 1000 Ω. Use 1 kΩ. Check the resistor's power: P = I² × R = 0.010² × 1000 = 0.1 W, so a common ¼ W (0.25 W) resistor is fine.

Harder example 2: the 555 timer and a voltage divider

A 555 timer in one-shot (monostable) mode stays on for t = 1.1 × R × C. You want 5 seconds and you have a 100 µF capacitor. Divide both sides by 1.1 × C:

R = t / (1.1 × C) = 5 / (1.1 × 100 × 10⁻⁶) = 45 455 Ω ≈ 45 kΩ The nearest E12 value is 47 kΩ, which gives 1.1 × 47 000 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 5.2 s. Electrolytic capacitors are often ±20%, so the real time could be anywhere from about 4.1 s to 6.2 s anyway.

A voltage divider scales a 12 V signal down to a microcontroller pin: Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2). You pick R2 = 10 kΩ and want Vout = 3.3 V. Solve for R1: multiply both sides by (R1 + R2), divide by Vout, then subtract R2.

R1 = R2 × (Vin − Vout) / Vout = 10 k × (12 − 3.3) / 3.3 = 26.4 kΩ → use 27 kΩ (gives 3.24 V) Rounding R1 up makes Vout slightly lower, which is the safe direction for a 3.3 V input.

Harder example 3: the RC formula

A capacitor charging through a resistor from a supply VS reaches the voltage V = VS(1 − e−t/RC) after time t. (The next sections explain e and ln.) How long until it reaches a chosen voltage V? Undo the operations in reverse order:

V / VS = 1 − e−t/RC → e−t/RC = 1 − V/VS → −t/RC = ln(1 − V/VS) → t = RC × ln( VS / (VS − V) ) The step from ex to x is "take the natural log (ln) of both sides", because ln undoes ex, just as division undoes multiplication.

Numbers. R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 µF, so RC = 1.0 s. An Arduino running at 5 V reads a pin as HIGH above about 3.0 V. Time for the capacitor to get there: t = 1.0 × ln(5 / 2) = 1.0 × 0.916 = 0.92 s.

Ratios, percentages and tolerance

Intuition

A ratio compares two things of the same kind: "this is three times that". A percentage is a ratio out of 100. In electronics they answer questions like "how far off is this part allowed to be?", "how much of my voltage did the cable eat?" and "how much power does this converter waste?"

The rule

  • To use a percentage, turn it into a decimal: 5% = 5/100 = 0.05. "5% of 1000" = 0.05 × 1000 = 50.
  • Increase by 10% = multiply by 1.10. Decrease by 10% = multiply by 0.90.
  • Percentage change = (new − old) / old × 100%.
  • Efficiency = power out / power in × 100%.

Circuit examples

Tolerance. A 1 kΩ resistor marked ±5% (gold band) may be anywhere from 1000 × 0.95 = 950 Ω to 1000 × 1.05 = 1050 Ω, and it is still a good part. If your meter reads 1012 Ω, nothing is wrong. A 4.7 kΩ ±1% resistor (brown band) is 4653–4747 Ω.

Cable volt drop. A 12 V supply feeds a camera at the end of a long cable. The camera sees 10.8 V. The drop is 12 − 10.8 = 1.2 V, and 1.2 / 12 × 100% = 10% of the supply is lost in the cable. Many 12 V devices accept about ±10%, so this camera is right at the edge.

Efficiency. A buck converter takes 24 V at 0.25 A (6.0 W) from a plant-room supply and delivers 5 V at 1.0 A (5.0 W) to a Raspberry Pi. Efficiency = 5.0 / 6.0 = 83%. The missing 1.0 W turns into heat in the converter.

Common misconception

"Down 10% then up 10% gets you back where you started." It does not. 100 × 0.9 = 90, then 90 × 1.1 = 99. Each percentage is "of" a different starting number. This is why tolerances and volt drops along a chain must be worked out step by step, not added in your head.

Straight-line graphs and log scales

Intuition

A graph turns a formula into a picture. For a resistor, plot current (I) up the side against voltage (V) along the bottom. Double the voltage and the current doubles, so the points sit on a straight line through zero. A steep line means a lot of current for a little voltage: a small resistance.

The rule

A straight line is y = m x + c. The slope m = rise / run = Δy / Δx (Δ, "delta", means "change in"). c is where it crosses the vertical axis. For a resistor, I = (1/R) × V + 0, so the slope of an I–V graph is 1/R and it passes through zero.

An LED's I–V graph is not a straight line. It stays near zero until about 1.8 V, then shoots up. That curve is why an LED needs a series resistor: a tiny change in voltage makes a huge change in current. See T11 and the p–n junction deep dive.

Reading log-scale graphs

Datasheets often use a logarithmic (log) scale. On a log axis, each equal step multiplies by 10 instead of adding a fixed amount: 10, 100, 1000, 10 000. This lets one graph show 10 Hz and 100 kHz together, and the shape of the curve stays readable at both ends. You meet log axes in filter and amplifier frequency responses, thermistor resistance tables, cable attenuation charts and battery discharge curves.

To read a value between grid lines on a log axis, measure what fraction p of the way across the decade it sits. The value is (start of decade) × 10p. Halfway between 1 kHz and 10 kHz is 1 k × 100.5 = 3.16 kHz, not 5.5 kHz. Halfway on a log scale is a multiplying halfway (×3.16 then ×3.16 again), not an adding halfway.

Exponentials and the number e

Intuition

Charge a capacitor through a resistor. At the start the capacitor is empty, the full supply voltage sits across the resistor, and a big current flows. As the capacitor fills, its voltage rises, the voltage left across the resistor shrinks, and so the current shrinks too. The closer you get to full, the slower you go. You approach the supply voltage forever and never quite arrive.

The key fact: the speed at which the gap closes is proportional to the size of the gap. Anything that behaves like that follows an exponential curve, and the natural base for that curve is the number e ≈ 2.718. Radioactive decay, a hot soldering iron cooling, a capacitor discharging and a thermistor warming up all follow the same shape.

The rule

Call the gap g = VS − v. The current is g / R, and current fills the capacitor at dv/dt = i / C. So dg/dt = −g / (RC). The function whose rate of change is proportional to itself is the exponential, so g = VS e−t/RC, which gives:

Charging: v = VS (1 − e−t/τ) Discharging: v = V0 e−t/τ τ = R × C τ (tau) is the time constant, in seconds when R is in ohms and C in farads. Every τ, the remaining gap shrinks to 1/e = 37% of what it was. So after 1τ you are 63% of the way there.

R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 µF, VS = 5 V. τ = 10 000 × 0.000 1 = 1.0 s.

Logarithms and decibels

Intuition

A logarithm answers the question "how many times do I multiply?". log10(1000) = 3, because 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000. log10(2) = 0.301, because 100.301 = 2. The natural log, ln, asks the same question with e instead of 10.

The magic property is this: logs turn multiplying into adding. log(a × b) = log a + log b. A signal that passes through an amplifier (×100 power), then a long cable (×0.25), then a splitter (×0.5) ends up at 100 × 0.25 × 0.5 = 12.5 times the original power. Multiplying a chain of ratios is awkward in your head. Adding them is easy. That is the whole reason the decibel exists.

The rule

Power: dB = 10 × log10(P2 / P1) Voltage or current: dB = 20 × log10(V2 / V1) Voltage uses 20 because power goes as voltage squared (P = V² / R), and log(x²) = 2 log x. So a voltage ratio and the matching power ratio give the same number of dB. Positive dB is a gain; negative dB is a loss.

Two numbers to memorise: ×2 power = +3 dB and ×10 power = +10 dB. From those you can build almost anything in your head. ×4 = ×2 × 2 = 3 + 3 = 6 dB. ×20 = ×10 × 2 = 13 dB. ×1000 = 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 dB.

dBm is an absolute power: decibels compared with 1 milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW, +20 dBm = 100 mW, +30 dBm = 1 W. A Wi-Fi signal at −65 dBm is 10−6.5 mW, about 0.3 nanowatts. Fibre and wireless technicians talk in dBm all day.

Circuit example: a fibre loss budget

A fibre link is 2 km long. Using common planning values (check the real figures on the job's specification): fibre loss 0.35 dB per km, 0.5 dB per connector pair, 0.1 dB per splice. With 2 connector pairs and 4 splices:

Loss = 2 × 0.35 + 2 × 0.5 + 4 × 0.1 = 0.7 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 2.1 dB If the transmitter launches −3 dBm, the receiver should see about −3 − 2.1 = −5.1 dBm. 2.1 dB of loss means 10−0.21 = 0.62, so 62% of the light arrives. Adding was much easier than multiplying 0.92 × 0.89 × 0.89 × …

Trigonometry for AC

Intuition

Imagine a point going round a circle of radius 1 at a steady speed. Watch only its height. It rises from zero to the top, falls back through zero to the bottom, and rises again. Plot that height against time and you get a sine wave. An AC generator does exactly this: a coil spins in a magnetic field, and the voltage it makes follows the height of the spinning coil. That is why mains power, and the output of every AC plug-pack, is a sine wave.

The rule

  • sin θ is the height of the point on a circle of radius 1 at angle θ. cos θ is its sideways position.
  • Angles come in degrees (a full turn is 360°) or radians (a full turn is 2π ≈ 6.283 rad). 1 rad ≈ 57.3°. Radians measure the angle by the length of arc on a radius-1 circle, which is why the maths of waves uses them.
  • A sine-wave voltage is v(t) = Vp sin(ωt + φ). Vp is the peak voltage. ω (omega) is the angular frequency in rad/s: ω = 2πf, where f is the frequency in hertz (turns per second). φ (phi) is the phase angle: how far ahead or behind this wave is compared with another.
  • Period T = 1 / f. For 50 Hz mains, T = 20 ms and ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad/s. A phase shift of 90° is a quarter of a period: 5 ms at 50 Hz.
Calculator trap

Your calculator has a degree mode (D on screen) and a radian mode (R). sin(30) is 0.5 in degree mode and −0.988 in radian mode. If a trig answer looks strange, check the mode first. Use radians whenever ωt is involved, because ω is in radians per second.

Square roots: RMS and √3

Intuition

An AC voltage is always changing, so which number do you quote? We quote the one that gives the same heating as DC. A heater on 230 V AC gets just as hot as it would on 230 V DC. That "equivalent heating" value is the RMS (root mean square) value: square the wave, take the mean (average), then take the square root. Squaring is used because heating goes as V².

The rule

For a sine wave: VRMS = Vp / √2 ≈ 0.707 × Vp Vp = √2 × VRMS ≈ 1.414 × VRMS This √2 is only for sine waves. A square wave that switches between +V and −V has RMS = V. Other shapes need a "true RMS" meter.

Circuit examples

  • 24 V AC plug-pack: peak = 1.414 × 24 = 33.9 V. Peak to peak (top to bottom) = 67.9 V. Rectify and smooth it with a bridge and a capacitor and you get about 33.9 − 1.4 (two diode drops) ≈ 32 V DC, not 24 V. This surprises everyone the first time; see P06. Choose a capacitor rated well above 35 V.
  • Mains (theory only): 230 V RMS has a peak of 325 V. Australian low-voltage supply uses 230 V between each phase and neutral. Three phases are 120° apart, and the voltage between two phases is √3 times bigger: 230 × 1.732 = 398 V, quoted as 400 V. This is why you hear "230/400 V". All mains work in Australia is legally restricted to licensed electricians. You learn the numbers to understand drawings and talk to electricians, not to touch it.
  • Adding noise: two independent noise sources of 3 µV and 4 µV RMS do not add to 7 µV. Random signals add as root-sum-of-squares: √(3² + 4²) = 5 µV.
In Australia

The nominal supply is 230 V, 50 Hz (AS 60038), with an allowed range commonly quoted as +10% / −6% (about 216–253 V). Measuring mains is electrical work: in Australia it is limited to licensed electricians, regulated by the electrical safety regulator in your state or territory. Confirm the current rules with that regulator, because they change and differ between states.

Vectors and phasors, in brief

Intuition

A vector is an arrow: it has a length and a direction. A phasor is the arrow from the unit-circle widget, frozen at one moment. Its length is the amplitude of an AC wave and its angle is the wave's phase. Two AC signals of the same frequency add like arrows, not like plain numbers, because their peaks happen at different times.

The rule

In a resistor, voltage and current are in step (0°). In a capacitor, the voltage lags the current by 90°. So in a series R–C circuit, the resistor's voltage and the capacitor's voltage are at right angles. Use Pythagoras for the length and trigonometry for the angle:

Z = √(R² + X²) φ = tan⁻¹(X / R) power factor = cos φ = R / Z Z is the impedance (total opposition to AC, in ohms). X is the reactance of the capacitor or inductor. For a capacitor XC = 1 / (2πfC).
Try this

With a 12 V AC plug-pack (ELV only), put a 1.5 kΩ resistor in series with a 2.2 µF film (non-polarised) capacitor rated 63 V or more. At 50 Hz, XC = 1 / (2π × 50 × 2.2 µF) ≈ 1.45 kΩ. Measure the AC voltage across the resistor, then across the capacitor. You should read roughly 8.6 V and 8.3 V, which add to about 17 V, yet the supply is only 12 V. Square them, add, and take the square root: about 12 V. (Plug-packs read a little high with a light load, so expect the numbers to scale up slightly.)

Estimation and sanity checks

Intuition

The most useful maths skill on the job is not exact calculation. It is knowing roughly what the answer should be before you calculate or measure, so you notice at once when something is wrong. A wrong meter range, a misread colour band or a slipped prefix gives an answer that is 1000 times off. A quick estimate catches it.

The rules of thumb

  • volts ÷ kΩ = mA. 5 V across 10 kΩ = 0.5 mA. 12 V across 1 kΩ = 12 mA.
  • mA × kΩ = V. 4 mA through 250 Ω (0.25 kΩ) = 1 V. That is the bottom of a 4–20 mA loop read across a 250 Ω resistor.
  • V × mA = mW. 12 V × 10 mA = 120 mW.
  • Know typical sizes: an indicator LED runs at 2–20 mA; an Arduino pin should stay below about 20 mA; a small 12 V CCTV camera draws a few hundred mA (more at night with infrared on); a ¼ W resistor must stay below 250 mW.
  • Check direction: if R goes up, I should go down. If a cable gets longer, the volt drop should go up.

Circuit example: is 30 A through a 330 Ω resistor plausible?

A classmate's notes say "30 A through the 330 Ω resistor". Check it:

V = I × R = 30 × 330 = 9900 V P = I² × R = 30² × 330 = 297 000 W Nearly 10 kV and 300 kW, through a part the size of a grain of rice, on a bench powered by a 12 V plug-pack. Impossible. The note almost certainly meant 30 mA.

Now check 30 mA: V = 0.030 × 330 = 9.9 V. Plausible on a 12 V supply. But P = 0.030² × 330 = 0.30 W, which is more than a ¼ W resistor can take. So even the corrected version found a real problem: the resistor will run hot and should be ½ W. The sanity check was worth doing twice.

Cable estimate. Copper of 0.5 mm² cross-section has about 0.034 Ω per metre (resistivity of copper ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, divided by 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ m²). A camera 50 m away needs 100 m of conductor (there and back): 3.4 Ω. At 0.5 A, that drops 1.7 V. So a 12 V supply delivers about 10.3 V to the camera. You can work this out in your head before you buy the cable, and it tells you to use thicker cable or a higher supply voltage. Fault-finding in P21 starts with exactly this kind of estimate.

On the job

On an Australian site nobody will ask you to integrate anything. They will ask "why is the camera at the end of the car park rebooting?", "is this 4–20 mA reading right?", "will this 24 V supply run eight more door strikes?" or "how much fibre loss should this link have?". Each one is prefixes, Ohm's law, a percentage, a dB sum or a quick estimate. Being fast and right at this level is what makes a technician trusted.

Check yourself

A capacitor is marked 223. What is it in pF, nF and µF?

22 followed by 3 zeros = 22 000 pF. One step up the ladder (÷1000) is 22 nF, and one more is 0.022 µF. All three are the same capacitor.

You want to enter 2.2 kΩ on a calculator. Why is "22 ×10ˣ 2" correct but "10 ×10ˣ 3" wrong for 1 kΩ?

The ×10ˣ key means "times ten to the power of". 22 × 10² = 2200, which is right. "10 ×10ˣ 3" means 10 × 10³ = 10 000, which is ten times too big. For 1 kΩ, type 1 ×10ˣ 3.

A 12 V, 5 W lamp is running. What is its resistance while hot? Which formula do you rearrange?

P = V² / R. Multiply both sides by R, then divide by P: R = V² / P = 144 / 5 = 28.8 Ω. (Measured cold with a meter it will read much lower, because a filament's resistance rises a lot with temperature.)

What range of values is a 4.7 kΩ ±1% resistor allowed to have?

1% of 4700 is 47. So 4700 − 47 = 4653 Ω up to 4700 + 47 = 4747 Ω. A reading of 4.71 kΩ is fine.

On a log axis, what frequency sits exactly halfway between 100 Hz and 1 kHz?

Halfway on a log scale is half a decade: 100 × 100.5 = 316 Hz. Not 550 Hz. On a log axis, equal distances mean equal multiplications.

An amplifier gives +20 dB of power gain, then a cable loses 6 dB. What is the overall power ratio?

Add the dB: 20 − 6 = +14 dB. Then convert: 10 dB is ×10, and 4 dB is about ×2.5 (3 dB = ×2, a little more). So about ×25. Exactly: 101.4 = 25.1.

R = 47 kΩ and C = 10 µF. What is the time constant, and roughly how long until the capacitor is "fully" charged?

τ = 47 000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.47 s. "Fully" (99.3%) is 5τ ≈ 2.4 s. After 1τ (0.47 s) it is at 63% of the supply.

Your meter says 0.25 A flows through a 10 kΩ resistor on a 5 V Arduino circuit. Do you believe it?

No. The most current 5 V can push through 10 kΩ is 5 / 10 000 = 0.5 mA. 0.25 A is 500 times more. Either the reading is really 0.25 mA (check the range and the unit on the display), the resistor is not 10 kΩ, or the meter is measuring something else. Estimate first, then trust the meter only if it agrees.

Skills checklist

  • I can convert between p, n, µ, m, k, M and G without a calculator, and read 4k7, 2R2 and a 3-digit capacitor code.
  • I can enter and read scientific and engineering notation on my calculator, using the ×10ˣ and ENG keys correctly.
  • I round final answers to 2–3 significant figures and pick the nearest standard (E12) part.
  • I can rearrange Ohm's law, the LED resistor formula, a voltage divider and the RC charging formula by algebra.
  • I can work out a tolerance range, a percentage volt drop and an efficiency.
  • I can find R from the slope of an I–V graph and read a value between grid lines on a log axis.
  • I know what τ = RC means and the 63% / 5τ rules.
  • I can convert power and voltage ratios to dB and back, and add up a simple loss budget.
  • I can use ω = 2πf, switch between degrees and radians, and convert RMS to peak for a sine wave.
  • I estimate before I measure, and I catch answers that are 1000 times off.
