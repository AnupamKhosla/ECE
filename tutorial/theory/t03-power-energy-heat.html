Power, energy and heat

Where the energy in a circuit goes, how fast it goes there, and how to stop it cooking your parts. You will be able to size a resistor, read a power bill, explain why power lines run at hundreds of kilovolts, and work out how long a battery will last.

Reading 45 minLevel FoundationMaths Algebra, one derivativePractice P02, P10, P13

How we found out

In the 1700s James Watt improved the steam engine, and to sell it he compared it with horses: one "horsepower" was the rate at which a horse could keep lifting a load. The idea of power as a rate of doing work comes from that practical question. The SI unit of power, the watt, was later named after him.

In 1841 James Prescott Joule, the son of a Manchester brewer, put coils of wire in water and measured how fast the water warmed. He found that the heat produced was proportional to the square of the current, times the resistance, times the time. Over the next few years he showed, with a falling weight turning a paddle in water, that a fixed amount of mechanical work always made the same amount of heat. Heat was not a separate fluid ("caloric") as many believed; it was another form of energy. This was a major step towards the law of conservation of energy. The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him.

Fifty years later, power engineering became a business battle. Thomas Edison's first central power station in New York (1882) supplied direct current (DC) at about 110 V. At that voltage, the I²R loss in the cables was so large that customers had to be within about a kilometre or two of the station. George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla backed alternating current (AC), because transformers can step AC up to high voltage for transmission and back down for use. In 1896 AC power was sent about 35 km from Niagara Falls to the city of Buffalo, and AC won the grid. The reason is one formula from this chapter: Ploss = I²R.

Power: energy per second

Energy (W or E, in joules, J) is the capacity to do work or make heat. Power (P, in watts, W) is the rate at which energy is transferred: 1 W = 1 J/s. A 10 W LED lamp turns 10 joules of electrical energy into light and heat every second.

Deriving P = VI

Put together the two definitions from T01:

  • Voltage is energy per charge: V = W/Q (joules per coulomb).
  • Current is charge per second: I = Q/t (coulombs per second).

Multiply them: (joules per coulomb) × (coulombs per second) = joules per second. Using calculus for a moment: the energy delivered when a small charge dQ passes through a voltage V is dW = V dQ. Divide by the small time dt:

P = dW/dt = V × dQ/dt = V × I This works for any part: resistor, motor, LED, battery charging or discharging. V is the voltage across the part and I the current through it.

For a resistor, Ohm's law V = IR lets us write the same power two more ways:

P = V I = I² R = V² / R Use I²R when you know the current (for example, a cable carrying a known load current). Use V²/R when you know the voltage across the resistor (for example, a resistor straight across a supply).

Each form tells a different story. I²R says: for a fixed current, more resistance means more heat, which is why a bad crimp or loose screw terminal (a few extra milliohms in a high-current path) can glow. V²/R says: for a fixed voltage, less resistance means more heat, which is why a short circuit across a battery is so violent.

Where the energy goes in a resistor

Inside a resistor, the field accelerates each electron between collisions (T02). At each collision the electron gives its extra energy to the vibrating atoms of the material. Faster vibrating atoms are what "hotter" means. So electrical energy becomes heat, at a rate of exactly I²R. This is Joule heating (or ohmic heating). It is the useful part of a kettle, toaster or heater, and the wasted part of everything else.

Heat, temperature and power ratings

Power tells you how fast heat is made. Temperature depends on how fast that heat can escape. A part gets hotter until heat flows out to the air as fast as it is made. A simple model is

ΔT = P × θ ΔT is the temperature rise above the surrounding air, in °C. θ (theta) is the thermal resistance from the part to the air, in °C per watt. Small parts and parts in still air have large θ; heatsinks and fans make θ smaller.

Example: a regulator in a TO-220 package (the common three-legged package with a metal tab) has a thermal resistance to air of roughly 50–65 °C/W without a heatsink. In Figure 1 it must lose 7 W. ΔT ≈ 7 × 50 = 350 °C. Long before that, its built-in thermal protection shuts it down. It needs a large heatsink, or better, a buck converter (P10).

Resistor power ratings

Every resistor has a power rating: the power it can turn into heat continuously, at a stated surrounding (ambient) temperature, without damage. Common ratings: 1/16 W (0402 surface-mount), 1/10 W (0603), 1/8 W (0805), 1/4 W (1206 and the usual through-hole resistor), 1/2 W, 1 W, 2 W, and 5 W or more for ceramic-cased wirewound types. The rating depends on size: a bigger body has more surface to lose heat from.

Two practical facts about the rating:

  • At its full rating, a resistor is already very hot. A small resistor at its rated power can run at well over 100 °C: hot enough to brown a circuit board and burn your finger. Designers keep it cooler by choosing a rating at least twice the actual power.
  • The rating falls when the air around it is hot. Inside a sealed enclosure on a sunny roof, the "ambient" may be 60 °C or more. The datasheet gives a derating curve.

What happens when the rating is exceeded

A little over the rating, the resistor runs very hot, the colour bands darken, and its value drifts, usually upwards, over weeks. Well over the rating, the protective coating blisters and smokes, the board under it chars, and the resistive film or wire burns through, leaving an open circuit. Some resistors (called fusible resistors) are designed to fail open safely in exactly this way and act as a fuse. A charred resistor is usually a symptom: something else, often a shorted transistor, capacitor or diode, pushed too much current through it. Replacing only the resistor usually burns the new one too.

Worked examples

  1. LED resistor, 5 V supply. Red LED at 1.9 V, 330 Ω, so I = (5 − 1.9)/330 = 9.4 mA. PR = I²R = 0.0094² × 330 = 29 mW. A 1/4 W (250 mW) resistor is at about 12% of its rating: cool.

  2. Same LED on 24 V. R = (24 − 1.9)/0.010 ≈ 2.2 kΩ for 10 mA. PR = 22.1 V × 0.010 A = 0.22 W. That is 88% of a 1/4 W rating: hot. Use a 1/2 W or 1 W resistor. Surprising but common: on 24 V, the resistor wastes over ten times more power than the LED uses.

  3. 100 Ω across 12 V. P = V²/R = 144/100 = 1.44 W. A 1/4 W resistor is at almost 6 times its rating: it will smoke within seconds. You need at least a 2 W part, and a 5 W one would stay cooler.

  4. A loose terminal. A screw terminal carrying 10 A has a poor contact of 0.05 Ω. P = I²R = 100 × 0.05 = 5 W, concentrated in a spot a few millimetres across. That is enough to melt plastic and start a fire. This is why terminals are torqued, ferrules are used on stranded wire, and thermal cameras find faults in switchboards.

Safety

Overloaded resistors and regulators can reach temperatures that burn skin and char plastic, even at 5 V or 12 V. Touch-test a part only with the back of a finger, briefly, after switching off; better, use an infrared thermometer. Fuse every battery and supply you build with, close to the source.

Why power lines use high voltage

Suppose a power station must send 10 MW to a town through lines with a total resistance of 5 Ω. The power is P = VI, so the current needed depends on the voltage. The loss in the line is I²R. Look at what happens:

Ploss = I² Rline = (P / V)² × Rline For a fixed power delivered, line loss is proportional to 1/V². Double the voltage and you cut the loss to a quarter, or use a quarter of the copper for the same loss.

So the grid steps voltage up for long distances and down for use. Transformers (T08) do this efficiently for AC, which is the historical reason the grid is AC. Australia's transmission networks run at voltages such as 220, 275, 330 and 500 kV; distribution feeders in streets commonly run at 11 or 22 kV; a pole or pad-mount transformer then supplies 230 V single-phase and 400 V three-phase to buildings. All of that is licensed electrical and network work, far outside the ELV limit of this course.

The same idea appears at ELV scale. Power over Ethernet uses roughly 44–57 V rather than 12 V, so that the thin Cat6 conductors carry less current for the same power. Building services often prefer 24 V over 12 V for the same reason (try it in the cable calculator in T02).

Common misconception

"P = V²/R says higher voltage gives more loss, so high-voltage lines should waste more." In V²/R, V must be the voltage across the resistance, which for a line is the small voltage drop along the wire, not the line's voltage to earth. The line's voltage decides the current through it (I = P/V), and the loss is I²R. Always ask: which voltage, across which part?

Energy, kWh and your power bill

Energy = power × time. In joules: E = P × t with P in watts and t in seconds. The joule is tiny for household use, so electricity retailers use the kilowatt-hour (kWh): the energy used by 1 kW for 1 hour.

1 kWh = 1,000 W × 3,600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J = 3.6 MJ Energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (hours). Cost = energy (kWh) × price per kWh.

An Australian bill usually has two main parts: a usage charge in cents per kWh, and a fixed daily supply charge. Many homes also have a solar feed-in tariff credited for exported energy. Prices vary a lot by state, retailer, plan and time of day, so the table below uses an illustrative usage price of 30 c/kWh and supply charge of A$1.00 per day. Check your own bill for real figures.

On the job

Clients ask installers about running costs. A CCTV system with 16 PoE cameras at about 6 W each plus a 40 W recorder draws about 136 W, 24 hours a day: 0.136 kW × 8,760 h ≈ 1,190 kWh a year, roughly A$360 at 30 c/kWh. The same arithmetic sizes the UPS or battery backup for it. BMS technicians use kWh data from energy meters (often read over Modbus, see P14) to show building owners where energy goes.

In Australia

Many appliances carry the Energy Rating Label, which shows stars and an estimated energy use in kWh per year. That kWh figure times your tariff gives an estimated yearly running cost. Electricity prices and plans are regulated differently in each state; the Australian Energy Regulator and state bodies publish reference prices. Confirm current figures with them or your retailer.

Efficiency

Every real device turns some of its input into something other than the intended output, almost always heat. Efficiency η (eta) is

η = Pout / Pin Ploss = Pin − Pout η is between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). Energy is conserved, so whatever does not come out as useful output comes out as loss, mostly heat.
DeviceTypical efficiency (indicative)Where the loss goes
Linear regulator 12 V → 5 V~42% (it is always Vout/Vin at best)Heat in the regulator
Buck (switch-mode) converter85–95%Switching and resistance losses in the transistor, inductor and capacitors
Plug-pack power supply~80–90% at full load, worse at light loadTransformer and switching losses, standby
Incandescent lampa few percent as visible lightMost of the power leaves as infrared heat
LED lampseveral times better than incandescentHeat at the LED and driver
Large electric motor~85–95%Winding I²R, iron losses, friction
Rooftop solar panel (sunlight → electricity)~20%Light not absorbed, heat in the cells

A linear regulator's efficiency is easy to predict: the same current flows in and out, so η = (Vout × I)/(Vin × I) = Vout/Vin. From 12 V to 5 V that is 5/12 ≈ 42%, no matter how good the chip is.

Efficiencies multiply in a chain. A 90% charger into a 95% battery round-trip into a 90% converter gives 0.90 × 0.95 × 0.90 ≈ 77% overall.

Maximum power transfer

Every real source has internal resistance r (T01). Connect a load RL. How should you choose RL to get the most power into the load?

If RL is very large, almost no current flows, so little power. If RL is very small, lots of current flows but almost no voltage is left across the load, so again little power. The best is somewhere in between. With I = ℰ/(RL + r):

PL = I² RL = ℰ² RL / (RL + r)²

To find the maximum, differentiate with respect to RL and set the result to zero (the quotient rule from Class 12):

dPL/dRL = ℰ² [ (RL + r)² − 2RL(RL + r) ] / (RL + r)⁴ = 0
⟹ RL + r = 2RL ⟹ RL = r The load gets the most power when its resistance equals the source's internal resistance. Then Pmax = ℰ²/4r.

Example: a source with ℰ = 12 V and r = 2 Ω. With RL = 2 Ω: I = 12/4 = 3 A, PL = 9 × 2 = 18 W. But the internal resistance also gets 3² × 2 = 18 W. The source makes 36 W and only half reaches the load. With RL = 10 Ω: I = 1 A, PL = 10 W (less), but only 2 W is wasted inside, so efficiency is 83%.

So "matching" (RL = r) is right where the signal is weak and precious and wasting half is acceptable: radio receivers and antennas, which is why RF systems use a fixed impedance such as 50 Ω or 75 Ω for every cable and connector. It is the wrong goal for power delivery. The grid, batteries and power supplies are designed with r much smaller than the load, so that almost all the energy reaches the load.

Common misconception

"Maximum power transfer means maximum efficiency." At maximum power transfer, efficiency is exactly 50%. In the 1840s this result (then called Jacobi's law) led some people to think electric power could never be more than 50% efficient. Edison's team realised the goal for a power system is the opposite: keep the source and line resistance tiny compared with the load.

Battery energy: Wh = V × Ah

Battery capacity is usually given in amp-hours (Ah) or milliamp-hours (mAh). That is a charge rating (1 Ah = 3,600 C), not an energy rating. To get energy, multiply by the voltage:

Energy (Wh) = Voltage (V) × Capacity (Ah) Watt-hours make different batteries comparable. A 1 Ah cell at 3.7 V holds more than twice the energy of a 1 Ah cell at 1.2 V.

Worked example: alarm panel standby time

An alarm panel and its detectors draw 0.35 A at 12 V on standby. It has a 12 V 7 Ah SLA battery. Ideal standby time = 7 Ah ÷ 0.35 A = 20 hours. Real batteries deliver less: capacity falls at higher current and low temperature, and an older battery may have only about 80% of its rating left. A designer would allow for this, for example 7 × 0.8 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 16 hours, and then check the time the relevant standard or client requires. In energy terms: 84 Wh × 0.8 ÷ (12 V × 0.35 A = 4.2 W) ≈ 16 hours.

Bench tip

The "mAh" printed on a power bank is the capacity of its internal 3.7 V cells. Its USB output is 5 V or more, and the boost converter loses some energy. So you never get the printed mAh at 5 V. Compare batteries in Wh, not mAh. Airlines also limit power banks by Wh (commonly 100 Wh without approval); check your airline's current rules.

Check yourself

Show that volts × amps gives watts, using units only.

V = J/C and A = C/s. So V × A = (J/C) × (C/s) = J/s = W. Power is energy per charge times charge per second.

A 470 Ω resistor is connected across 12 V. What power does it dissipate, and is a 1/4 W part OK?

P = V²/R = 144/470 ≈ 0.31 W. That is 123% of 0.25 W: over the limit. Use at least a 1/2 W, preferably a 1 W, resistor so it runs at about 30–60% of rating.

Why does doubling the transmission voltage cut the line loss to a quarter?

For the same power, I = P/V, so doubling V halves the current. Loss = I²R, and halving I divides I² by four.

A 2.2 kW heater runs 3 hours a day for 90 days. At an illustrative 30 c/kWh, what does it cost?

2.2 kW × 3 h × 90 = 594 kWh. 594 × A$0.30 ≈ A$178.

A linear regulator makes 3.3 V at 0.5 A from a 12 V input. What is its efficiency and how much heat must it lose?

η = 3.3/12 ≈ 27.5%. Heat = (12 − 3.3) × 0.5 = 4.35 W. That needs a real heatsink; a buck converter would be the better choice.

A source has ℰ = 9 V and r = 3 Ω. What load gives the maximum power, and how much?

RL = r = 3 Ω. I = 9/6 = 1.5 A. PL = 1.5² × 3 = 6.75 W (= ℰ²/4r = 81/12). The same 6.75 W is wasted inside the source, so the efficiency is 50%.

Which stores more energy: a 3.7 V 3,000 mAh cell or a 12 V 1.2 Ah SLA battery?

3.7 × 3 = 11.1 Wh versus 12 × 1.2 = 14.4 Wh. The SLA stores more energy even though its mAh number (1,200) is smaller.

A resistor in a power supply is charred. Why should you not just replace it and switch on?

Resistors usually burn because something else failed (for example a shorted capacitor, diode or transistor) and pushed too much current through them. Unless you find and fix that cause, the new resistor will burn too, or worse.
