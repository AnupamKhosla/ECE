Multimeter mastery

The multimeter is the tool you will use most on any technical job. By the end of this lab you will measure DC and AC volts, current, resistance, continuity and diodes with confidence, choose the right setting and lead sockets every time, and avoid the classic mistakes that blow fuses.

Know your meter

Every digital multimeter has the same four parts: a display, a rotary dial that chooses what to measure, sockets (jacks) for the test leads, and a few buttons (HOLD, RANGE, REL, SELECT). The jacks matter most, because the wrong jack is how meters get damaged.

JackUsed forInside the meter
COMThe black lead, alwaysThe meter's reference (0 V)
VΩ (often also diode, continuity, Hz)Red lead for volts, ohms, continuity, diode testVery high resistance input, about 10 MΩ on volts
mA µARed lead for small currents (up to a few hundred mA)A small shunt resistor and a fuse
A (10 A or 20 A)Red lead for big currentsA tiny shunt (~0.01 Ω) and a bigger fuse

Dial symbols you will see: V⎓ (or V with a straight and dashed line) = DC volts. V~ = AC volts. mV = millivolts. Ω = resistance. A diode symbol (▷|) = diode test. A sound-wave symbol = continuity beeper. A, mA, µA = current (often with a SELECT button to switch between DC and AC).

Common misconception

Wrong: "The meter measures whatever the dial says, wherever the leads are."
Right: The jack decides what is connected inside the meter. In the A or mA jack, the red lead is joined to COM through a fraction of an ohm. That is perfect in series with a load and a dead short across a supply. Think "jack first, then dial".

Voltage: measure across

Voltage is a difference between two points, like the height difference between two steps. So you always measure voltage across something: one probe on each side, in parallel with it. The circuit stays exactly as it is.

Lab: DC volts

  1. Set up. Black lead in COM, red lead in VΩ. Dial to V⎓ (DC volts). On a manual meter choose the 20 V range.

  2. Measure an AA cell. Red on +, black on −. A new alkaline cell reads 1.55–1.62 V. A "flat" one reads under about 1.2 V (but see the tip below).

  3. Swap the probes. The reading becomes −1.58 V. Nothing is wrong: the minus sign tells you the red probe is on the lower voltage. On a job this is how you find which wire is positive.

  4. Measure the 9 V battery (new: 9.3–9.6 V) and the 3×AA pack (4.6–4.8 V).

  5. Measure across each part of your P02 circuit: resistor ≈ 2.8 V, LED ≈ 1.9 V, total ≈ 4.7 V.

Bench tip

A battery with nothing connected can read almost full voltage and still be nearly dead. Its internal resistance has risen, so the voltage collapses as soon as current flows. Always test a battery under load: measure the AA pack while it lights the LED, or test a 12 V supply while the camera is running. "Measure under load" is a field rule you will use for life.

Lab: AC volts

Set the dial to V~ and measure the output of your AC plug-pack (leads only, never the pack's mains pins). A 12 V AC pack typically reads 13–15 V AC with no load, because transformers are rated at full load. On the DC range the same output reads close to 0 V or jumps around: the DC range averages the waveform, and a sine wave averages to zero. On V~ the meter shows the RMS value, the AC voltage that would give the same heating as that DC voltage. The peak is √2 times higher: 12 V RMS has a peak of about 17 V.

Input impedance: why the voltmeter barely changes the circuit

A typical digital meter has an input resistance of about 10 MΩ on the volts ranges. Put it across a part and it adds a 10 MΩ path in parallel. For most circuits that is nothing. But in high-resistance circuits it matters:

1 MΩ ∥ 10 MΩ = (1 × 10) / (1 + 10) = 0.909 MΩ Measure the middle of a 1 MΩ / 1 MΩ divider on 9 V. You expect 4.50 V. The meter turns the bottom resistor into 0.909 MΩ, so it reads 9 × 0.909 / 1.909 ≈ 4.29 V, 5% low. On a 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider the same meter makes an error of only 0.05%. P04 explores this "loading effect".
On the job

On site you may measure a disconnected cable core that runs beside mains cables in a tray and read several volts, even tens of volts, AC. That is a "ghost" or phantom voltage, coupled in through cable capacitance; the 10 MΩ meter is too light a load to drain it. Many field meters have a LoZ (low impedance) mode that loads the line so ghost voltages collapse. Never assume a ghost; treat unknown voltages near mains as real, and leave that cabinet to the electrician.

Current: measure in series

Current is a flow: charge per second past a point. To measure it, the current has to flow through the meter. You must open the circuit and make the meter part of the loop (Figure 1, right).

  1. Power off. Always open the circuit with the power off.

  2. Move the red lead to the mA jack (or the 10 A jack if you expect more than about 200–400 mA, or have no idea). Dial to mA or A, and DC.

  3. Break the loop and bridge the gap with the meter. On the P02 board: pull the black jumper and put the probes where its two ends were. Red towards the + side of the gap.

  4. Power on and read. Expect about 8 mA. A minus sign means the current flows into COM: swap the probes or just note it.

  5. Power off, put the jumper back, and move the red lead back to VΩ. Make it a reflex.

Inside, the meter measures current by measuring the tiny voltage across a known shunt resistor (Ohm's law again). That voltage is lost from your circuit and is called the burden voltage. On the mA range it might be a few ohms, which drops about 8 mA × 10 Ω = 0.08 V: small but not zero, so the LED current reads slightly lower than without the meter.

Safety

The current jacks are protected by fuses. A good field meter uses ceramic HRC fuses, designed to break a very large fault current without exploding. Replace a blown fuse only with the exact type and rating printed on the meter. The 10 A range is often rated for short periods only (for example 10 A for 30 seconds, then a rest); check your manual.

Check your fuse

If the current ranges read 0 and the circuit dies when the meter is inserted, the fuse is probably blown. Test it: dial to Ω, red lead in VΩ, and touch the red probe tip into the mA jack. A good fuse and shunt read a few ohms or less. "OL" means the fuse is open. Repeat for the A jack (should read close to 0 Ω).

Resistance, continuity and diode test

Resistance: power off, part isolated

On Ω, the meter pushes a small known current from its own battery through the part and measures the voltage. That only works if no other source is pushing current. Two rules:

  1. Power off. An external voltage gives a meaningless reading and can damage the meter on cheap models.
  2. Isolate the part. In a circuit, other parts sit in parallel with it, and the meter measures the combination. Measure resistors out of the board, or lift one leg.

Other habits: short the probes together and note the lead resistance (0.1–0.5 Ω); subtract it from low readings, or use REL (relative) mode to zero it. Do not hold both probe tips with your fingers on values above about 100 kΩ, because your body (roughly 100 kΩ to 1 MΩ hand to hand) adds a parallel path. "OL" means over limit: above the range, or open circuit.

Continuity: the beeper

Continuity mode beeps when the resistance is below a threshold (often somewhere around 20–50 Ω, depending on the meter). You do not look at the meter, you listen, so your eyes stay on the probes. Use it for: wires and cable cores, fuses, switch contacts, breadboard rails, PCB tracks, and the "is this the same wire?" question. Power must be off.

Diode test

On the diode setting, the meter pushes about 1 mA through the part and shows the voltage across it. That is exactly the forward voltage at about 1 mA.

Why it works

The forward voltage at about 1 mA is set by the semiconductor: about 0.6 V for silicon, about 0.3 V for a Schottky metal–semiconductor junction, and about 1.8–2 V for a red LED whose wider band gap makes red photons. Reverse bias widens the depletion region and only a tiny leakage flows, so the meter shows OL.

Auto-ranging, resolution and accuracy

A meter's display has a fixed number of counts. A "2000-count" meter shows at most 1999. On a manual 2 V range a 1.534 V cell shows 1.534; on the 20 V range it shows 1.53; on the 200 V range only 1.5. Choose the lowest range that is above your reading. If you do not know what to expect, start on the highest range and work down. "OL" or "1" at the left means the value is above the range.

An auto-ranging meter does this for you: it starts high and steps down. It is slower (half a second to settle) and sometimes hunts between ranges on a changing signal; press RANGE to lock it. Always look at the units on the display: mV vs V and kΩ vs MΩ are the most common reading errors.

Accuracy is written like "±(0.5% + 2 counts)". On a 20.00 V range reading 12.00 V: 0.5% of 12 = 0.06 V, plus 2 counts of 0.01 V = 0.02 V, total ±0.08 V. So 11.92 to 12.08 V are all "correct". Do not worry about the last digit.

True RMS: a cheap meter calculates AC volts assuming a pure sine wave. Distorted waveforms (from dimmers, variable-speed drives or switch-mode supplies) can read 10–40% wrong. A true-RMS meter computes the real heating value. Buy true RMS for field work.

Field uses

These are the measurements you will make in the first months of a BMS, security or telecom job. All of them are on ELV circuits, usually inside a cabinet that may also contain mains equipment, which is why the CAT rating and your probe discipline matter.

24 V AC and 24 V DC on controllers

Many BMS controllers, valve actuators and damper actuators run on 24 V AC from a small control transformer. Access-control and alarm panels mostly use 12 V or 24 V DC from a power supply with a backup battery. First job on a "dead controller" call: measure the supply at the controller's own terminals.

  • 24 V AC on V~: expect roughly 24–28 V AC. A lightly loaded transformer reads high. A reading well below about 21–22 V suggests an overloaded transformer or a long, thin feed. Check the device's datasheet for its allowed range.
  • 24 V DC on V⎓: battery-backed supplies "float" the battery at about 27 V, so 27.0–27.6 V on a 24 V system is normal. For 12 V systems, 13.6–13.8 V is normal float.
  • If the controller is dead but its supply terminals read correctly, the problem is the controller (or its fuse). If the terminals read 0 V, work back towards the supply, measuring at each joint, until you find where the voltage disappears.
In Australia

You may measure the 24 V output of a control transformer, but the transformer's 230 V side is mains: wiring it, replacing it or measuring it is work for a licensed electrician. On site, follow your employer's isolation and test-before-touch procedures. Confirm current requirements with your state's electrical safety regulator.

A 12 V camera supply under load

A very common call-out: "the camera reboots at night." At night the camera switches on its infrared (IR) LEDs and its current might rise from 0.4 A to 0.9 A. On a long thin cable, the voltage drop rises with it.

Rloop = 2 × 40 m × 0.034 Ω/m = 2.7 Ω → Vdrop = 0.9 A × 2.7 Ω ≈ 2.5 V A 40 m run of 0.5 mm² copper figure-8 cable (about 0.034 Ω per metre per core; current goes out on one core and back on the other, hence 2 ×). The supply reads 12.0 V, but the camera gets about 9.5 V at night and browns out. In daylight (0.4 A) it gets about 10.9 V and seems fine.

So: measure at the camera end, with the camera under its worst load (cover the light sensor with tape to force night mode). Fixes: thicker cable (P05 has a voltage-drop calculator), a supply closer to the camera, or PoE.

Measuring a 4–20 mA loop

Industrial and building sensors (pressure, flow, humidity transmitters) often send their reading as a current between 4 mA and 20 mA. The current is the same everywhere in a series loop, so long cables and connection resistance do not change the value. 4 mA means 0% of range, 20 mA means 100%, and 0 mA means the loop is broken: a "live zero" that lets the controller detect faults.

value = 0 + (I − 4) / 16 × (10 − 0) bar = (12 − 4) / 16 × 10 = 5.0 bar Worked example: a 0–10 bar transmitter reading 12.00 mA means 50% of range, 5.0 bar. If the controller shows 4.2 bar but your meter reads 12.00 mA, the transmitter is fine and the controller's scaling is wrong. If the meter also says 10.7 mA, the transmitter (or the actual pressure) is the issue. A reading below about 3.6 mA or above about 21 mA is commonly used as a "sensor failed" signal (the NAMUR NE 43 convention).
On the job

Before opening a live loop, tell the operator or put the point into manual/override on the BMS. The moment you break the loop, the controller sees 0 mA, and its program may raise an alarm or drive a valve or fan to a safe position. Some transmitters have TEST terminals so you can measure loop current without breaking it. Clamp meters for milliamps exist too and are common with instrument technicians.

Finding a broken wire with continuity

A cable run is in the ceiling and you cannot reach both ends with your probes at once. Use the loop-back method:

Note the resistance too. Two cores of 30 m of 0.5 mm² should read about 2 × 30 × 0.034 ≈ 2 Ω. If a pair beeps but reads 30 Ω, there is a bad joint or a corroded terminal somewhere: "continuity" is not the same as "a good connection". A long tail lead (a reel of wire to bring the far end back to you) also works.

CAT ratings and probe care

P01 explained measurement categories. For daily use: your meter's rating is only as good as its leads. The leads have their own CAT rating printed on them; the system is rated at the lower of the two.

  • Inspect before use: cracked insulation, exposed metal near the plug, loose tips. Replace damaged leads; do not tape them.
  • Fingers behind the finger guards on the probes, always.
  • Tip caps: many CAT III/IV probe sets come with caps that leave only a few millimetres of metal exposed, to prevent a probe slipping and bridging two terminals. Many sites require them.
  • Connect black first, remove it last. Then the red, "live" probe is the only one you move.
  • Keep the meter's battery fresh. A low battery can make readings wrong without warning on cheap meters.
  • Store the meter switched off, with the red lead in VΩ, so your next measurement starts safe.
Safety

Your multimeter work in this course is on ELV only. A meter rating gives you no permission to measure mains. If a reading surprises you with an unexpected high voltage, step back, do not touch anything, and report it.

Common misconception

Wrong: "To see how many amps a power supply has, put the meter on amps across it."
Right: A supply does not "contain" a current. It provides a voltage, and the load decides the current (I = V/R). The maximum current it can safely provide is printed on its label. An ammeter across it is a load of almost 0 Ω, which asks for an almost unlimited current.

Theory T01 · Charge, current and voltage

Check yourself

Why does a voltmeter need a very high resistance and an ammeter a very low one?

A voltmeter goes in parallel, so any current it draws is stolen from the circuit; 10 MΩ draws almost nothing. An ammeter goes in series, so its resistance adds to the loop and drops voltage; a fraction of an ohm drops almost nothing. Each design disturbs the circuit as little as possible in its own connection.

Your meter reads −11.9 V on a 12 V DC supply. Is the supply faulty?

No. The minus sign means your red probe is on the negative terminal. Swap the probes or just note which terminal is positive. A digital meter on DC shows polarity; it does not get damaged by it.

A 0–100% humidity transmitter is 4–20 mA. Your meter reads 15.2 mA. What humidity is that?

(15.2 − 4)/16 × 100 = 11.2/16 × 100 = 70% RH.

You measure a 1 kΩ resistor still soldered on a board and get 680 Ω. Why?

Another path on the board is in parallel with it. 1 kΩ ∥ Rother = 680 Ω means Rother ≈ 2.1 kΩ. Lift one leg (or power off and isolate the part) to measure the resistor alone.

On the diode test, a part reads 0.62 V one way and OL the other. A second part reads 0.003 both ways. What are they?

The first is a healthy silicon diode (or a transistor junction): about 0.6 V forward, blocking in reverse. The second is shorted: it conducts both ways with almost no voltage drop.

A camera supply reads 12.1 V at the power supply. The camera at the end of a 50 m cable reboots every night. What do you measure, where, and when?

DC volts at the camera's own terminals, with the camera in night mode (IR on, cover the light sensor). You will probably find the voltage well below the camera's minimum, because the IR current makes the cable drop several volts. Measuring at the supply end, or in daytime, hides the problem.

You put the probes across a 12 V supply with the red lead still in the 10 A jack. What happens, and why?

The 10 A jack connects the probes through a shunt of about 0.01 Ω. I = 12 / 0.01 = 1200 A in theory; in practice the supply's limit or the fuse acts. The fuse blows (if it is a proper one) or the leads, meter or supply are damaged. You have measured nothing, because an ammeter must be in series with a load.

A continuity test on a pair of 30 m cable cores beeps but reads 25 Ω. Is the cable fine?

Not necessarily. 30 m of 0.5 mm² should read about 2 Ω for the pair. 25 Ω suggests a high-resistance joint, a corroded or loose terminal, or a damaged core. Under load that resistance will drop voltage and heat up. Continuity is not the same as a good connection; read the number, not just the beep.

Skills checklist

  • I always put the black lead in COM and choose the red jack before turning the dial.
  • I can measure DC and AC voltage across a part and explain a negative reading.
  • I can measure current in series, and I move the red lead back to VΩ afterwards.
  • I can check my meter's fuses with the ohms range.
  • I measure resistance only with power off and the part isolated.
  • I can use continuity and loop-back testing to find a broken core.
  • I can identify a diode's or LED's anode with the diode test and know the typical readings.
  • I can convert a 4–20 mA reading into a percentage and an engineering value.
  • I measure supplies under load, at the far end of the cable.
