CCTV and IP camera installation

How a modern camera system works from the lens to the hard drive, how to choose the right camera and lens for each spot, how to size the recorder and the storage, and what a security technician actually does on an Australian job from site survey to handover.

The big picture: what a CCTV system is

CCTV means closed-circuit television: cameras whose video goes only to a private recorder and screens, not to public broadcast. Today almost every new commercial system is an IP camera system, where each camera is a small network computer. Many homes, shops and older buildings still have analogue or HD-over-coax systems, and you will service those too.

Every system, old or new, has the same four jobs:

  1. Capture. A lens focuses light onto an image sensor.
  2. Encode. The picture is turned into a compressed digital stream (H.264 or H.265).
  3. Transport. The stream travels over coax or Ethernet cable to a recorder.
  4. Record and view. A recorder writes it to hard drives and shows it on screens and phone apps.

The key difference between the old and new families is where the encoding happens.

Analogue and HD-over-coax

Old analogue cameras sent a PAL video signal (about 720 × 576 pixels) on RG59 coax with BNC connectors. Power came on a separate pair of wires, often in a combined "siamese" cable (coax plus a power pair). The recorder is a DVR (digital video recorder): it digitises and compresses the video.

HD-over-coax is the upgrade path. It sends high-definition video over the same coax. There are three competing formats: HD-TVI, HD-CVI and AHD. A camera and DVR must speak the same format, although most current DVRs (often sold as "hybrid" or "XVR" recorders) accept several formats and some IP cameras too. Its strengths are that it reuses existing coax and it reaches further than Ethernet: often a few hundred metres on good RG59, compared with 100 m for Cat6. Check the datasheet for each format and resolution.

IP cameras

An IP camera has a sensor, an image processor, a video encoder and a network port, all in the housing. It has an IP address, a web page for setup, and it sends one or more video streams over the network. It is usually powered by PoE (Power over Ethernet) from the switch or recorder. The recorder is an NVR (network video recorder) or, on big sites, a VMS (video management software) running on a server.

Analogue / HD-over-coax

  • Coax + separate power
  • DVR encodes the video
  • Long cable runs, simple
  • Limited analytics and resolution
  • Common in older homes and shops

IP

  • One Cat6 for data and power
  • Camera encodes the video
  • 100 m per cable run, then a switch
  • High resolution, analytics, scalable
  • Standard for new commercial work
On the job

A typical week for a junior CCTV technician: pulling Cat6 through ceilings, terminating RJ45s and patch panels, mounting cameras on ladders or an EWP (elevating work platform), setting IP addresses on a laptop, aiming and focusing cameras, and going back on service calls when "camera 7 is offline". You will also replace old DVR systems with IP. Being able to work on both families is an advantage when you look for work.

Inside the camera: sensor, resolution, IR, low light and WDR

The image sensor

The sensor is a CMOS chip with millions of tiny photodiodes, one per pixel. Each photodiode turns arriving light into a small charge, which is then read out and digitised. Sensor sizes use an old "inch" naming, such as 1/3", 1/2.8" or 1/1.8". A bigger sensor with the same pixel count has bigger pixels, and a bigger pixel collects more light, so it gives a cleaner picture at night.

Why it works

A photodiode is a reverse-biased p–n junction. Each photon with enough energy makes an electron–hole pair, and the junction's electric field sweeps them apart as a tiny current. More light gives more pairs and a bigger signal. At night only a few photons arrive per pixel, so random noise becomes visible as "grain". That grain also makes the compressed stream bigger, because the encoder thinks the scene is changing.

Frame rate is how many pictures per second the camera sends. Australia used the PAL standard (25 frames per second), so many cameras default to 25 fps. For recording, 10–15 fps is often enough for people walking. Fewer frames means less storage. Fast scenes, like cash counting or vehicle gates, may need more. The specification usually tells you.

Infrared (IR) night vision

Most cameras have IR LEDs. Silicon sensors are sensitive to near-infrared light that our eyes cannot see. At night the camera:

  • moves a small IR-cut filter out of the light path (you hear a click). In daytime this filter blocks IR so colours look right;
  • switches the image to black and white;
  • turns on its IR LEDs, usually at 850 nm (a faint red glow is visible) or 940 nm (invisible, but shorter range).

The IR range on the box (for example "IR 30 m") is measured in ideal conditions. Use about half to two-thirds of it for planning. IR light reflects strongly off nearby surfaces such as eaves, walls, gutters and spider webs. This is the number one cause of washed-out, foggy night images.

Low light and "colour at night"

Low-light performance is quoted in lux (lumens per square metre). For reference, a lit street is very roughly 10 lux and moonlight is far below 1 lux. Many brands sell "full-colour" night cameras. They combine a large-aperture lens (a low F-number, such as F1.0, which lets more light in), a bigger sensor, and often a small warm-white light that switches on when needed. They give colour images at night, which helps to describe clothing and vehicles. But the white light can annoy neighbours, so think about where it points.

Wide dynamic range (WDR)

Picture a camera inside a shop, looking at the glass front door on a sunny day. Outside is thousands of times brighter than inside. A normal sensor exposes for one or the other: either people at the door are black silhouettes, or the outside is pure white. True WDR takes two or more exposures per frame (short and long) and blends them. It is quoted in decibels:

ratio = 10dB/20  →  120 dB = 106 : 1 A camera with 120 dB WDR can show detail in areas a million times brighter than its darkest areas in the same frame. "Digital WDR" (DWDR) only brightens the shadows in software. It helps a little, but it is not the same.

Lenses: focal length, field of view and pixels per metre

This is the most important design skill in CCTV, and a favourite interview topic. Customers ask, "Will I be able to see his face at the gate?" The honest answer depends on how many pixels land on each metre of the scene at that distance.

Focal length versus angle of view

The focal length (f, in mm) is the distance from the lens's optical centre to the sensor when it is focused far away. A short focal length spreads the sensor's width over a wide angle. A long focal length squeezes the same sensor onto a narrow slice of the scene, so a distant target fills more pixels.

HFOV = 2 · atan( w / 2f ) HFOV is the horizontal field of view (angle). w is the active sensor width in mm and f is the focal length in mm. Example: a 1/2.8" sensor is about 5.6 mm wide. With an 8 mm lens: 2 · atan(5.6 / 16) = 2 · atan(0.35) ≈ 38.6°. This simple "pinhole" formula works well for longer lenses. Short lenses (2.8 mm, 4 mm) have barrel distortion and their datasheets quote wider angles than the formula gives. For design, always use the angle in the camera's datasheet.

Varifocal lenses let you change f during installation (2.8–12 mm is common). Motorised varifocal cameras let you zoom and focus from the laptop or NVR. This saves a ladder climb, and it is the norm on commercial jobs.

Scene width and pixels per metre

At distance d, the camera covers a horizontal strip of width W. The horizontal pixels are spread across that strip:

W = 2 · d · tan(HFOV / 2)     PPM = Hpx / W PPM is pixels per metre at the target distance. Hpx is the horizontal resolution (2560 for a typical 4 MP camera).

Worked example 1. A 4 MP camera (2560 px wide) with a 2.8 mm lens (datasheet about 100°) looks at a person 10 m away. W = 2 × 10 × tan 50° = 2 × 10 × 1.19 ≈ 23.8 m. PPM = 2560 / 23.8 ≈ 107 px/m. That is good for observing activity, but not for identifying a face.

Worked example 2. The customer wants to identify people at a gate 12 m away, with a 2560 px camera. Identification needs about 250 px/m (see below). So W must be at most 2560 / 250 = 10.24 m. Then tan(HFOV/2) = 10.24 / (2 × 12) = 0.427, so HFOV/2 ≈ 23° and HFOV ≈ 46°. An 8 mm lens (about 40°) works: W = 2 × 12 × tan 20° ≈ 8.7 m, so PPM ≈ 293 px/m. The 2.8 mm lens would give only about 90 px/m.

Detection levels, in plain terms

The industry describes what an image lets an operator do at a given pixel density. The figures most people quote come from the international standard IEC 62676-4 (video surveillance application guidelines). Australia's CCTV standard series, AS 4806, describes the same idea using how tall a person appears on the screen. A project specification will say which method and which level each camera must meet.

LevelAboutWhat the image lets you do
Detect25 px/mTell that a person or vehicle is present
Observe62.5 px/mSee what people are doing, and general detail such as clothing colour
Recognise125 px/mTell with confidence whether a person is someone already known, for example a staff member
Identify250 px/mEnough face detail to establish who a person is
Inspect1000 px/mRead fine detail, such as small text or a card

These numbers assume good light, focus and compression. A dark, noisy, blurred or heavily compressed image gives less real detail than the pixel count suggests. Also think about the vertical angle. A camera mounted high and aimed steeply down sees the tops of heads and caps, not faces. Aim face cameras at the point people walk through, at a shallow angle.

Power over Ethernet and the NVR

PoE in one page

PoE sends DC power on the same Cat6 cable as the data. The switch or NVR port is the PSE (power sourcing equipment) and the camera is the PD (powered device). Before it sends power, the PSE checks for a signature resistor in the PD. So it never pushes power into a laptop. The voltage is roughly 44–57 V DC, which is within extra-low voltage.

StandardCommon namePower at the switch portPower available at the device
IEEE 802.3af (Type 1)PoE15.4 W12.95 W

IEEE 802.3bt (Type 3)PoE++ / 4PPoE60 W51 W
IEEE 802.3bt (Type 4)PoE++ / 4PPoE90 Wabout 71 W

The difference between the two power columns is the I²R loss in up to 100 m of thin copper. Two budgets matter: per port (does the port's class cover the camera?) and total (the switch's whole PoE budget). A fixed camera may draw 4–8 W in daytime and more at night when the IR LEDs come on. A PTZ with a heater can need PoE+ or more. Some outdoor PTZs need a separate 24 V AC supply.

Worked example. A 16-port PoE switch has a 150 W PoE budget. You connect 14 turret cameras rated at 7 W maximum (IR on) and 2 PTZ cameras rated at 25 W. Total = 14 × 7 + 2 × 25 = 98 + 50 = 148 W. That is just under the budget, with no margin. On a cold night with the PTZ heaters on, cameras may reboot. Use a bigger switch or split the load across two.

Common misconception

"The camera worked on the bench, so the cable is fine." A camera can link up and stream in daytime, then drop out every night. At night the IR LEDs raise the power draw. A badly terminated pair, or a cable that is too long or not pure copper, then drops too much voltage. Copper-clad aluminium (CCA) cable has higher resistance and is a common cause. Test the run with a proper tester and check the PoE draw on the switch's status page.

What the NVR does

  • Channels. The number of cameras it can record (4, 8, 16, 32, 64…).
  • Incoming bandwidth. The total Mbps it can accept from all cameras, for example 160 or 256 Mbps. Exceed it and channels drop out.
  • Decoding. How many streams it can display at once on its HDMI output.
  • Drive bays and RAID. Surveillance-grade hard drives (designed to write all day); some models offer RAID so one drive failure does not lose footage.
  • Built-in PoE ports. Many small NVRs have them. The cameras plugged in there usually go on a private internal network that the NVR manages, which makes setup "plug and play".

H.264, H.265 and the stream

Video compression stores a full picture (an I-frame) every so often. In between, it stores only what changed (P-frames). The gap between I-frames is the GOP or I-frame interval. A static car park compresses very well. A tree waving in the wind, rain, or night-time sensor noise does not. H.265 (HEVC) usually needs noticeably less bitrate than H.264 for similar quality, often quoted at roughly 30–50% less, depending on the scene. Most brands also offer "smart" codec modes that stretch the GOP when nothing moves. Cameras usually send a main stream (full resolution, for recording) and a sub-stream (low resolution, for phone apps and multi-camera views).

CBR (constant bitrate) keeps the bitrate fixed. VBR (variable bitrate) lets it rise with scene complexity up to a cap. With VBR, your storage use at night can be higher than in the day.

ONVIF: getting brands to talk

ONVIF is an industry forum that publishes interface standards called profiles. A camera and recorder that both support the same profile can work together even if they are different brands. Profile S covers basic video streaming and PTZ. Profile T covers newer streaming features, including H.265. Profile G covers recording and storage. Profile M covers analytics metadata. In practice, ONVIF almost always gives you live video and recording across brands. Advanced features, such as line-crossing events, people counting or smart search, often work fully only when the camera and NVR are the same brand. Every camera also offers a raw RTSP stream address, which is handy for testing.

In Australia

There is no single national rule for how long footage must be kept. Retention comes from the client's policy, their insurer, or the rules for their industry (for example, some licensed venues and gaming sites have specific camera and retention conditions set by the state liquor or gaming regulator). Thirty days is a common request, but always build to the written specification, not a habit.

Network design for cameras

On anything bigger than a home system, cameras should not share a flat network with office PCs. A VLAN (virtual LAN) splits one physical switch network into separate logical networks. Traffic between them must go through a router or firewall, where you control it.

  • IP plan first. Make a spreadsheet before you arrive: camera name, location, IP, switch and port, MAC address, model, lens.
  • Uplink bandwidth. 48 cameras × 6 Mbps = 288 Mbps, so the uplink from the camera switch to the NVR must be at least gigabit.
  • Time. Point every camera and the NVR at the same NTP time server. Footage with the wrong time is much less useful as evidence.
  • Security basics. Strong unique passwords (never the default), current firmware, UPnP off, unused services off, and cloud "P2P" access only if the client's IT policy allows it.

Mounting, weatherproofing, labelling and handover

Mounting

  • Use the junction box or back box made for that camera model. It hides the cable joint and gives a solid, sealed base.
  • Mount high enough to be out of easy reach (often around 2.5–3.5 m on walls), but not so high that faces are lost.
  • Do not aim into the rising or setting sun, or straight at bright lights.
  • Keep IR cameras away from close surfaces in front of them (eaves, gutters, walls), which reflect IR back into the lens.
  • Fix to something solid: masonry anchors in brick, proper fixings in sheet metal, never only into plasterboard for outdoor cameras.

Weatherproofing and cable entry

Outdoor cameras carry an IP rating (ingress protection, AS 60529 / IEC 60529). The first digit is dust (6 = dust-tight), the second is water (6 = powerful water jets, 7 = temporary immersion). IP66 or IP67 is normal for outdoor cameras. IK10 is the impact (vandal) rating. The camera is only as waterproof as your cable joint.

  • Use outdoor-rated (UV-stabilised) cable outside, in conduit where it can be damaged.
  • Fit the camera's supplied weatherproof RJ45 boot or gland on the cable tail, and tighten it properly.
  • Seal wall penetrations with a suitable sealant. Where cables pass through fire-rated walls or floors, they must be fire-stopped with an approved system (see C07).

Safety

CCTV work is mostly at height: ladders, scaffolds and EWPs. Follow the site's working-at-heights rules; an EWP needs the right licence or training for its type. Before you drill in a building built before about 1990, ask for the asbestos register: asbestos was widely used in Australian buildings and was banned nationally only from the end of 2003. Cameras run on ELV PoE, but a new 230 V outlet for an NVR, PTZ power supply or rack must be installed by a licensed electrician. Never open a camera power supply or a mains-powered NVR to work inside it.

Labelling and handover

  • Label both ends of every cable, the patch-panel port and the camera itself with the same ID (for example CAM-07).
  • Give each camera a clear name in the NVR, such as CAM-07 Loading dock N.
  • Hand over: as-built drawings with camera positions, the camera schedule (name, model, IP, MAC, switch port, lens, target level), retention settings, warranty details, and credentials, handed over in the secure way the client chooses.
  • Show the client how to search footage and export a clip. Then export one together and play it on another computer to prove it works.

A job from site survey to commissioning

  1. Site survey. Walk the site with the client and a floor plan. For each camera, write down the purpose (what must be seen, and at what level), the distance, the mounting point, the cable route, and the lighting at night. Find the comms room, power, ceiling access and fire-rated walls. Ask for the asbestos register. Take photos.

  2. Design and quote. Choose cameras and lenses using pixels per metre (the widget above). Size the NVR: channels, incoming Mbps, storage and drive bays. Check switch PoE budgets. Measure cable runs and keep each under 90 m of permanent cable. Write the IP plan.

  3. Pre-configure on the bench. Update firmware, set passwords, IP addresses, names, time zone and NTP, and codec settings. Label each box with its future location. An hour at the bench saves a day on ladders.

  4. Rough-in. Run cables, conduit and catenary, install junction boxes, fire-stop penetrations, and label both ends.

  5. Fit-off. Terminate and test every cable (record the test results), mount the cameras, seal and weatherproof the joints, and patch them into the switch.

  6. Commission. Add the cameras to the NVR. Aim and focus them. Set image settings (WDR, exposure), recording schedules, motion or line-crossing rules, and privacy masks over areas that must not be recorded. Check the bitrate and incoming bandwidth. Come back at night and check every IR image. Confirm the oldest recording after a few days matches the retention target.

  7. Handover. Documentation, training, a clip export test, and the sign-off sheet. Book the first maintenance visit if there is a service contract.

Security licensing

In Australia

Installing, servicing and advising on security equipment such as CCTV, alarms and access control is licensed by each state and territory, not nationally. Some examples:

  • NSW: the Security Licensing & Enforcement Directorate (SLED), part of NSW Police Force, issues security licences. Technical security work falls under its Class 2 licences, and the business you work for needs a master licence.
  • Victoria: the Licensing & Regulation Division (LRD) of Victoria Police regulates private security, including security equipment installers.
  • Queensland: the Office of Fair Trading licenses security providers.

Expect identity and criminal-history checks and a set of required training units. The exact licence class, training units and whether a trainee can work under supervision before being licensed all differ by state and change over time. Confirm the current requirements with your state's regulator before you enrol in any course. C11 covers licences in more detail.

Data cable that connects to a telecommunications network is also customer cabling under the ACMA rules, so many CCTV employers ask for an ACMA cabling registration as well. See C07.

Privacy and surveillance law

In Australia

Surveillance and privacy law differs by state and territory. For example, NSW has the Workplace Surveillance Act 2005 and the ACT has the Workplace Privacy Act 2011, which require employers to notify staff about camera surveillance in advance and to display signs. Victoria's Surveillance Devices Act 1999 bans surveillance in workplace toilets, washrooms and change rooms. Recording audio is regulated more strictly than video in every state, so most installers leave camera microphones off unless the client has legal advice. The federal Privacy Act 1988 also applies to many organisations. As the technician, you are not the client's lawyer: point out signage, privacy masking and audio, record what the client decided, and tell them to check the law for their state.

Training and entry roles

Typical entry roles are trainee or apprentice security technician, CCTV installer, security installer's offsider, and data cabler on a security team. Common requirements in ads are a driver's licence, a White Card (general construction induction), working-at-heights training, an ACMA cabling registration, and the state security licence or the ability to get one.

Formal training sits in the national CPP (Property Services) training package. The Certificate II in Security Operations is mainly the qualification for front-line guards and crowd controllers. The technical path is a Certificate II or III in electronic or technical security, which covers installing and commissioning alarms, CCTV and access control. Qualification names and codes are updated from time to time, and each state's regulator lists which qualification or units it accepts for each licence class. Check that list first, then choose a registered training organisation (RTO).

On the job

Interviewers for junior roles mostly test whether you are safe, reliable and teachable, and whether you understand the basics in this chapter. Bring photos of your bench work: a tidy patch panel, a labelled camera VLAN, a storage calculation. That shows more than a list of courses. C12 has portfolio ideas.

Typical interview questions

  • "A client wants to identify faces at a gate 12 m away. How do you pick the camera and lens?" (Pixels per metre, as in worked example 2.)

  • "How much storage does 16 cameras at 4 Mbps for 30 days need?" (About 21 TB; show the steps.)

  • "The client asks you to record audio in the staff room. What do you say?" (Raise the legal issue, do not decide it yourself, record the client's decision.)

Check yourself

Where is the video encoded in an analogue system, and where in an IP system?

In an analogue or HD-over-coax system the DVR digitises and compresses the video. In an IP system the camera encodes it, and the NVR only stores and displays streams that are already compressed. This is why an IP camera's settings (codec, bitrate, frame rate) live on the camera.

A 2 MP camera (1920 px) with a 100° lens views a doorway 6 m away. What is the pixels per metre, and what level is that?

W = 2 × 6 × tan 50° ≈ 14.3 m. PPM = 1920 / 14.3 ≈ 134 px/m. That just reaches "recognise" (125) but is well short of "identify" (250). A longer lens aimed at the doorway would be needed for identification.

Why does a 4 mm lens give a wider view than an 8 mm lens on the same camera?

The angle is set by the sensor width and the focal length: HFOV = 2·atan(w/2f). A shorter f makes a larger angle, so the same sensor width covers a wider part of the scene. Each metre of the scene then gets fewer pixels.

A switch has a 120 W PoE budget. Can it run 12 cameras that each draw 6.5 W by day and 9 W at night?

At night: 12 × 9 = 108 W, which is under 120 W, with only about 10% margin. It will work, but it is tight. Check each camera's maximum rating (not the typical value) and whether anything else draws from the same budget. A bigger budget would be a safer design.

Eight cameras at 6 Mbps, continuous recording, 14 days. Roughly how much storage?

8 × 6 = 48 Mbps. 48 × 3600 × 24 ÷ 8 = 518 400 MB ≈ 518 GB per day. × 14 ≈ 7.3 TB. With 20% headroom, about 8.7 TB, so two 6 TB drives or one 10 TB drive.

What does ONVIF guarantee, and what does it not?

If the camera and recorder share a profile (for example Profile S or T), you can expect live video, recording and basic control to work across brands. It does not guarantee that a brand's advanced analytics or smart events will work on another brand's recorder. Those often need same-brand equipment or a VMS integration.

Why should the cable enter a camera junction box from below or behind, not from the top?

Water runs along a cable under gravity. A cable entering from the top guides rain straight into the gland and onto the RJ45 joint. Entry from behind keeps the cable inside the wall. Entry from below with a drip loop makes water drip off the lowest point before it reaches the gland.

Who decides whether you need a security licence for CCTV work, and how do you find out?

The state or territory regulator where the work is done, for example SLED in NSW or the Licensing & Regulation Division of Victoria Police. Requirements differ by state and change over time, so check the regulator's current information before you start a course or a job.

Skills checklist

  • I can explain the difference between analogue/HD-over-coax and IP camera systems.
  • I can choose a camera type (bullet, dome, turret, PTZ, fisheye) for a location and say why.
  • I can calculate scene width and pixels per metre, and choose a lens for a target level.
  • I can explain IR, the IR-cut filter, low-light ratings and true WDR.
  • I can check per-port and total PoE budgets for a camera job.
  • I can size NVR storage, incoming bandwidth and drive count from bitrate and retention.
  • I can sketch a camera VLAN design and list basic camera security settings.
  • I can describe weatherproof cable entry, labelling and a proper handover.
  • I know that security licensing and surveillance law are state-based, and where to check them.
