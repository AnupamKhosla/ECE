T36

Camera optics, calibration and stereo depth

A camera image is a projection of a scene. Reliable measurements depend on controlled geometry, lighting and calibration.

Time 30 minutesBefore this T00 · T10 · T24

A pixel to millimetre conversion has conditions

In an ideal pinhole model, x in the image is proportional to fX/Z. The same object takes fewer pixels as it moves away. A scale marked at the table plane cannot describe a raised workpiece accurately unless the geometry is accounted for. Intrinsics describe the camera’s projection and distortion. Extrinsics describe the position and orientation relative to another frame.

Stereo disparity estimates the distance

After rectification of horizontal stereo images, a corresponding feature lies on the same row in both views. If the baseline B and focal length f are fixed, the difference d between horizontal pixel coordinates yields Z = fB/d. Sensitivity is approximately |ΔZ| = fB|Δd|/d² for small changes. As a target gets farther, the same one-pixel error matters more.

Watch on YouTube · Look for what happens to depth when the relative camera geometry moves or is corrected. Dynamic Calibration: Keeping Stereo Depth Accurate · Luxonis.

Example. f = 600 px and B = 75 mm produce 750 mm at disparity 60 px and 1,500 mm at 30 px. One extra disparity pixel changes the results by about 12 mm and 48 mm respectively.

The camera and the robot need a common frame

A detection gives image coordinates, or under calibration a point in the camera’s coordinate frame. Transform it into the robot’s frame before interpreting it as a target position. Lighting on reflective surfaces can produce wrong or missing depth. Time can also separate a camera image from the robot pose it should be paired with. Test known targets across distance and lighting first. OpenCV calibration and geometry.

For an undistorted, rectified pinhole model with principal point (cx, cy) and focal lengths fx, fy in pixels, a point with axial depth Z back-projects to X = (u − cx)Z/fx and Y = (v − cy)Z/fy. Use the optical frame convention from the camera model. With u = 400 px, cx = 320 px, fx = 600 px and Z = 750 mm, X = 100 mm. At that bearing, a 12 mm error in depth alone shifts X by about 1.6 mm. If the camera interface returns distance along the ray, convert it to axial depth before using these formulas.

A calibration can have small image reprojection error and still miss physical positions when the camera mount or fixture moves. Check an independently known target in the region and height where the part is handled. At robot speed 0.20 m/s, pairing a target with a robot pose 50 ms late adds 10 mm of potential translation along the direction of motion, even with a good static transform.

Exposure has a time and a duration

For constant sideways motion in a calibrated image region, blur travel in pixels equals target speed × exposure duration × image scale. At 400 mm/s, 2 ms and 4 px/mm, travel is 3.2 px. A global exposure pairs all rows with the same interval. In a rolling shutter, two rows have different exposure midpoints, so shortening their integration reduces blur and the time difference can still distort shape. Check the trigger-to-exposure delay and the meaning of any image timestamp before pairing frames with an encoder. P176 · Reproduce blur and rolling-shutter shear.
