Model a robot’s steering or an air handler’s output as a measured process. Decide what to command, how to recognise failure and when to leave the output off.

Open-loop and closed-loop control

Open-loop control applies a command without measuring the result. A robot’s 50% PWM setting can produce different speeds as the battery and floor change. Closed-loop control measures the process, compares it with the setpoint and uses the error to adjust its command. The sign matters. If the adjustment makes the error grow, the loop is acting in the wrong direction.

e = setpoint − measurement   ·   u = clamp(Kpe + bias, umin, umax)

Proportional action changes the command immediately with error. Integral action accumulates error to correct a persistent offset. When the output is saturated, accumulation can cause windup and slow recovery. Anti-windup can stop or unwind the integral under defined conditions. Derivative action responds to the rate of change and can amplify noise. Delay and excessive gain can turn corrective action into oscillation. Start by testing a single change and recording the response over time. University of Michigan motor speed control tutorial.

Read a step response before tuning

With the feedback loop off in a simulator, increase duty from 20% to 40%. Suppose speed rises from 600 to 1,000 rpm. The static gain is 400 rpm per 0.20 duty fraction, or 2,000 rpm per unit duty. If it reaches 852 rpm, about 63% of the 400 rpm increase, at 0.18 s after the speed first begins to rise, 0.18 s is the time constant of a rough first-order model. Measure the separate delay from command to the start of that rise. Motor friction, changing load and driver limits can make a different step behave differently.

What you accept depends on the job. Compare rise time (time from 10% to 90% of the requested change), overshoot over the target, time to enter and stay within a chosen band, and final error. Agree on the band and the observation window. A delayed reading marked as present speed can look like a badly tuned loop. Plot setpoint, measurement, output and the sample timestamps together.

Implement PI with real units and an output clamp

e[k] = r[k] − y[k]   ·   ΔI = Ki × e[k] × Δt
uraw = uff + Kpe[k] + I   ·   u = clamp(uraw, 0, 1)

If speed is in rpm and output is a duty fraction, Kp has units duty/rpm and Ki has units duty/(rpm·s). With 200 rpm error, Kp = 0.001 duty/rpm, Ki = 0.004 duty/(rpm·s), and Δt = 0.02 s, proportional action adds 0.20 duty and one integration step adds 0.016 duty. Carry the integral across ticks. If the candidate output is above 1 and error is positive, skip that integration step. Allow a negative error to unwind a high integral. Apply the analogous rule at the lower limit.

Feedforward uff comes from the approximate demand needed before feedback corrects the error. For the step above, moving from 600 to 900 rpm suggests adding about 0.15 duty to the original command. Change load and verify how much the PI term must add. Check a target beyond the drive’s range, then lower it and compare recovery with and without anti-windup.

Sample the right quantity at the right rate

At 2,400 counts per wheel revolution and a 20 ms speed window, one count is 1.25 rpm. At low speed, a one-count change or uneven scheduling is visible as speed noise. A longer window improves count resolution and adds delay. Measuring time between valid edges is another option at low speed, with a timeout for zero motion. Verify sign and scaling before closing the loop. In a cascaded servo, the inner current loop normally responds faster than the velocity loop, which responds faster than the outer position loop. Keep each boundary observable so you know which loop saturates first.

Hysteresis, permissives and proof

Watch on YouTube · Webinar: Basic Actuator Trouble Shooting - Tips and Tricks · Belimo
Identify which checks establish command, supply and mechanical response independently in a real actuator investigation.

A controller can start cooling above 25 °C and stop below 23 °C. The gap is hysteresis. Between the limits the controller remembers the current state. A permissive is a condition that must be satisfied to allow action. An interlock prevents or stops a conflicting action. Feedback from a current switch, airflow switch, encoder or auxiliary contact gives independent evidence that a commanded action occurred.

In the BMS trainer, a simulated fan is commanded on, but its proof input stays off. After the proof deadline, drive the output off and latch an alarm. Record the command, feedback, alarm and timestamp separately. A proof switch can itself stick, so a field test should also check behaviour with the command off. Life-safety and plant protection require the approved system design and site procedures.

Check yourself

A robot veers away from the line when the left sensor sees it. What do you check first?

Check sensor polarity and whether the command actually turns the robot left. Raise the wheels for this direction test. A swapped motor or reversed error sign can produce positive feedback even with a small gain.
