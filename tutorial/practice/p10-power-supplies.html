Power supplies: regulators, buck converters, batteries

You will build a 5 V supply with a 7805 and work out how hot it gets, set any voltage with an LM317, measure why a buck converter runs cool, size a standby battery for an alarm panel, and calculate cable voltage drop for a 12 V camera at the end of a long run: the single most useful sum in security installation.

Safety

All of this lab is extra-low-voltage DC. Regulators and load resistors get hot enough to burn (80–120 °C) without any warning glow: touch briefly with a fingertip, or not at all. Batteries can deliver huge currents into a short circuit: a 12 V 7 Ah sealed lead-acid battery will melt a thin wire and can start a fire. Fuse every battery lead close to the battery. Lithium-ion cells can catch fire if punctured, shorted or charged wrongly. The 230 V side of any power supply, and wiring a 24 V AC control transformer to mains, is licensed electrician's work.

1 · The 7805 linear regulator

A linear regulator is a transistor in series with the load that automatically adjusts how much voltage it "throws away" so that the output stays fixed. The 7805 gives 5.0 V out from any input from about 7 V up to its maximum (around 35 V for common versions; check your datasheet).

Two limits: dropout and heat

Dropout voltage is the minimum difference between input and output for the regulator to keep working. For a standard 7805 it is about 2 V, so you need at least about 7 V in. Below that, the output simply follows the input down, about 2 V lower. "Low-dropout" (LDO) regulators need only a few hundred millivolts.

Heat is the bigger limit. The regulator passes the full load current while dropping (Vin − Vout) across itself:

P = (Vin − Vout) × I = (12 − 5) × 0.5 A = 3.5 W 3.5 W is a lot for a part the size of a fingernail. Efficiency is only Vout/Vin = 5/12 = 42%: more power is wasted as heat than delivered.

Worked example: does it need a heatsink?

Heat flows out of the chip through thermal resistances, measured in °C per watt. A bare TO-220 package in still air is roughly 50–65 °C/W from junction (the silicon) to air. The chip should stay below about 125 °C (the 7805 shuts itself down if it gets too hot, which shows up as the output cutting in and out).

  • No heatsink: 3.5 W × 60 °C/W = +210 °C. Impossible: it will go into thermal shutdown.
  • Allowed total rise in a 40 °C cabinet: 125 − 40 = 85 °C. Total thermal resistance allowed: 85 / 3.5 = 24 °C/W.
  • Junction-to-case is about 5 °C/W and the thermal pad about 1 °C/W, so the heatsink must be better than about 24 − 6 = 18 °C/W. Choose one rated around 10–15 °C/W for margin.
  • Or reduce the heat: feed it from 9 V instead of 12 V (P = 4 × 0.5 = 2 W), or use a buck converter (section 3).

  1. Build Figure 1 on the breadboard, capacitors right at the pins. Input from the 9 V battery first.

  2. No load: measure output. Expect 4.8–5.2 V (the 7805 is typically ±4%).

  3. 100 Ω load (50 mA): output barely changes. Regulator heat = (9 − 5) × 0.05 = 0.2 W: slightly warm.

  4. Dropout test: swap the input to the 4×AA pack (about 6 V). Output falls to about 4 V. The regulator has run out of headroom.

  5. Heat test: 12 V plug-pack input, 22 Ω 5 W load (about 230 mA). P = 7 × 0.23 ≈ 1.6 W. Without a heatsink the tab gets too hot to touch within a minute (+80–100 °C). Power off, fit the heatsink, repeat: much cooler.

Why it gets hot

Power is voltage × current. The current through the regulator is the same as the load current, and the voltage across it is everything you are not using. A linear regulator is basically an automatically adjusted resistor: it can only throw voltage away as heat. Energy is conserved, so 12 V × 0.5 A going in must equal 5 V × 0.5 A going to the load plus 3.5 W of heat.

2 · The LM317: any voltage you like

The LM317 is an adjustable linear regulator. It keeps exactly 1.25 V between its OUT and ADJ pins. Put a resistor R1 across those pins and a fixed 1.25 V / R1 current flows through it, then on down through R2 to ground. The output therefore sits at 1.25 V plus the voltage across R2.

Vout = 1.25 × (1 + R2 / R1) R2 = R1 × (Vout / 1.25 − 1) For 9 V with R1 = 240 Ω: R2 = 240 × (7.2 − 1) = 1488 Ω → 1.5 kΩ gives 9.06 V. A small current also flows out of the ADJ pin (about 50 µA), which adds a few tens of millivolts; ignore it for most work. Watch the TO-220 pin-out: it is different from the 7805, and the tab is OUT, not ground.

3 · Buck converters: why they run cool

A buck converter (step-down switching regulator) does not burn off the extra voltage. A transistor switches the input on and off very fast (about 150 kHz on LM2596-type modules), and an inductor and capacitor smooth the pulses into steady DC. The output voltage is set by the duty cycle (P09): roughly Vout ≈ D × Vin. The switch is either fully on (tiny voltage across it) or fully off (no current through it), so it wastes very little. The inductor stores energy while the switch is on and keeps pushing current to the load while it is off.

Worked example: 12 V to 5 V at 2 A

7805-style linearBuck converter at 90%
Power to the load5 V × 2 A = 10 W10 W
Power from the 12 V supply12 V × 2 A = 24 W10 / 0.9 = 11.1 W
Input current2 A (same as output)11.1 / 12 = 0.93 A (less than output)
Heat in the regulator14 W (needs a big heatsink)1.1 W (warm)

Notice the buck's input current is less than its output current. Power in ≈ power out, so when the voltage steps down, the current steps up. That surprises people the first time they measure it.

  1. Set the module before connecting a load. 12 V into IN+ and IN−. Meter on OUT. Turn the multi-turn trim pot until the output reads 5.0 V. (These pots can take 10–20 turns to move noticeably; some modules are shipped set near the maximum.)

  2. Load it with 10 Ω 5 W (0.5 A, 2.5 W).

  3. Measure four numbers: Vin, Iin (meter in series with the input), Vout, Iout.

  4. Calculate efficiency = (Vout × Iout) / (Vin × Iin). Expect Iin about 0.23–0.27 A and efficiency about 75–90%.

  5. Feel the module. Barely warm. Compare with the 7805 doing the same job: (12 − 5) × 0.5 = 3.5 W.

Bench tip: cheap modules

Many low-cost buck modules use cloned chips and small inductors. Treat the headline current ("3 A") as a peak, and plan on about half of it continuously. They also make high-frequency switching noise; keep them away from sensitive analogue inputs, and add the 100 nF decoupling from P07 at the circuit you are powering.

4 · Plug-packs and building power: 12 V DC, 24 V DC, 24 V AC

Read a plug-pack label before you plug anything in: output voltage, AC or DC, current rating, and the polarity symbol (a dot with + on the centre means centre-positive, the usual for 12 V gear). DC barrels come in 2.1 mm and 2.5 mm centre-pin sizes that look almost identical: a 2.5 mm plug on a 2.1 mm socket gives an intermittent fault. Modern plug-packs are regulated switch-mode supplies; older heavy ones are transformers whose output rises well above the label at light load (P06).

In Australia

The ELV limit you have been working inside comes from AS/NZS 3000 (the Wiring Rules): up to 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC. Fixed wiring of the 230 V side of any building power supply, transformer or battery charger must be done by a licensed electrician. Capacity in amp-hours (Ah) is current × time. A 7 Ah SLA can supply 0.35 A for 20 hours (that is the "20-hour rate" it is tested at). At higher currents you get less: perhaps 5–6 Ah at a 1-hour discharge, depending on the battery. Cold reduces capacity; heat shortens life. SLA batteries in panels are typically replaced every few years (often 3–5); follow the manufacturer and the site's maintenance schedule.

Lithium safety

Never charge Li-ion cells without a charger made for them, never puncture or crush them, never short them, and do not use unprotected loose cells for projects. Damaged lithium cells can catch fire hours later. Product Safety Australia (run by the ACCC) publishes guidance on lithium-ion battery safety; read it before building anything with 18650s.

Standby battery sizing

An alarm or access-control system must keep running from its battery for a required standby time after mains fails, and then still have enough left to run in alarm (sirens, strobes, locks) for a required time. The sum is simple:

Ah needed = (Istandby × Tstandby + Ialarm × Talarm) × derating Currents in amps, times in hours. The derating factor (often 1.25 or more) covers battery ageing, temperature and the fact that a battery is "flat" well before 0 Ah. The required times come from the standard that applies to the system (for example AS/NZS 2201.1 for intruder alarms, AS 1670.1 for fire detection), the client's specification and the panel maker. Confirm the current requirement for each job.

Worked example. An alarm panel with keypad and detectors draws 250 mA in standby. The siren and strobe add up to 800 mA in alarm. Suppose the specification asks for 24 hours standby then 30 minutes of alarm. Ah = (0.25 × 24 + 0.8 × 0.5) × 1.25 = (6 + 0.4) × 1.25 = 8.0 Ah. A 7 Ah battery is not enough; fit a 12 Ah, or reduce the standby load.

On the job

Battery tests are routine maintenance on alarm and access-control panels. A battery that reads 13.6 V on float can still be dead: its voltage collapses as soon as it carries load. Techs test under load (many panels have a battery test function, and battery testers exist for this) and record the date. Measure the standby current of a panel with the meter in series with the battery lead after mains is switched off at the panel's own supply; the panel's installation manual usually has a battery calculation sheet that does what the widget above does.

6 · Fuses and polyfuses

A fuse is a thin metal link that melts when too much current flows. Its job is to protect the cable (and prevent fire) when there is a short circuit, not to protect the device. Choose a fuse rating above the normal current (usually about 1.25–1.5 times it) and below what the cable can safely carry.

  • Fast-blow fuses open quickly; use them for electronics that has no switch-on surge.
  • Slow-blow (time-delay, "T" marked) fuses tolerate a short inrush, like the capacitor charging in P07 or a motor starting.
  • Blade fuses (car type) are common in 12 V distribution because they are cheap and easy to check.
  • Polyfuses (PTC resettable fuses) are plastic with carbon particles. Overcurrent heats them, the plastic expands, the carbon paths break, and resistance jumps. They limit current instead of melting, and reset after the power is removed and they cool. They have a hold current (will not trip) and a trip current (about twice the hold), are slower than fuses, and drop a little voltage even when cold. Security power supplies often use one PTC per output.

  1. Fuse test. 12 V plug-pack → inline holder with a 0.5 A fuse → 22 Ω 5 W (0.55 A). The fuse may survive for a while (fuses are slow near their rating). Swap to 10 Ω (1.2 A): it blows within seconds. Check the fuse on continuity.

  2. Polyfuse test. Replace the fuse with a 0.5 A hold PTC and the 10 Ω load. After a few seconds the current drops to a few tens of milliamps as it trips (measure it, and feel the PTC: warm). Remove power, wait a minute, re-apply with the 22 Ω: it works again.

7 · Cable voltage drop: the 12 V camera at the end of a long run

This is the calculation that separates a reliable installation from a call-back. Every metre of copper has resistance. Current flows out on one conductor and back on the other, so the cable's resistance counts twice: the loop resistance.

Rloop = 2 × L × (ρ / A) Vdrop = I × Rloop L is the one-way run length in metres. ρ (rho) for copper is about 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C, so resistance per metre = 0.0172 / A, with A in mm². Copper's resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so a cable in a hot ceiling space is a few percent worse.

Worked example: the camera that reboots at night

A 12 V camera draws 0.25 A by day. At night its infrared LEDs switch on and it draws 0.5 A. It is 50 m from the power supply on 0.5 mm² two-core cable. The camera's data sheet says 12 V DC ±10%, so it needs at least 10.8 V.

  • Loop resistance: 2 × 50 × 0.0344 = 3.44 Ω.
  • Day: drop = 0.25 × 3.44 = 0.86 V → camera gets 11.1 V. Works.
  • Night: drop = 0.5 × 3.44 = 1.72 V → camera gets 10.3 V. Below 10.8 V. The IR switches on, the voltage sags, the camera browns out and reboots, the IR switches off, the voltage recovers, and the cycle repeats. The customer says "the camera goes off at night".

Fixes, from best to worst: use heavier cable (1.5 mm²: loop 1.15 Ω, drop 0.58 V, camera gets 11.4 V); move the power supply closer; use PoE or a 24 V camera; or set an adjustable supply slightly higher, only if every device on that output tolerates the higher voltage when it draws little current.

  1. Measure your cable. Twist the two conductors together at the far end of your 10–20 m length. Measure the resistance across the near end on the lowest ohms range (subtract the reading with the probes touching). That is the loop resistance. Compare with the table.

  2. Load it. Untwist the far end and connect the 22 Ω 5 W resistor there. Power the near end from the 12 V plug-pack.

  3. Measure at both ends. Voltage at the supply end, then at the resistor. The difference is the drop. Check it against I × Rloop (I ≈ 12 / (22 + Rloop)).

  4. Double the conductors. If the cable has four cores, use two in parallel for + and two for −. Loop resistance halves; so does the drop.

On the job

Voltage drop explains a large share of "intermittent" faults in CCTV, access control and alarms: cameras that reboot when IR comes on, maglocks that do not hold properly at the end of a long run, readers that beep and reset when the lock fires, and devices that work on the bench but not on site. Measure the voltage at the device terminals, while it is drawing its highest current (IR on, lock energised, siren sounding). This is also why 24 V and PoE are popular for long runs: for the same power, doubling the voltage halves the current, and the percentage drop falls to a quarter.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The supply reads 12 V at its terminals, so power is fine." Right: what matters is the voltage at the device while it works hardest. With no load, a long cable drops nothing at all (V = I × R, and I = 0), so a meter on an idle circuit always looks perfect. Measure under load, at the far end.

Check yourself

A 7805 feeds 300 mA from a 15 V input. How much heat, and is a bare TO-220 OK?

P = (15 − 5) × 0.3 = 3 W. At roughly 60 °C/W for a bare TO-220, that is a rise of about 180 °C: far too hot, it will shut down. Use a heatsink of about 15 °C/W or better, a lower input voltage, or a buck converter.

Choose R2 for 12 V out from an LM317 with R1 = 240 Ω. What minimum input do you need?

R2 = 240 × (12 / 1.25 − 1) = 240 × 8.6 = 2064 Ω. Use 2.0 kΩ (11.67 V) or 2.2 kΩ (12.7 V), or 1.8 kΩ plus a 500 Ω trim pot to set it exactly. Allow about 3 V of headroom: input at least 15 V.

A buck converter delivers 5 V at 3 A from 24 V with 88% efficiency. What is the input current?

Pout = 15 W. Pin = 15 / 0.88 = 17.0 W. Iin = 17.0 / 24 = 0.71 A. Less than a quarter of the output current, because power, not current, is (nearly) conserved.

Why do security power supplies output 13.6–13.8 V and not 12.0 V?

Because they also keep a 12 V lead-acid battery charged. A 12 V lead-acid battery has six cells and needs about 2.25–2.3 V per cell to float-charge, which is about 13.5–13.8 V. The connected devices are designed to accept that range.

Size the battery: 400 mA standby for 24 h, 1.2 A alarm for 30 min, derating 1.25.

(0.4 × 24 + 1.2 × 0.5) × 1.25 = (9.6 + 0.6) × 1.25 = 12.75 Ah. A 12 Ah battery is just short; choose 18 Ah or reduce the standby load. (The standby and alarm times must come from the applicable standard and specification.)

A maglock draws 0.5 A at 12 V, 80 m away on 14/0.20 alarm cable. What voltage reaches it?

14/0.20 is about 0.44 mm², so about 0.039 Ω/m. Loop resistance = 2 × 80 × 0.039 ≈ 6.3 Ω. Drop = 0.5 × 6.3 ≈ 3.1 V. The lock gets about 8.9 V and will have reduced holding force. Use heavier cable, double up cores, use a 24 V lock and supply, or put the supply near the door.

What does a fuse protect, and how do you choose its rating?

Mainly the cable and wiring, against overheating and fire in a fault. Choose a rating above the normal running current (about 1.25–1.5 times) and below the safe current of the smallest cable it protects. Use slow-blow where there is inrush.

Why does a meter on an idle long cable show no voltage drop?

Drop = I × R. With no load current (the meter draws almost nothing), the drop is zero whatever the resistance. You must measure while the device draws its normal or maximum current.
