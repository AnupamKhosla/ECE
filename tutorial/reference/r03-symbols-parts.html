Schematic symbols and part identification

Read a schematic, name a part from its body, and decode resistor, capacitor and chip markings. Symbols are shown in the international (IEC) style and the older US style side by side. A decoder near the end reads colour bands and chip codes for you.

Read the legend first

A schematic shows how parts connect, not what they look like. Each part is drawn as a symbol. The same part can have more than one symbol, because different standards and different countries chose different pictures.

IEC style (international)

Based on IEC 60617. A resistor is a plain rectangle. This is the style on most current drawings and in most manufacturer manuals outside the USA.

US style (older ANSI/IEEE)

A resistor is a zig-zag. You will meet it in US datasheets, hobby sites, older manuals and imported equipment.

In Australia

Older Australian drawings may cite AS 1102 (graphical symbols for electrotechnical documentation). Most drawings now use IEC 60617-style symbols. Standards are revised and replaced, so a standard number on an old drawing can be out of date. If a job or contract cites a standard, ask for the edition that applies and confirm it with Standards Australia.

Bench tip

Always read the drawing's legend or symbol list. Symbols differ by drafter and by country. If there is no legend, use the label next to the symbol (R12, K3, F1) and the parts list, and ask before you guess.

Common misconception

"A symbol always means one fixed part." It does not. A plain rectangle is a resistor in IEC style, but it can also be a relay coil, and with a line through it, a fuse. The legend and the label decide, not the shape alone.

Switches, relays and fuses

Switch (SPST)S or SW. One blade, one path. Shown open (off).
Changeover switch (SPDT)S or SW. COM joins either NC or NO. Same idea as a relay changeover contact.
Push-button, normally openS or PB. Makes the circuit while pressed.

A relay is drawn in two places. The coil is one symbol. Its contacts are separate symbols, often on a different part of the drawing, and share a label with the coil (K1 coil, K1 contacts). Contacts are always drawn with the coil off.

Safety

"Earth", "chassis" and "0 V" are not the same thing. Do not join them because they look alike on a drawing. The protective earth conductor is a safety system, and mains wiring is licensed electrical work in Australia. You work at extra-low voltage only (at most 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC).

How to read an unfamiliar schematic

  1. Legend and title. Read the title block, revision and legend first. Note which standard the symbols follow.

  2. Power. Find the supply. By habit, positive rails run along the top and 0 V (ground) along the bottom.

  3. Signal direction. By habit, inputs are on the left and outputs on the right. Follow one signal at a time.

  4. Net names. A label such as "+12V" or "RESET" joins every point with the same name, even with no wire drawn between them.

On the job

On site you read symbols on panel drawings, wiring diagrams and as-built sets: fuses, relay coils and contacts, terminals, earth bars and 12/24 V rails. Techs who can read a legend quickly find the fault faster. See P22 for the drawing types.

Bench tip

The same package does not mean the same pinout. A BC547 and a 2N3904 both come in TO-92. With the flat face toward you and the legs down, the BC547 reads C-B-E left to right, and the 2N3904 reads E-B-C. Always check the datasheet for that exact part, or find the base with the diode-test mode (P03).

Safety

An electrolytic or tantalum capacitor fitted backwards, or used above its voltage rating, can vent, burst or overheat. Match the polarity, choose a voltage rating comfortably above the circuit voltage (at least 1.5 times), and keep your face back when you first power a new build. Large capacitors can hold their charge after the power is off. Discharge them through a resistor, not a screwdriver. Everything on this page assumes extra-low voltage only.

Bench tip

Part numbers are printed on most through-hole parts. A four-digit date code such as 2315 often means year 23, week 15, but that is not universal. SMD parts often carry only a short code. A code like "1AM" does not name one part; look it up in the maker's marking list or datasheet before you replace it.

On the job

The part you replace most is the fuse, followed by electrolytic capacitors in power supplies and relays. Before you fit one, check polarity and rating. A flyback diode across a relay coil has its band toward the positive side. Photograph the old part and its position before you remove it.

Easy way to remember the digits: black is 0, brown is 1, then the rainbow in order for 2 to 7 (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet), then grey 8 and white 9. A rhyme if you like one: Better Be Right Or Your Great Big Venture Goes Wrong.

  1. Find the tolerance band. It is usually gold or silver, or it sits a little apart from the others. Turn the resistor so that band is on the right. Gold and silver can never be a first digit, so if one is on the left, you are reading from the wrong end.

  2. Count the bands. 4 bands: two digits, a multiplier, then tolerance. 5 bands: three digits, a multiplier, then tolerance. 5-band parts are usually 1 % metal-film resistors.

  3. Work it out. Join the digits into one number and multiply. Yellow-violet-red = 47 × 100 = 4 700 Ω = 4.7 kΩ.

  4. Check it. Is the answer a standard value (table below)? If not, you probably misread a band. Then confirm with a meter (P03). Measure with the resistor out of the circuit, or with one leg lifted, otherwise other parts in parallel pull the reading down.

Go deeper: why E24 steps are about 10 % apart

Each E24 step multiplies by 101/24 ≈ 1.10. With a ±5 % tolerance, a 4.7 kΩ part can be anywhere from 4.47 to 4.94 kΩ, and the next value up (5.1 kΩ) runs from 4.85 to 5.36 kΩ. The ranges overlap a little, so any resistance you need is covered by some standard part. E96 steps are about 2.4 % apart, to suit ± 1 % parts.

Go deeper: why a letter for the decimal point?

A printed decimal point is tiny and can vanish on a worn or small part. The international marking code (IEC 60062) puts a letter where the point would be, so 4R7 cannot be misread as 47. It also marks the unit: R for ohms, K for kilohms and M for megohms.

Check yourself

A ceramic capacitor is marked 104K. What is its value and tolerance?

The first two digits are 10, and the third digit 4 means four zeros: 10 × 104 = 100 000 pF. That is 100 nF (0.1 µF). The letter K means ± 10 %.

A resistor's bands read brown, black, orange, gold from one end. What is its value? What clue tells you that you have read it from the right end?

Brown = 1, black = 0, orange = × 1 000, so 10 × 1 000 = 10 kΩ, gold = ± 5 %. From the other end it would start with gold, and gold is never a first digit. A gold or silver band on the left means you have it backwards.

One SMD resistor is marked 4R7 and another is marked 472. How far apart are the values?

4R7 uses R as the decimal point, so it is 4.7 Ω. 472 is 47 × 102 = 4 700 Ω. They differ by a factor of 1 000. Fitting the wrong one is a common cause of a circuit that works badly.

You find a small moulded SMD capacitor with a stripe at one end, and you treat it like an electrolytic: stripe means negative. What is the risk?

On a moulded SMD tantalum capacitor, the stripe marks the positive end, which is the opposite of an electrolytic can. Fitted backwards, it can fail short and overheat. Check the board's silkscreen and the datasheet for that part. Aluminium SMD electrolytics mark the negative side with a dark mark on top.

One drawing shows a resistor as a zig-zag and another shows it as a plain box. Are they different parts? What should you do if a plain box has the label K2?

No. They are the same part in two drawing styles: US (zig-zag) and IEC (box). A box labelled K2 is not a resistor. K is the usual letter for a relay, so it is probably a relay coil. This is why you read the legend and the label, not just the shape.

A DIP-8 chip has its notch at the top. Where are pin 1 and pin 5?

Pin 1 is at the top left, next to the notch. Pins count down the left side (1, 2, 3, 4), then up the right side. So pin 5 is at the bottom right and pin 8 at the top right. That is anticlockwise when viewed from above.

Skills checklist

  • I can name the common symbols and tell IEC from US style, and I read the legend before I trust a symbol.
  • I use reference letters and labels (R, C, Q, K, F) to confirm what a symbol is.
  • I can read 4-band and 5-band resistors and check them with a meter.
  • I can decode SMD resistor codes: 3-digit, 4-digit, R as a decimal point and zero-ohm links.
  • I can read 3-digit capacitor codes (104, 103, 222) and the J, K, M tolerance letters.
  • I can find pin 1 on a DIP, SOIC and transistor package.
  • I check polarity (diode band, LED flat side, electrolytic stripe, tantalum stripe) before I power anything.
