Mains, three-phase, earthing and RCDs (Australia)

You will work in buildings full of 230 V and 400 V wiring, even though you will never work on it. This chapter explains how Australian mains is delivered, why 400 V appears between phases, how the MEN earthing system and the RCD (safety switch) protect people, what current does to a body, and where the legal line sits between your ELV work and an electrician's LV work.

Read this first

This is a theory chapter. It contains no instructions for working on mains, and none should be inferred. In Australia, electrical work on low-voltage (230/400 V) installations and equipment is legally restricted to licensed electricians. Never open a switchboard, remove a cover, or test mains circuits. The purpose of this chapter is to make you a safer, more useful technician beside the electricians you will work with.

A short history: from 240 V to 230 V, and the safety switch

Tesla's polyphase system (T07) became the three-phase supply that almost every grid uses today. Australian cities began with a mix of DC and AC supplies at different voltages, then standardised during the twentieth century on 240 V, 50 Hz single-phase and 415 V three-phase. To align with international practice, Australian Standard AS 60038 changed the nominal voltage to 230 V (400 V three-phase) around the year 2000. The wires did not change; the tolerance band was set so that existing 240 V supplies still complied, which is why many homes still measure about 240 V.

On the safety side, the residual current device (RCD) was developed in Europe in the mid twentieth century to detect small leakage currents to earth. Australia began requiring RCDs (called safety switches in everyday Australian English) on power circuits in new homes in the early 1990s, and later editions of the Wiring Rules widened the requirement. Electrical deaths in homes have fallen as RCDs spread. The rule details are in AS/NZS 3000 and state regulations; check the current edition and your state regulator for exact requirements.

How power reaches a building

Electricity is generated, stepped up by transformers (T08) to high voltage for long-distance transmission, stepped down at zone substations to distribution voltages, and finally stepped down near the customer to 400/230 V.

Three-phase: why 400 V = 230 V × √3

The local transformer has three secondary windings, one for each phase, connected in a star (also called wye). One end of each winding joins at the star point, which becomes the neutral. The other ends are the three actives (also called phases or lines: L1, L2, L3, or traditionally the red, white and blue phases). Each active is 230 V RMS from neutral, but the three voltages are shifted by 120° (one third of a cycle, 6.67 ms) from each other.

The voltage between two actives is the difference of two 230 V phasors 120° apart. Draw the two arrows from the same point and join their tips: the triangle has two sides of 230 and an included angle of 120°, so the third side is

Vline = 2 × 230 × sin(60°) = 230 × √3 ≈ 398 V ≈ 400 V "Line-to-line" voltage between two actives = √3 × "line-to-neutral" voltage. The same √3 explains the old 240/415 V pair (240 × 1.732 ≈ 415).

Why bother with three phases?

  • Less copper for the same power. In a balanced load the three phase currents add to zero at every instant, so the neutral carries little or no current. Three (or four) wires deliver three times the power of one single-phase pair.
  • Smooth power. Single-phase power pulses at 100 Hz (T07). Three phases together deliver constant total power, so big motors run smoothly.
  • A rotating magnetic field for free. Three coils spaced 120° around a motor, fed from three phases 120° apart in time, produce a field that rotates. That is Tesla's induction motor, which drives most pumps, fans and chillers in the buildings you will service (C03).

Worked example. A 20 kW balanced heating load, power factor 1. On three-phase 400 V: I = P / (√3 × Vline) = 20 000 / (1.732 × 400) ≈ 28.9 A per phase. The same 20 kW on single-phase 230 V would need 20 000 / 230 ≈ 87 A, which is why commercial buildings, lifts, chillers and EV fast chargers use three-phase.

Common misconception

"Neutral is safe to touch because it's connected to earth." Neutral carries the full load current back to the transformer, and it is only near earth potential while it is intact. If a neutral is disconnected somewhere, the neutral on the load side can sit at the full active voltage through the load. Electricians treat neutral as live for exactly this reason. Also, never trust colours: older installations, mistakes and other countries' conventions all exist. This is one more reason mains testing belongs to licensed people with the right instruments and procedures.

The MEN system in plain words

Australia (and New Zealand) use the MEN system: Multiple Earthed Neutral. In plain words:

  1. The supply network's neutral is connected to earth at the distribution transformer and at many other points along the network. That is the "multiple".
  2. At each building's main switchboard, the neutral bar is connected to the main earth bar by one link, the MEN link.
  3. The main earth bar is also connected to an earth electrode (a metal stake driven into the ground) by the main earthing conductor.
  4. Every circuit's protective earth wire runs from the earth bar to the metal case of each Class I appliance, each earth pin on a socket, and so on.

Why earthing makes a fault trip the breaker

Imagine a toaster with a metal case. A wire inside chafes and the active touches the case.

Without an earth connection, nothing happens at first: there is no complete path, so no fault current flows and the breaker does not trip. The case now sits at 230 V, waiting. The next person who touches the case while also touching a tap or standing on a damp floor completes the circuit through their body.

With the earth connected, the active is now joined to the case, the case to the earth wire, the earth bar to the neutral through the MEN link, and the neutral back to the transformer. This earth-fault loop is made of copper and has a low impedance, often well under an ohm to a couple of ohms. A huge current flows immediately.

Worked example. Suppose the total fault-loop impedance is 0.8 Ω. Fault current ≈ 230 / 0.8 ≈ 290 A. The circuit is protected by a 20 A circuit breaker with a "C" curve, which trips instantly (magnetically) for currents above about 5–10 times its rating, so 100–200 A. 290 A is above that, so it trips in a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, isolating the fault. During those milliseconds the case does rise in voltage, because the fault current flows through the earth wire's resistance, typically to something like a third to a half of 230 V. That is why the protection must be fast. The Wiring Rules set maximum disconnection times and maximum fault-loop impedances, and electricians test them when they verify an installation.

Common misconception

"Earthing works by sending the fault current into the ground." In the MEN system, the soil is a poor conductor compared with copper: an earth stake might have tens of ohms to the general mass of earth. Most of the fault current returns through the MEN link and the neutral, which is why the current is large enough to trip the breaker. The earth stake's main jobs are to hold the whole earthing system near the potential of the ground people stand on, and to provide a backup path if something else fails.

In Australia

A tingle or shock from a tap, shower, metal fence or appliance can be a sign of a broken neutral or earthing fault in the MEN system. It is treated as an emergency: stop using it, keep people away, and report it straight away to the local electricity distributor and a licensed electrician. Do not investigate it yourself.

RCDs: the safety switch

A circuit breaker protects the cables: it trips at amps or hundreds of amps. A human heart can be stopped by tens of milliamps, which a 20 A breaker will never notice. That gap is filled by the RCD.

The principle is beautifully simple, and you already know it from the current transformer (T08). Both the active and the neutral of a circuit pass through one small toroidal core. In a healthy circuit, every electron that goes out on the active comes back on the neutral, so the two currents are equal and opposite and their fields cancel in the core: no flux, no signal. If some current leaks to earth, for example through a person touching an active part while standing on the ground, the return current on the neutral is smaller than the active current. The difference, called the residual current, makes a net flux in the core, which induces a voltage in a sense winding. The sense circuit trips a mechanism that opens both active and neutral.

  • 30 mA is the standard trip sensitivity for personal protection. It is chosen to be below the level at which a shock lasting a fraction of a second is likely to cause ventricular fibrillation for most adults.
  • Speed matters as much as sensitivity. Standards require a 30 mA RCD to trip within 300 ms at its rated leakage and much faster at higher leakage; real devices typically trip in a few tens of milliseconds.
  • An RCD does not limit the current to 30 mA. You will still feel a nasty shock. It limits the time, which is what keeps the heart in rhythm.
Common misconception

"With an RCD, you cannot be electrocuted." An RCD only detects current that leaks to earth. If someone is standing on an insulating surface and touches active with one hand and neutral with the other, the current goes out on active and back on neutral through their body: active and neutral currents are still equal, and the RCD sees nothing. The breaker sees only a normal-sized load. RCDs dramatically reduce risk; they do not remove it. And an RCD that is never tested may not work when needed, which is why the test button exists and why electricians periodically test RCDs with instruments.

Circuit breakers and fuses

Both are overcurrent devices. Their job is to protect wiring and equipment from overheating (overload) and from the huge currents of a short circuit.

Breaker curves exist because loads differ. A motor or a big switch-mode supply draws a high inrush current at switch-on, which would trip a sensitive B-curve breaker; a C or D curve rides through it. On the job

You will meet the same ideas at extra-low voltage. A 12 V or 24 V DC power supply for cameras or access control has fused outputs or electronic current limiting to protect the cables you run. A lead-acid or lithium backup battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short, so its lead must be fused close to the battery terminal. The physics of "protect the cable from the source's fault current" is identical; only the voltages differ. See P10.

What electric current does to a body

Your nerves and heart run on tiny electrical signals. An external current through your body can override them. The effects depend on the size of the current, its path (hand-to-hand and hand-to-feet paths cross the chest and heart), its duration, and its frequency (50 Hz AC is, unluckily, close to the worst frequency for the heart; DC is less likely to cause fibrillation at the same current). The international reference is IEC 60479 (published in Australia as AS/NZS 60479). The figures below are indicative for 50 Hz AC, hand-to-feet, for an adult:

Hundreds of mA and moreFibrillation likely even for short times; burns at contact points and inside the body

"It's the current that kills": half right

True: the physiological damage is done by current flowing through tissue, not by voltage as such. But current does not choose itself. It is set by the voltage divided by the body's impedance, and the body's impedance is not a fixed number. Dry, intact skin has a high resistance at low voltage; wet or broken skin, large contact areas and higher voltages all reduce it. Above roughly 50–100 V, the skin itself starts to break down electrically, so the impedance drops further just when the voltage is rising. Published data (IEC 60479) give typical total body impedances in the order of a few thousand ohms at 25 V and around a thousand ohms at 230 V, for a hand-to-hand path with large dry contacts.

Worked comparison (indicative). At 230 V with about 1000 Ω: I ≈ 230 mA, deep in the dangerous zone. At 24 V with about 3000 Ω: I ≈ 8 mA, which may be felt and is unpleasant in wet conditions but is far less likely to be dangerous. That ratio is the whole idea behind the ELV limits: below them, under normal conditions, the voltage cannot push a dangerous current through a person. So the correct statement is: current kills, voltage decides whether you get that current.

Safety

Even ELV is not harmless in every situation: wet locations, broken skin, and especially current paths inside the body need much lower limits, which is why the Wiring Rules apply stricter rules to bathrooms and pools. And a 12 V battery that cannot shock you can still deliver hundreds of amps into a dropped spanner, causing burns and fires. Respect every source.

AS/NZS 3000, ELV and LV, and the law

AS/NZS 3000, "Electrical installations", is known everywhere as the Wiring Rules. It is the joint Australian/New Zealand standard for how electrical installations are designed, built and verified, and state laws call it up, so following it is a legal requirement for electrical work. It defines the voltage bands you need to know:

BandACRipple-free DCExamples
Extra-low voltage (ELV)Not exceeding 50 VNot exceeding 120 V12/24 V DC security and BMS supplies, 24 V AC control transformers' secondaries, PoE (about 44–57 V DC), batteries
Low voltage (LV)Above ELV, up to 1000 VAbove ELV, up to 1500 V230 V outlets and lights, 400 V three-phase, solar arrays on the DC side of many inverters
High voltage (HV)Above 1000 VAbove 1500 V11 kV and 22 kV distribution, transmission lines

Note the confusing name: in the electrical industry, "low voltage" means mains, up to 1000 V AC. In casual talk and in some security and AV marketing, "low voltage" is used loosely for 12–24 V cabling. On an Australian site, "LV" means 230/400 V, and "ELV" means your work.

Who may do what

  • LV electrical work (installing, altering, repairing or testing 230/400 V wiring and equipment) must be done by a licensed electrician, and contracting for it needs an electrical contractor licence. This applies in every state and territory, with only narrow exemptions.
  • ELV work generally does not need an electrical licence, but other rules apply: telecommunications and data cabling needs ACMA cabling registration (C07), and installing security equipment needs a security licence in most states (C11).
  • Equipment that straddles both, such as a power supply with a 230 V input and a 12 V output, or an access control panel with a mains-fed supply inside, needs an electrician for the mains connection. You connect the ELV side after it is installed and covered.
In Australia: confirm with your regulator

Electrical licensing is run by each state and territory, and rules and exemptions differ and change. Examples of regulators include Energy Safe Victoria and Queensland's Electrical Safety Office; other states have their own fair trading, building or energy safety regulators. Before you rely on any rule in this chapter for real work, confirm the current requirement with the regulator in the state where you will work, and ask your employer what licences and site inductions they require.

What an ELV technician must still know when working near switchboards

You will often work in comms rooms, plant rooms and risers with switchboards nearby. You do not work on them, but you must work safely around them.

  • Never open, remove or reach behind a switchboard cover, escutcheon or cabinet panel that guards LV parts. If your task needs access, an electrician does that part.
  • Recognise LV. Know what LV cables, terminals, circuit breakers and warning labels look like, and assume anything you cannot positively identify is live.
  • Segregation. ELV cabling (data, security, BMS) must be kept separated from LV cabling by distance or barriers; telecommunications cabling has its own separation rules in the ACMA cabling standard (AS/CA S009). Never run your cable through a switchboard or share a conduit with power cables.
  • Isolation and lock-out are not yours to do on LV circuits. If an electrician has isolated something for you, respect their locks and danger tags; never remove another person's lock or tag.
  • Report and stop. If you find damaged LV cables, scorch marks, a hot smell, buzzing equipment or a missing cover, stop, keep people away and report it.
  • Know the emergency. Know where the main switch and the site's emergency procedure are. Workers around LV are often required to hold current CPR and low-voltage rescue training; ask your employer which courses they need.
On the job

A typical CCTV or access control job: the electrician installs a 230 V outlet or a hard-wired supply in the comms cabinet; you mount the 12/24 V power supply, run the cables, terminate the ELV outputs, connect batteries with an inline fuse, and commission the system. When you meet a BMS controller sitting in a mechanical switchboard next to 400 V motor starters, the same split applies: you land the ELV sensor and comms wiring only after the LV compartment is closed or separately guarded (C01).

Try this (no electricity needed)

Look at your home's switchboard. Open only the outer door that householders normally open, and never remove any cover, panel or escutcheon. Identify the main switch, the circuit breakers, and the RCDs (they have a "T" or "Test" button and are often labelled "safety switch"). Count how many circuits each RCD covers. If the property owner agrees, press each RCD's test button (that is what it is for; it is a user test designed for occupants) and note what turns off. Then reset it. If an RCD does not trip when tested, tell the owner to have it checked by an electrician.

Check yourself

Why is the voltage between two phases 400 V and not 460 V?

The two phase voltages are 120° apart, not 180°. The difference of two 230 V phasors 120° apart is 2 × 230 × sin 60° = 230√3 ≈ 400 V. They would only give 460 V if they were exactly opposite.

In the MEN system, where does most of the fault current flow during an active-to-case fault?

Through the protective earth wire to the main earth bar, through the MEN link to the neutral bar, and back along the neutral to the transformer. That all-metal loop has low impedance, so the current is large enough to trip the breaker quickly. Little flows through the soil.

An appliance's case is not earthed and the active touches it. What happens, and why is this dangerous?

Nothing visible: no fault current flows, so the breaker does not trip. The case sits at 230 V until someone touches it and provides a path to earth. Only an RCD would then limit the shock duration.

How does an RCD detect a fault, and what kind of shock can it not detect?

It passes active and neutral through one core; if their currents differ by more than about 30 mA, the net flux trips it. It cannot detect current that goes out on active and returns on neutral through a person who is insulated from earth, because then active and neutral currents are still equal.

Why can't a 20 A circuit breaker protect a person from electric shock?

A breaker trips at amps (thermally) or many tens of amps (magnetically). A shock current of 50–200 mA can be lethal but is far below the breaker's threshold. Breakers protect cables; RCDs protect people.

"It's the current that kills, not the voltage." What is right and wrong with this?

Right: the damage is caused by current through tissue, especially the heart. Wrong as a safety rule: the current is set by the voltage divided by body impedance, and body impedance falls at higher voltages and with wet skin. A low voltage cannot push a dangerous current through a person in normal conditions; mains can. That is why ELV is defined as at most 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC.

What are the AS/NZS 3000 limits for ELV, and which of these is ELV: 24 V AC, PoE at about 50 V DC, 230 V AC, a 100 V DC battery string?

ELV: not exceeding 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC. 24 V AC: ELV. PoE around 44–57 V DC: ELV (DC limit is 120 V). 230 V AC: LV. A 100 V ripple-free DC battery string: within the DC ELV limit, but large batteries still carry serious fire and burn hazards and may have their own rules.

You are installing an access control power supply with a 230 V input. Which parts are yours?

A licensed electrician makes the 230 V connection (or installs the outlet it plugs into, where that is permitted) and ensures the LV parts are covered. You mount and cable the ELV side: outputs, battery with fuse, lock and controller wiring, and commissioning. Confirm the exact split with your employer and state rules.
