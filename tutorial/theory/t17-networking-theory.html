How networks really work: Ethernet, IP, TCP/UDP

How a camera's picture gets to a recorder, one layer at a time: Ethernet frames and MAC addresses, how a switch learns where everything is, IPv4 addresses and subnet masks worked by hand, ARP, DHCP, routing, TCP and UDP ports, and why one badly set multicast stream can flatten a whole camera network.

Reading ~85 minLevel Beginner → intermediateBuilds on T15, T16Used in P16, P18, P19, P20, C02, C05

Why a technician needs the theory

Almost every system you will install or commission now sits on an Ethernet network: IP cameras, access controllers, BMS controllers talking BACnet/IP or Modbus TCP, IoT sensors, intercoms, even some fire-panel gateways. When one of them stops working, the fault is often not in the device. It is in how the device talks to its neighbours: a wrong mask, a missing gateway, a VLAN, a blocked port, or a link that cannot carry the load.

Practice lab P19 teaches the hands-on skills: setting a static IP, finding devices, budgeting PoE, building a VLAN. This chapter is the mental model underneath, so the settings stop being magic words and you can work out the answer instead of guessing. Everything here is extra-low voltage: Ethernet signals are a few volts, and PoE is about 50 V DC.

A network is an agreement: the layers

Two machines can only swap data if they agree on many things at once: what a 1 and a 0 look like on the cable, how to name the machine next door, how to name a machine in another building, how to notice lost data, and what the data means. Engineers split these agreements into layers. Each layer does one job, uses the layer below as a service, and offers a service to the layer above. A protocol is the set of rules for one layer.

Think of posting a parcel. You write a letter (the application's message). You put it in an envelope with the street address (the IP address). The depot then puts it in a mailbag labelled for the next truck only (the Ethernet frame, whose address changes at every hop). At the far end each wrapper is removed in reverse order.

Each layer answers one question:

  • Link: how do I get bits to the device on the same cable or switch?
  • Internet: how do I get a packet to a device on another network?
  • Transport: which program gets it, and does it all arrive, in order?
  • Application: what does the data mean?
Bench tip

"It's a layer 1 problem" is an installer's joke for "check the cable first", and it is right most of the time. A wrong IP address (layer 3) is useless to debug if the link light (layer 1) is off. Always work from the bottom up. The fault-finding table near the end of this chapter does exactly that.

Ethernet frames and MAC addresses

On a wired LAN (local area network), the unit of data is the frame. Everything above it, the IP packet, the TCP segment and your actual data, rides inside the frame's payload like letters inside a mailbag.

The MAC address

A MAC address (media access control address) is a 48-bit number, written as six bytes in hexadecimal, for example A4:BB:6D:12:AB:34. It is burned into the network chip at the factory and names one network interface. Two halves have a meaning:

  • The first three bytes (A4:BB:6D) are the OUI, the maker's registered prefix. Free "OUI lookup" websites tell you the brand, which is a quick way to identify an unknown device on a site.
  • The last three bytes are the maker's serial number for that interface.

Two bits of the first byte are flags. Write A4 in binary: 1010 0100. The lowest bit (0) says unicast (one receiver); a 1 would mean a group address. The next bit (0) says globally unique; a 1 would mean "locally administered", which is what phones use when they randomise their MAC for privacy. Two special addresses to remember: FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF is the broadcast address (everyone on the local network), and addresses starting 01:00:5E are IPv4 multicast groups.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The MAC address is the device's true identity and travels with the data everywhere." Right: a MAC address only has meaning on the one cable or switch network the frame is crossing. When a router forwards a packet, it throws away the old frame and builds a new one with new MAC addresses. Only the IP addresses (and ports) stay the same end to end. This is why a remote site never sees your laptop's MAC.

Speeds, pairs and the frame check

The FCS (frame check sequence) at the end is a 32-bit CRC calculated from every other byte. The receiver recomputes it, and a frame that does not match is silently dropped. A switch port that shows rising "CRC" or "FCS error" counters is telling you about a bad cable, bad termination, interference, or a duplex mismatch. That is a layer 1 fault found with a layer 2 counter.

All copper Ethernet is limited to a 100 m channel: in structured cabling that is up to 90 m of fixed cable plus up to 10 m of patch cords (see C07). Beyond that you use fibre or a switch in between. Modern links are full duplex (each pair direction carries data at once), and the two ends auto-negotiate speed and duplex. The one habit to avoid is forcing one end to a fixed setting while the other end auto-negotiates: it can produce a duplex mismatch with slow speeds and CRC errors.

How a switch learns where everything is

Old networks used a hub: whatever came in on one port was repeated out of every other port, so everyone shared one wire and collided. A switch is smarter. It reads the destination MAC of each frame and sends the frame only where it needs to go. The clever part is that nobody tells the switch where devices are. It learns by watching:

  1. Learn from the source. A frame arrives on port 1 with source MAC A. The switch writes "A is on port 1" in its MAC address table (also called the CAM table).

  2. Look up the destination. If the destination MAC is in the table, the switch sends the frame out of that one port only.

  3. Flood if unknown. If the destination is not in the table yet, or it is the broadcast address, the switch copies the frame out of every port except the one it came in on.

  4. Forget slowly. Entries that are not refreshed expire, commonly after about five minutes, so the table follows devices that move.

    Loops and broadcast storms

    Because a switch floods broadcasts, a loop is dangerous. If you plug a patch cable from one port of a switch into another port of the same switch (or build a ring of switches), a broadcast frame circulates forever, and each trip copies it again. Within seconds every port is saturated: this is a broadcast storm. All the link lights blink in unison and the network is dead. Managed switches run STP/RSTP (spanning tree), which notices loops and blocks one link. Cheap unmanaged switches do not. "Someone plugged both ends of a cable into the same switch" is one of the most common causes of a whole site going down.

    Broadcast domains and VLANs

    A broadcast domain is the set of devices that all receive a broadcast frame sent by any one of them. A plain switch network is one broadcast domain, however many switches are chained together. Routers do not forward broadcasts, so each router port starts a new domain.

    Why care? Discovery tools, DHCP requests and ARP (below) are broadcasts. In a small network that is background noise. In a network with hundreds of devices, or where PCs and cameras share the same wire, it becomes both a load and a security problem: any PC can see, and try to talk to, every camera.

    A VLAN (virtual LAN) fixes this in software. A managed switch can be told "ports 1 to 12 belong to VLAN 10, ports 13 to 24 to VLAN 20". The switch then behaves as if it were two separate switches: broadcasts and floods stay inside their own VLAN. The standard is IEEE 802.1Q.

    • An access port belongs to one VLAN. The device plugged in (a camera, a PC) knows nothing about VLANs; the switch quietly adds and removes the label.
    • A trunk port carries several VLANs on one cable, for example from a switch to another switch or to a router. Each frame gets a 4-byte tag holding a 12-bit VLAN ID (usable values 1 to 4094) so the far end knows which VLAN it belongs to.
    • Traffic cannot cross from one VLAN to another except through a router or Layer 3 switch, because they are different broadcast domains. By convention each VLAN gets its own subnet.
    Common misconception

    Wrong: "Putting cameras on their own VLAN makes them secure." Right: a VLAN only separates the traffic. If the router lets everything through, the VLAN protects nothing. The security comes from the rules on the router or firewall between the VLANs. VLANs also do not help against a person who plugs a laptop into a camera port that has no port security.

    IPv4 addresses

    MAC addresses name an interface on one local network. They are flat and have no location, so they cannot work for the whole internet. For that we need the IP address, which has a network part (where) and a host part (who), like a street name and a house number.

    An IPv4 address is 32 bits. People write it as four octets (8 bits each) in decimal, separated by dots. Each octet is a number from 0 to 255 because eight bits can hold 2⁸ = 256 values. To convert, add up the place values of the bits that are 1:

    Bit position76543210
    Value1286432168421
    77 = 64 + 8 + 4 + 101001101
    192 = 128 + 6411000000

    So 192.168.1.77 is 11000000.10101000.00000001.01001101. You will not convert often, but you must be able to do it on paper when a subnet puzzle arrives.

    The three private ranges are never routed on the public internet. Your site's router uses NAT (network address translation) to swap a private address for its single public one on the way out, and swap back on the way in. It is also why a camera with a private address cannot be reached from the internet unless you deliberately open a path.

    In Australia

    Many mobile and fixed-wireless connections, and some home services, sit behind carrier-grade NAT: the provider shares one public address between many customers, so you cannot accept incoming connections at all. Remote viewing of a CCTV system then has to use a vendor cloud relay or a VPN rather than a port forward. Never forward camera ports straight to the internet; exposed cameras are found and attacked quickly. Ask your client's IT provider what the service actually offers.

    Subnet masks and CIDR

    How does a device know whether another IP address is on its own network? It uses a subnet mask, a 32-bit number made of a block of 1s followed by 0s. The 1s mark the network part and the 0s mark the host part. Writing the number of 1s after a slash gives the shorter CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing) notation: 255.255.255.0 has 24 ones, so it is /24. The old "class A, B, C" names (/8, /16, /24) survive in speech, but real networks use any prefix length.

    network = IP AND mask    broadcast = network OR (NOT mask)    usable hosts = 2(32 − prefix) − 2 AND keeps a bit only where both are 1, so ANDing with the mask wipes the host bits to zero and leaves the network address. The broadcast is all host bits set to 1. Two addresses are lost per subnet: the network address (all host bits 0) and the broadcast address (all host bits 1).

    Worked example 1: 192.168.1.0/24

    Mask 255.255.255.0 has 8 host bits. Network 192.168.1.0. Broadcast 192.168.1.255. Usable hosts 192.168.1.1 to 192.168.1.254, which is 2⁸ − 2 = 254. This is the common home and small-site subnet. A device at 192.168.1.50 and one at 192.168.1.200 are on the same subnet and talk directly through a switch.

    Worked example 2: 192.168.1.77/26

    /26 means 26 network bits and 6 host bits, so the mask is 255.255.255.192 and each subnet holds 2⁶ = 64 addresses. There is a quick shortcut called the block size method: block = 256 − 192 = 64. Subnets start at multiples of 64: .0, .64, .128, .192. The address .77 falls in the block that starts at .64. So the network is 192.168.1.64, the broadcast is the next network minus 1 (192.168.1.127), the usable range is .65 to .126, and there are 62 hosts. Figure 5 shows the same answer in bits.

    Worked example 3: splitting a /24 into four /26 subnets

    Worked example 4: how big a subnet do I need?

    A camera system has 32 cameras, 1 NVR, 2 PoE switches, 1 gateway and 1 laptop: 37 addresses. Allow about 50 % for growth: roughly 56. A /27 holds only 30, too small. A /26 holds 62, just enough. In practice many installers use a /24 for a camera VLAN because it is simple and forgiving. Use a smaller prefix only when a plan requires it or when several VLANs must share a block.

    The default gateway
    
    
    Every IP device is set up with four things: an IP address, a subnet mask, a default gateway and (usually) a DNS server. The gateway is the address of the router on this subnet, the exit door. Before sending, the device always asks one question: "Is the destination in my own subnet?" It answers by ANDing the destination with its own mask and comparing with its own network.
    
    
      
      
    • Yes, same subnet: send the frame directly to the destination's MAC address (found with ARP, below).
      • 
      
    • No, different subnet: send the frame to the gateway's MAC address, with the destination IP unchanged, and let the router take it from there.
      • 
    
    
    
    The gateway must itself be inside the device's own subnet, otherwise the device cannot reach it either. A convention is to give the router the first or last usable address, like 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.1.254.
    
    
    
      
    SymptomLikely cause
    
      
    Can reach devices in the same subnet but nothing else, and no internetGateway missing or wrong
    
      
    Camera answers ping from the router's side but its web page will not load from another VLANCamera has no gateway, so its replies never leave its own subnet
    
      
    Two devices with similar IPs cannot see each otherDifferent masks put them in different subnets (for example one /24 and one /26)
    
      
    Address starts 169.254The device asked for DHCP and nobody answered
    
    
    

    
    ARP: finding the MAC behind an IP address
    
    
    Layer 3 knows the destination's IP address, but the frame at layer 2 needs a MAC address. The bridge between the two is ARP (Address Resolution Protocol). The sender shouts to everyone: "Who has this IP? Tell me your MAC." The owner answers directly. The sender remembers the answer in its ARP cache (view it with arp -a) so it does not need to ask again for a few minutes.
    

    
    
    ARP also explains the pop-up "IP address conflict". When a device joins, it can send an ARP asking for its own address. If someone answers, two devices claim the same IP. It is one of the most common faults on sites where cameras are set with static addresses by different people. ARP is an IPv4 tool. IPv6 uses a similar mechanism called neighbour discovery.
    
    
    Try this
    On your own laptop at home, run arp -a in a terminal and look at the list. You will see your router's IP next to its MAC, plus other devices you recently talked to. Compare the first three bytes of a MAC with a free OUI lookup site to see the maker. Do this only on a network you own.
    

    
    DHCP, static addresses and reservations
    
    
    DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) hands out the four settings automatically. A new device has no address yet, so it must start with broadcasts:
    
    
      
      
    1. Discover. The device broadcasts from 0.0.0.0 to 255.255.255.255: "Is there a DHCP server?" (UDP port 67 on the server, 68 on the client.)
      2. 
      
    2. Offer. A server answers with an unused address, mask, gateway and DNS, valid for a lease time.
      3. 
      
    3. Request. The device replies: "I'll take that one." (Another server's offer is then politely declined.)
      4. 
      
    4. Acknowledge. The server confirms. The device renews quietly at about half the lease time.
      5. 
    
    
    
    Because step 1 is a broadcast, a DHCP server only serves its own VLAN, unless a relay on the router forwards the request. A second router with DHCP switched on, plugged in by mistake, becomes a rogue DHCP server and hands half the devices a wrong gateway.

    Why do cameras and controllers get static or reserved addresses? Because other things refer to them by address. The NVR has a camera's IP stored. A BMS front end polls a BACnet/IP controller or a Modbus TCP meter at a fixed IP. A firewall rule says "allow 10.10.10.10 to 10.10.10.0/24 on TCP 554". A door controller reports to a server at an address. If DHCP gave the device a new address after a power cut, every one of those links would break, and the first sign would be a camera showing "video loss".

    Routing versus switching
    
    
    A switch moves frames inside one network using MACs. A router moves packets between networks using IP addresses. They are different tools, and mixing them up is a very common mistake.

    Each packet has a TTL (time to live) number that every router reduces by one. When it reaches 0 the packet is thrown away and an error returns. That stops lost packets circling forever, and it is how traceroute lists each router on the way. A "Layer 3 switch" is a switch with routing built in, used to route between VLANs at wire speed. The box in a home or small office is several devices in one: router, 4-port switch, Wi-Fi access point, DHCP server, firewall and NAT.
    
    
    Common misconception
    Wrong: "A switch and a router do the same job; a router is just a bigger one." Right: a switch extends one network, a router connects separate networks. Connect two switches and you have one big broadcast domain. Connect two networks with a router and each stays its own domain. Plugging two networks that both use 192.168.1.0/24 into one router will not work without NAT, because the router cannot tell which is which.
    

    

    
    One request from end to end
    
    
    Put it all together. A laptop at 192.168.10.50/24 (gateway 192.168.10.1) opens the web page of a camera at 192.168.20.15:
    
    
      
      
    1. Same subnet? The laptop ANDs 192.168.20.15 with its /24 mask: 192.168.20.0, which is not its own 192.168.10.0. So the frame must go to the gateway.
      2. 
      
    2. ARP for the gateway. It broadcasts "who has 192.168.10.1?" and gets the router's MAC.
      3. 
      
    3. TCP handshake begins. The first frame (SYN to port 80) has destination MAC = router, destination IP = camera.
      4. 
      
    4. The router forwards. It reads the destination IP, finds 192.168.20.0/24 connected on port 2, does its own ARP for the camera, lowers the TTL and sends a new frame.
      5. 
      
    5. The reply comes back. The camera sees 192.168.10.50 is not in its subnet, so it sends the answer to its gateway. If the camera has no gateway set, the reply is lost and the page never loads, although ping from the router's side works fine.
      6. 
      
    6. HTTP. After the handshake the laptop sends its web request (GET) and the camera returns the page, in many frames.
      7. 
    
    

    
    TCP, UDP and ports
    
    
    IP delivers a packet to a machine, but a machine runs many programs at once: a web server, a streaming service, a time client. The port number (0 to 65,535) selects the program. The pair of IP address and port, such as 192.168.1.20:554, is a socket. The server listens on a well-known port; the client picks a random high port for its side.
    
    
    Two transport protocols sit on top of IP, with opposite philosophies:
    
    
    
      
    TCP: registered post
    Connection-oriented. A three-way handshake (SYN, SYN+ACK, ACK) sets up the connection. Every byte is numbered and acknowledged, lost segments are sent again, and data arrives in order. Cost: handshake delay, 20-byte header, and a stall when something is lost. Used for web pages, file transfer, Modbus TCP, MQTT, and the control side of video streaming.
    
      
    UDP: a postcard
    Connectionless. Just send. No handshake, no acknowledgements, no resending, no ordering. Cost: nothing is guaranteed. Benefit: very low delay, an 8-byte header, and it works with broadcast and multicast. Used for DHCP, DNS, NTP, BACnet/IP, and the live video itself in most cameras.

    Port numbers are conventions, not laws. A vendor can move a service to any port, and some camera brands use extra vendor-specific ports for their own software. The device's network menu and manual are the authority. A firewall rule is simply "allow this source to that destination on this protocol and port", for example "allow the NVR to the camera VLAN on TCP 554".
    
    
    Ping is neither TCP nor UDP. It uses ICMP, which has no ports. A device that answers ping has a working layer 1 to 3 path, but nothing yet is proved about its web page or video. "Ping works, the page does not" points to layers 4 to 7: a wrong port, a blocked port, a stopped service, or a missing gateway for the reply.
    
    
    On the job
    BACnet/IP finds devices with a Who-Is broadcast on UDP 47808. Routers do not forward broadcasts, so a BMS front end on one subnet will not see controllers on another. The fix is a BBMD (BACnet Broadcast Management Device) on each subnet, which forwards the broadcasts to its peers, or "foreign device" registration. Modbus TCP has no such problem because the client connects straight to a known IP on port 502. See C02.
    

    
    Unicast, broadcast, multicast and why camera networks flood
    
    
    Data can be addressed to one, to many or to all:
    
    
      
      
    • Unicast: one sender, one receiver. Almost everything: web pages, Modbus, a camera sending its stream to an NVR.
      • 
      
    • Broadcast: one sender, every device in the broadcast domain (ARP, DHCP discover, BACnet Who-Is).
      • 
      
    • Multicast: one sender, any number of receivers that asked to receive. The sender transmits one copy to a group address from 224.0.0.0 to 239.255.255.255. IPv4 multicast groups map to MAC addresses starting 01:00:5E. For example 239.1.1.1 becomes 01:00:5E:01:01:01: the low 23 bits of the IP address are copied into the MAC.
      • 
    
    
    
    Multicast is very efficient when many viewers watch the same stream: the camera or server sends 4 Mbps once, instead of 4 Mbps to each of ten viewers (40 Mbps). It is used by some video management systems, video walls and IPTV. But there is a catch in how a switch works. It learns MACs from the source field of frames. A multicast group MAC is only ever a destination; it is never a source. So the switch has nothing in its table for it, and by default it does what it always does with an unknown destination: it floods it out of every port.
    
    
    That is how a single multicast camera stream can bring a network down. Numbers: 32 cameras each sending 4 Mbps of multicast video, with no IGMP snooping on the switches. Every port in the VLAN receives all 32 × 4 = 128 Mbps. A camera or PC on a 100 Mbps port is asked to take 128 Mbps, which is more than the port can carry. The port saturates, the device drops out or reboots, and a Wi-Fi access point on the VLAN is worse because multicast on Wi-Fi is sent at a very low, basic data rate and eats up airtime.
    
    
    The cure is IGMP snooping. IGMP (Internet Group Management Protocol) is how a receiver says "I want to join group 239.1.1.1". A switch with snooping switched on listens to those messages and builds a table: group → ports that asked. It then sends the stream only to those ports. Two conditions have to be met:
    
    
      
      
    • Snooping must be on in every switch between source and receivers, in that VLAN.
      • 
      
    • Something must act as the IGMP querier (usually the router or one switch) that asks about every 125 seconds "who still wants what?". If there is no querier, memberships time out after a few minutes and the stream stops, a puzzling fault that appears minutes after you set things up.
      • 
    
    

    
    
    Common misconception
    Wrong: "IP cameras always flood the network, so camera systems need special switches." Right: by default most IP cameras send unicast to the NVR, which is fine on any switch. The flooding problems come from (1) multicast streams without IGMP snooping, (2) a loop causing a broadcast storm, or (3) simply too many streams meeting at one link, covered next.
    

    
    Bandwidth arithmetic for a CCTV system
    
    
    Network speeds are quoted in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps). File sizes and disks use bytes (MB, TB). One byte is 8 bits, so 8 Mbps is 1 MB per second. This is the source of many "my 100 Mbps link only moves 12 MB/s" complaints, and it is not a fault.
    
    
    Take 32 cameras, each streaming 4 Mbps (a typical figure for a 4 MP H.264 camera; the real number depends on codec, frame rate, scene and CBR or VBR, so always use the vendor's bitrate tool):
    
    
    Network load into the NVR = 32 × 4 Mbps = 128 Mbps
      Add about 5 to 10 % for Ethernet, IP and RTP headers: roughly 136 Mbps on the wire. A 100 Mbps link cannot carry that (and in practice delivers only about 94 Mbps of payload). A 1 Gbps link, which delivers about 940 Mbps, carries it at roughly 14 % load.
    
    
    Storage per camera per day = 4 Mbps × 86,400 s ÷ 8 = 43,200 MB = 43.2 GB
      32 cameras: 43.2 × 32 = 1,382 GB, about 1.38 TB per day. Keep 30 days: about 41.5 TB, then add 10 % margin and room for RAID: roughly 46 TB of raw disk. These are decimal terabytes, as disk makers count them.

    PoE budget basics
    
    
    PoE (Power over Ethernet) sends DC power to a device along the same cable pairs as the data, at about 50 V DC, which is still extra-low voltage. The switch (or a mid-span injector) is the PSE (power sourcing equipment) and the camera or access point is the PD (powered device). They agree on a power class when the cable is plugged in, so the PD can be safely powered without you setting anything.
    
    
    
      
    StandardCommon namePower at the switch portGuaranteed at the deviceTypical use
    
      
    IEEE 802.3af (Type 1)PoE15.4 W12.95 WFixed cameras, phones, readers
    
      
    IEEE 802.3at (Type 2)PoE+30 W25.5 WIR cameras, small PTZs, Wi-Fi access points
    
      
    IEEE 802.3bt (Type 3)PoE++60 W51 WLarge PTZs, multi-sensor cameras
    
      
    IEEE 802.3bt (Type 4)PoE++90 Wabout 71 WHeated housings, high-power devices
    
    
    
    
    The difference between the two power columns is heat lost in the cable (I²R, see T03), worst case over 100 m. Two budgets matter, and the second one catches people out:
    
    
      
      
    • Per-port limit: the most one port can supply (15.4 W, 30 W …).
      • 
      
    • Switch total PoE budget: the whole switch's power supply for PoE, for example 370 W. Many switches reserve power by the device's class, not by what it actually draws. A camera that draws 7 W but reports class 3 may still reserve 15.4 W of the budget.
      • 
    
    
    
    Worked example. A switch has a 370 W PoE budget. You connect 12 fixed cameras (class 3, 15.4 W reserved each) and 4 IR/PTZ cameras (class 4, 30 W each):
    
    
    12 × 15.4 W + 4 × 30 W = 184.8 + 120 = 304.8 W  →  370 − 304.8 = 65.2 W spare (17.6 %)
      It fits, but with under 20 % spare. Plan on the worst case, not the average: IR illuminators and heaters draw most at night and in winter, exactly when you least want a camera to reboot. Choose a bigger switch or spread the cameras across two.
    
    
    Safety
    Standard PoE is negotiated, so it is safe to plug a non-PoE laptop into a PoE port: the port stays unpowered until it detects a valid powered device. But "passive PoE" injectors and cheap kit that send fixed 12, 24 or 48 V on the spare pairs without negotiating can damage ordinary Ethernet ports. Never connect the output of a passive injector to a PC, a switch, or any device that is not labelled as designed for that supply. All of this stays at extra-low voltage; never connect mains equipment to any of it.

    IPv6 in one page
    
    
    IPv4 has only about 4.3 billion addresses, and they ran out. IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses, written as eight groups of four hexadecimal digits, with leading zeros dropped and one run of zero groups shortened to ::. For example 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:0000:0000:0001 is written 2001:db8::1 (the 2001:db8::/32 block is reserved for documentation).
    
    
      
      
    • A normal LAN subnet is a /64: the first 64 bits are the network, the last 64 identify the device. Nobody counts hosts.
      • 
      
    • No ARP and no broadcast. IPv6 uses neighbour discovery over multicast instead.
      • 
      
    • Every interface has a link-local address starting fe80::, always present on its own network.
      • 
      
    • Devices can build their own address from the router's advertised prefix (SLAAC), or get one by DHCPv6.
      • 
    
    
    
    Most building-services and security gear (cameras, BACnet/IP and Modbus TCP devices, access controllers) is still configured and documented in IPv4, so this course uses IPv4. You will meet IPv6 on routers, on new NBN and mobile services, and on modern operating systems, which run both at once ("dual stack").
    
    
    In Australia
    Many Australian internet providers offer IPv6 on NBN services. Whether it is switched on, and how it is delivered, differs by provider and by modem, so check what the client's service actually does before assuming. Cabling for data in Australia is also covered by registration and standards rules; see C07 for when a registered cabler is needed, and confirm the current rules with ACMA.

    Safety and permission
    Only plug into, scan or change a client's network with permission, and ask for the IP schedule and VLAN plan first. Port scanners and "find all devices" tools can disturb fragile building controllers, and unauthorised access to a computer network can be a criminal offence in Australia. On your own bench network you can practise freely.

    Check yourself
    
    
    A switch has just been powered on. Host A sends a frame to host C. Which ports receive it? What changes when C replies?
    The table is empty, so C is unknown and the frame is flooded out of every port except A's. Meanwhile the switch learns "A is on port 1" from the source MAC. When C replies, the switch learns "C is on port 3" from C's source MAC, and sends the reply to port 1 only, because A is now known. From then on A and C talk through ports 1 and 3 only, and the other ports no longer see their unicast traffic.
    
    
    Work out the network address, broadcast address and usable range for 10.20.30.141/27.
    /27 leaves 5 host bits: 32 addresses per subnet, mask 255.255.255.224. The block size is 256 − 224 = 32. Subnets in the last octet start at 0, 32, 64, 96, 128, 160 …. The value 141 sits in the block starting at 128. Network 10.20.30.128. The next network is 160, so the broadcast is 10.20.30.159. Usable range 10.20.30.129 to 10.20.30.158, which is 30 hosts.
    
    
    A laptop is 192.168.1.130/25 and a camera is 192.168.1.20/25. Both are in "192.168.1.x". Can they talk without a router?
    No. A /25 splits the 256 addresses into 0 to 127 and 128 to 255. The camera is in the lower half (network .0/25) and the laptop in the upper half (.128/25), so they are on different subnets. Each would send traffic for the other to its gateway. Fix: set both to the same mask (/24 if the site is a /24), or put them in the same half.
    
    
    32 cameras stream 4 Mbps each. What is the network load on the NVR, can a 100 Mbps NVR port take it, and roughly how much storage does one day need?
    32 × 4 = 128 Mbps (about 136 Mbps on the wire). A 100 Mbps port cannot carry that, and even less so in real life, where it delivers about 94 Mbps. A 1 Gbps port carries it at about 14 % load. Storage: 128 Mbps × 86,400 s ÷ 8 = 1,382,400 MB, about 1.38 TB per day.
    
    
    A BMS front end on 192.168.10.0/24 cannot find BACnet/IP controllers on 192.168.20.0/24, but it can poll a Modbus TCP meter there by its IP. Why?
    BACnet/IP discovery (Who-Is) is a broadcast on UDP 47808, and routers do not forward broadcasts. The front end therefore never reaches the other subnet's controllers. Fix it with a BBMD on each subnet (or foreign-device registration). Modbus TCP is a unicast TCP connection to port 502 on a known IP address, which a router forwards without trouble.
    
    
    Everything on a camera VLAN slows to a crawl whenever someone opens the multicast video wall. What do you check?
    Multicast without IGMP snooping. The switches flood the stream to every port, so every camera, PC and access point in the VLAN receives all of it. Turn on IGMP snooping in every switch in that VLAN, and make sure an IGMP querier exists (usually the router), otherwise memberships time out and the stream stops after a few minutes.
    
    
    Match the services to ports and transports: Modbus TCP, RTSP, BACnet/IP. A camera answers ping but its web page will not load. What does that tell you?
    Modbus TCP is port 502 over TCP. RTSP is port 554, mainly over TCP, with the video itself usually in RTP over UDP. BACnet/IP is port 47808 over UDP. Ping uses ICMP, so it proves layers 1 to 3 only. The web page needs TCP 80 or 443, so look at layer 4 and above: the port, a firewall rule, a stopped web service, or the camera's missing gateway for the reply.
    
    
    A switch with a 150 W PoE budget feeds 6 class 3 cameras (15.4 W each) and 2 class 4 PoE+ cameras (30 W each). Does it work?
    6 × 15.4 = 92.4 W, plus 2 × 30 = 60 W, total 152.4 W. That is 2.4 W over the 150 W budget, so the switch will refuse power to the last port or ports that came up (typically the lowest priority). Choose a switch with a bigger budget, or move cameras to a second switch. Even at 149 W you would have no margin.
    
    
    Go deeper (optional): /31 and /32, and why 2ⁿ − 2
    A /30 gives 4 addresses and 2 usable hosts, which is perfect for a link between two routers. A /31 has only 2 addresses; because a point-to-point link has no use for network or broadcast addresses, RFC 3021 lets both be used as hosts. A /32 is a single address: it identifies one device and appears in routing tables as a "host route", and as the loopback address of a router. The subtract-two rule applies to ordinary multi-host subnets only. The calculator above follows these rules.
