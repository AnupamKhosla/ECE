Sensor physics: how sensors turn the world into signals

Every number on a BMS screen started as a physical effect in a tiny piece of material: a resistance that changed with heat, a voltage made by two metals, a charge released by a warm body, a plate that bent. This chapter explains the physics of the sensors you will meet in buildings and security systems, gives you the numbers to check them with a meter, and shows how each one ends up as 0–10 V, 4–20 mA, a contact or a digital message at the controller.

From the world to a signal: the sensor chain

A sensor is a part whose electrical behaviour changes in a known way when something in the world changes. A transducer is the general word for any part that converts one kind of energy into another. In a building, almost every sensor follows the same chain of five steps.

Three questions help you with any new sensor. What physical effect does it use? What does it output when nothing is happening? What does a broken wire look like? The third question matters most for fault-finding, so we will answer it for every sensor.

Temperature: why resistance changes with heat

Temperature sensors are the most common sensors in buildings: room, supply air, return air, outside air, chilled water, hot water. Three of the four common types simply change their resistance or voltage with temperature. Chapter T02 explained resistance as electrons colliding with a vibrating lattice. Now heat changes that picture in two opposite ways.

Metals: resistance rises (PTC)

Hot atoms vibrate harder, so the free electrons collide more often. A metal has a huge, fixed number of free electrons, so more collisions simply means more resistance. Platinum rises about 0.39 % per °C. This is the RTD (resistance temperature detector).

Semiconductors: resistance falls (NTC)

In a metal-oxide ceramic few electrons are free at low temperature. Heat shakes more of them loose, so there are many more carriers to conduct. The extra carriers win by far over the extra collisions. This is the NTC thermistor (negative temperature coefficient).

Two metals: a voltage (Seebeck)

Join two different metals and keep their ends at different temperatures. A small voltage appears with no battery at all. This is the thermocouple, and it needs no power supply.

NTC thermistors and the Beta equation

A thermistor (thermal resistor) is a small bead or disc of metal-oxide ceramic. The ordinary BMS room and duct sensor is a 10 kΩ NTC: it has 10,000 Ω at 25 °C. Its resistance falls by a large percentage for every degree, which makes it very sensitive but curved.

Why does the curve look the way it does? To free a carrier, the ceramic needs a certain energy. The fraction of carriers that get that energy from heat grows like e−E/kT, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T is the temperature in kelvin. More carriers means lower resistance, so the resistance follows the same exponential in reverse. Engineers wrap this into one number from the datasheet, the Beta value (B), measured in kelvin.

R(T) = R0 · exp[ B · (1/T − 1/T0) ] (T and T0 in kelvin = °C + 273.15) R0 is the resistance at the reference temperature T0 = 298.15 K (25 °C). For a "10 kΩ, Beta 3950" part, R0 = 10,000 Ω and B = 3950 K. B is really an energy: B × k is the activation energy, here 3950 × 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K ≈ 0.34 eV.

How sensitive is it? Differentiate the formula and you get dR/dT = −B·R/T². At 25 °C that is −3950 × 10,000 / 298.15² ≈ −444 Ω per °C, or −4.4 % of the resistance for each degree. A platinum sensor changes by 0.39 % per °C. The NTC is about ten times more sensitive, which is why a cheap ADC can read it well. The price is the curve: you need the equation (or a lookup table) to convert, and the Beta model is only good to a fraction of a degree over a modest range around 25 °C. For wider ranges, controllers use the more accurate three-term Steinhart–Hart equation or the maker's table.

Reading it with a divider

The ADC reads voltage, so the NTC is put in a voltage divider with a fixed 10 kΩ resistor (T04, and the lab P12). With the NTC to ground and 5 V across the pair:

Vout = 5 V × RNTC / (RNTC + 10 kΩ) → 25 °C: 2.500 V, 50 °C: 1.320 V, 0 °C: 3.854 V Near 25 °C the output changes by about −56 mV per °C. A 10-bit Arduino step is 4.88 mV (T16), so one step is only 4.88 / 56 ≈ 0.09 °C. That is why a 10 kΩ NTC and a 10 kΩ resistor make such a good pair: the sensitivity is greatest when both resistances are equal.
Self-heating

Current through the NTC warms it slightly: the divider above puts 0.25 mA through 10 kΩ, which is P = I²R ≈ 0.6 mW. Datasheets give a dissipation constant, typically of the order of 1–2 mW per °C in still air, so the sensor reads a fraction of a degree high. Good controllers keep the current small for this reason. Do not "improve" a reading by swapping in a smaller fixed resistor.

What a broken wire looks like

An open circuit makes the NTC's resistance look infinite, so the controller reads very cold (often −40 °C or the bottom of the range). A short circuit reads very hot. A temperature stuck at an extreme value is almost always a wiring fault, not the weather. BMS controllers raise a "sensor fault" alarm for it.

RTDs: PT100 and PT1000

An RTD is a thin film or fine wire of platinum on a ceramic base. Platinum is chosen because it does not corrode, it can be made very pure, and its resistance rises in a smooth, repeatable way. The name tells you the resistance at 0 °C: PT100 = 100 Ω, PT1000 = 1000 Ω. The international standard (IEC 60751) fixes the curve, so a PT100 from any maker should agree with another. That interchangeability is why RTDs are the reference in industry.

R(T) = R0 · (1 + A·T + B·T²) for T ≥ 0 °C, A = 3.9083×10⁻³ /°C, B = −5.775×10⁻⁷ /°C² This is the Callendar–Van Dusen equation (IEC 60751). Rule of thumb for a PT100: about 0.385 Ω per °C. A PT1000 is exactly ten times larger: 3.85 Ω per °C. At 0 °C, PT100 = 100.00 Ω. At 100 °C it is 100 × (1 + 0.39083 − 0.005775) = 138.51 Ω.

An RTD must be measured by passing a small known current through it and reading the voltage. That current heats it, so it is kept to about 1 mA or less for a PT100 (power I²R = 0.1 mW). A PT1000 is read with a smaller current for the same signal, which is another reason it is popular.

In Australia

BMS and HVAC controllers in Australian buildings take many sensor types, and the controller's input must be configured to match the sensor. PT1000 and 10 kΩ NTC are very common for room, duct and pipe sensors. Older sites may have nickel-based 1000 Ω sensors. Always check the controller's list of supported input types and the sensor's datasheet before you wire anything.

Lead resistance: 2-, 3- and 4-wire

The sensor may sit 30 m from the controller. The copper cable has resistance too, and the controller cannot tell cable resistance from sensor resistance. This is the lead-resistance problem. A 0.5 mm² copper conductor has about 0.0344 Ω per metre (T16), so 30 m of cable has 1.03 Ω in each conductor.

Worked example

A PT100 sits 30 m from the controller on 0.5 mm² cable and is connected by two wires. Lead resistance is 2 × 1.03 = 2.06 Ω. The controller thinks the extra 2.06 Ω is sensor resistance and reads high by 2.06 / 0.385 = 5.4 °C. The same cable with a PT1000 adds the same 2.06 Ω, but the PT1000 changes by 3.85 Ω per °C, so the error is only 2.06 / 3.85 = 0.5 °C. With a 10 kΩ NTC changing 444 Ω per °C at 25 °C, the same 2 Ω is only 0.005 °C and can be ignored. This is the main reason HVAC uses PT1000 and NTC sensors on long runs, and PT100 with 3 or 4 wires in industry.

What a broken wire looks like

An open RTD circuit gives a huge resistance, so the controller reads very hot (the opposite of an NTC). A short gives about 0 Ω, which reads far below −100 °C. In a 3-wire RTD, if the sense wire breaks the controller can no longer subtract lead resistance and the reading jumps by a few degrees: a puzzling fault that looks like a sensor drifting.

Thermocouples: the Seebeck effect

Heat a metal rod at one end. The electrons at the hot end have more energy and spread out more, so some diffuse towards the cold end and build up there. The result is a small voltage along the rod. This is the Seebeck effect. How large the voltage is depends on the metal. If you use two different metals and join them at the hot end, the voltages along the two wires differ. Compare them at the cold ends and you get a net voltage that depends on the temperature difference between the hot end and the cold end.

V ≈ S × (Thot − Tcold), Type K: S ≈ 41 µV/°C Type K is chromel (nickel-chromium) against alumel (nickel-aluminium). It is the standard general-purpose thermocouple and works from below −100 °C to roughly 1,200 °C in normal use. A table value: 100 °C above a 0 °C reference gives 4.096 mV (40.96 µV/°C on average); 1000 °C gives 41.276 mV. The slope is close to, but not exactly, constant, so the 41 µV/°C rule is approximate.

The voltage is tiny. At 100 °C it is about 4 mV, and one degree is 41 millionths of a volt. This brings three practical consequences.

  1. It needs an amplifier or a transmitter near the sensor. A thermocouple-input card or a head-mounted transmitter turns the microvolts into 4–20 mA (T16). The T16 noise example showed that a signal this small is easily swamped by hum, so use twisted, shielded pairs and earth the shield at one end.
  2. You must use the right wire all the way. Every joint between two different metals makes its own thermocouple. Copper to chromel in a terminal is a junction. So the thermocouple (or matching extension cable) must run unbroken to the controller's terminals, with matching plugs.
  3. You must know the cold-junction temperature, as explained next.

Cold-junction compensation

The voltage depends on both temperatures. The controller is not at 0 °C: its terminal block might be at 25 °C. The "cold junction" is wherever the thermocouple wires meet the controller's copper. If the controller only looked at the voltage, a warm panel would make the process look colder than it is. So the controller has a small thermistor or sensor chip at its terminals, measures the terminal temperature, calculates the voltage that temperature would make, and adds it back. This is cold-junction compensation (CJC).

Worked example

A type K probe in a flue is connected to a transmitter whose terminals are at 25 °C. The transmitter measures 10.0 mV across its input. At 25 °C the cold junction accounts for 41 µV × 25 = 1.03 mV. The full voltage from 0 °C is 10.0 + 1.03 = 11.03 mV, so Thot ≈ 11.03 / 0.041 ≈ 269 °C by the linear rule. A proper table gives about 271 °C, so the shortcut is good here. Without compensation the transmitter would have reported 10.0 / 0.041 = 244 °C, which is 25 °C too low.

Common misconception

Wrong: "The voltage is made at the junction where the two metals touch." Right: the voltage develops along the wires, wherever they pass through a temperature gradient. The junction only joins the two wires so that the difference can be read at the other end. This is why a thermocouple measures the temperature at the hot junction only if the wire is uniform, and why damaged or mixed wire gives errors even when the tip is fine.

Bench tip

Plugs and sheaths of type K are not the same colour in all countries. The American code makes the red wire negative, which surprises people used to red meaning positive. The international (IEC) code differs again. Check the type marked on the probe and the datasheet instead of relying on colours.

What a broken wire looks like

A thermocouple input has almost no current flowing, so a break gives no sign by itself. Good inputs inject a tiny current to detect it and drive the reading to the top or bottom of the range (called burnout). A standard-grade type K is typically only good to about ±2.5 °C or 0.75 % of the reading (indicative; see the datasheet), so for ±0.5 °C accuracy near room temperature an RTD is the better choice. Thermocouples earn their place at high temperatures and fast response.

Motion: the PIR sensor

PIR means passive infrared: "passive" because it sends nothing out, it only listens for the infrared that warm objects already emit. Every object gives off infrared radiation, and a human body at about 34 °C skin temperature has its peak near λ = 2898 µm·K / 307 K ≈ 9.4 µm (Wien's law), which is in the far infrared (the 8–14 µm band). A PIR is built to see exactly that band.

The pyroelectric crystal

The heart of a PIR is a thin slice of pyroelectric material, such as lithium tantalate. In such a crystal, the atoms are slightly offset in a way that makes one face positive and the other negative. The size of this offset (the polarisation) changes with temperature. When infrared lands on the crystal and warms it, its polarisation changes, and charge flows onto the electrodes: a tiny current, for a moment. Then the crystal reaches a steady temperature, the polarisation stops changing, and the current stops. A pyroelectric crystal responds to a change in temperature, not to temperature itself. A small transistor built into the sensor turns the very high-impedance charge into a usable voltage.

Two elements and a Fresnel lens

  • Two elements wired in opposite polarity. If the whole room warms (sunlight, a heater switching on), both elements warm together and their signals cancel. If a warm person crosses from the view of element A to the view of element B, the output swings positive, then negative. The electronics look for those paired pulses.
  • A Fresnel lens (a moulded plastic lens with many small facets) divides the room into many narrow zones and focuses each one onto the elements. Ordinary glass blocks far infrared, but the thin polyethylene plastic of the lens passes it. A person moving from one zone into the next causes the repeated pulses.
Common misconception

Wrong: "A PIR detects a warm body, so it will always see a person in the room." Right: it detects a change in the infrared pattern across its zones. A person sitting still at a desk can make the lights switch off. Moving across the zones triggers it much more strongly than walking directly towards it. It also does not see through glass. In very hot weather, when the background is close to body temperature, sensitivity drops.

A security PIR is usually a relay contact (normally closed, with a tamper switch) in an alarm zone (C06). A BMS occupancy sensor is usually a dry contact into a digital input, with a time delay in the controller so the lights stay on for 10–15 minutes after the last movement. Dual-technology sensors add a second method (microwave or ultrasonic) so that both must agree, which cuts false alarms from heaters and sunlight. Typical false triggers are hot-air vents, curtains moving in the draught of an air-con outlet, and sun through a window falling on a warm wall.

Magnetic sensors: the Hall effect and reed switches

The Hall effect

Chapter T06 showed that a magnetic field pushes sideways on moving charge: the force on one charge q moving at speed v is F = qvB. Now run a current along a flat strip of conductor and put a magnetic field through the strip. The moving charges are pushed to one side, so one edge becomes slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Charge piles up until the sideways electric force balances the magnetic push. The voltage across the strip is the Hall voltage.

VH = I · B / (n · q · t) I = current along the strip, B = flux density through it (tesla), n = number of free charges per cubic metre, q = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, t = strip thickness. Worked: silicon-type material n = 10²² /m³, t = 0.1 mm, I = 5 mA, B = 50 mT gives VH = 0.005 × 0.05 / (10²² × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 1.6 mV. The same strip in copper (n = 8.5×10²⁸) gives about 0.0000000002 V. That is why Hall sensors use semiconductors: with few free charges, each one is pushed hard.

A silicon Hall element is built into a chip with an amplifier. The chip comes in two forms.

  • Linear (analogue) Hall sensor. Output is half the supply voltage with no field, and swings up or down in proportion to the field (a few millivolts per millitesla, depending on the part). It is ratiometric: it scales with the supply, which is fine if the ADC uses the same supply as its reference.
  • Hall switch. The chip has a comparator inside and gives a digital on/off when the field passes a threshold. Many switches respond to one magnetic pole only, so the magnet must be turned the right way round. Some are latching and need the opposite pole to switch back.

Current sensors. A current makes a field (Ampère, T06). Thread a conductor through a ring of magnetic material with a small gap, place a Hall chip in the gap, and the chip's output is proportional to the current, with electrical isolation from the measured conductor. Unlike a current transformer (T08), it works down to DC. Variable speed drives (T19), solar inverters and battery systems all use Hall current sensors. A DC clamp meter is a Hall device; a plain AC clamp meter is a current transformer. Illustrative numbers: a sensor with output 2.5 V at 0 A and 40 mV per amp reads 2.9 V at 10 A and 2.1 V at −10 A.

Position sensing. A small magnet on a door, shutter, damper blade or rotor, and a Hall chip fixed nearby, give a contactless position sensor with no mechanical wear. Brushless motor fans (T19) use three Hall sensors to know where the rotor is.

Reed switches

A reed switch is two thin strips of magnetic metal (the reeds) sealed in a glass tube filled with inert gas. Their tips overlap with a small gap. Bring a magnet close and both reeds become magnets: the tip of one becomes a north pole and the overlapping tip of the other a south pole. Opposite poles attract, so the tips touch and the switch closes. Take the magnet away and the springy reeds open again. The magnet does the switching directly; no power is needed at the switch.

On the job · security door contacts

A door contact in an intruder-alarm system is a reed switch in the frame and a magnet in the door. Typical gap rating is around 15–25 mm for surface contacts (wide-gap versions are larger; see the datasheet). It is wired in an alarm zone with an end-of-line (EOL) resistor so the panel can tell the difference between closed, open and cut or shorted cable (C06). A big magnet held against the frame can fool a plain reed contact. High-security sites use balanced (biased) magnetic switches with two reeds and a bias magnet, so a second magnet cannot hold the contact shut. In security these are abbreviated "BMS", which is a completely different BMS from the building management system.

On the job · fan and pump status

BMS jobs often use a current switch: a split-core clamp that goes around one motor cable and closes a contact when current flows (some use a Hall element or a current transformer). It proves that the motor is really drawing current, which is a better "running" signal than the start command. An electrician installs it in the switchboard; you wire the dry contact into a digital input.

Safety

Clamping a meter or sensor around a conductor means being inside a switchboard or motor terminal box. That is mains or three-phase. In Australia only a licensed electrician may open it and work on it. This course keeps you at extra-low voltage: practise on batteries and 12 V or 24 V DC circuits only.

Pressure and differential pressure

Pressure is force per area. The SI unit is the pascal (Pa): 1 newton on 1 square metre. A pascal is small, so HVAC air pressure is quoted in pascals (a duct might sit at 250 Pa) and water pressure in kilopascals or bar (1 bar = 100 kPa). Three kinds exist: gauge (compared with the air around), absolute (compared with a vacuum) and differential (the difference between two ports).

Strain gauge and the bridge

Most pressure sensors use the piezoresistive effect: when a conductor is stretched, its resistance changes. A stretched wire is longer and thinner (T02: R = ρL/A), and in a semiconductor the stretch also changes the crystal's resistivity. The sensitivity is the gauge factor, GF = (ΔR/R) / strain. Metal foil gauges have GF of about 2. Silicon gauges are far more sensitive, with GF of the order of 100.

Four gauges are mounted on a thin diaphragm (a flexible plate) and connected as a Wheatstone bridge. When pressure bends the plate, two gauges stretch and two compress, so two resistances go up and two go down. A bridge turns that small difference into a voltage and cancels the large common effects, such as temperature drift that moves all four gauges together.

Full bridge, all four gauges active: Vout = Vexc × (ΔR / R) Worked: Vexc = 5 V and ΔR/R = 1 % at full scale gives Vout = 50 mV. That is 10 mV per volt of excitation, which is about the size of a real pressure sensor chip. An amplifier inside the transmitter lifts 50 mV to 5 V or 10 V, or to 20 mA (T16).

Differential pressure in HVAC

A differential pressure (DP) sensor has two ports and measures P1 − P2. It is everywhere in air handling.

  • Filter condition. Air passing through a filter loses pressure. A clean filter might show about 80 Pa and a dirty one 250 Pa or more (indicative; the filter maker states the final pressure at which to change it). The BMS raises a "filter dirty" alarm above a setpoint.
  • Fan proof. A DP sensor across a fan reads high when air is moving and near zero when a belt breaks. It proves airflow, which the VSD's "run" signal does not (T19).
  • Duct static pressure. A VAV system keeps pressure in the supply duct at a setpoint (often a few hundred pascals; the designer sets it). The BMS varies the fan speed with a PID loop to hold it (C03).
  • Airflow. A pitot tube or flow station gives DP proportional to air speed squared: v = √(2·ΔP/ρ). With ΔP = 100 Pa and air density ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, v = √(166.7) = 12.9 m/s. A DP sensor is therefore often an airflow sensor in disguise, and the square root means the sensor is least accurate at low flow.
  • Water. A DP sensor across a chilled-water or hot-water circuit tells the BMS how hard to run the pump (T19).
What a broken tube looks like

DP sensors use two plastic tubes. A kinked, blocked or pulled-off tube gives a reading stuck near zero or at the last value. Tubes swapped between the high and low ports give a negative reading. A transmitter that reads 4.0 mA is at 0 Pa; if the loop reads 3.5 mA, the wiring or sensor is faulty (T16). Never blow into a sensor port to "test" it: you can exceed its pressure limit and destroy the diaphragm.

Humidity: the capacitive sensor

Air always contains some water vapour. Relative humidity (RH) says how much, as a percentage of the most the air could hold at the same temperature. Warm air can hold much more water than cold air. So RH changes when the temperature changes even if no water is added.

Common misconception

Wrong: "50 % RH is a fixed amount of water." Right: 50 % RH at 30 °C holds much more water than 50 % RH at 10 °C. The dew point is the temperature at which the air would be 100 % RH, and condensation forms on any surface colder than that. A handy rule for RH above 50 %: dew point ≈ T − (100 − RH) / 5. At 25 °C and 60 % RH, dew point ≈ 25 − 8 = 17 °C. A chilled-water pipe at 7 °C will sweat, and a 14 °C supply duct without insulation is a mould risk.

A capacitive humidity sensor is a tiny capacitor (T05). Its two electrodes are separated by a thin polymer film that absorbs water vapour from the air. Water is unusual: its relative permittivity (εr ≈ 80) is far higher than that of the dry polymer (around 3). The capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor is C = ε0εrA/d, so as the film absorbs water, εr rises and the capacitance rises. Typical sensors have a few hundred picofarads, changing by a fraction of a picofarad per 1 % RH. A small chip measures that capacitance (an oscillator whose frequency depends on C is one way) and outputs voltage, current or a digital message.

  • Because RH depends on temperature, humidity sensors always include a temperature sensor, and most output both.
  • The film is exposed to the air, so dust, condensation, cleaning chemicals and drift all affect it. Typical accuracy is ±2–3 % RH (indicative), and the sensor is checked or replaced on a schedule.
  • Place it where the air is representative: not in direct sun, not above a heater, not at an exhaust grille.
  • Comfort and building guidelines are often 40–60 % RH for offices (indicative; the designer's specification governs).

CO₂: how NDIR counts molecules

Fresh outdoor air has about 420–430 ppm of carbon dioxide (ppm = parts per million). People breathe out about 40,000 ppm (4 %). In a crowded, poorly ventilated room the CO₂ level rises, and the BMS reads it as a proxy for how many people are inside and how much fresh air they are getting. Around 1,000 ppm is a common benchmark in building guidance; the designer's specification sets the trigger. CO₂ at these levels is not toxic. It is an indicator of stale air.

The sensor type is NDIR: non-dispersive infrared. A CO₂ molecule is three atoms in a line, and it can vibrate in ways that absorb infrared of one specific wavelength, 4.26 µm. Shine light through the air, filter it to that wavelength, and measure how much arrives. The more CO₂, the more light is absorbed. This is the Beer–Lambert law:

I = I0 · exp(−k · c · L) I0 = light entering the tube, I = light reaching the detector, c = CO₂ concentration, L = length of the gas path, k = absorption constant for CO₂ at 4.26 µm. A longer tube gives a bigger effect, which is why NDIR cells have mirrors that fold a long path inside a small housing. At room levels the change in light is small, so a drift in the lamp can look like a change in gas.
Field trap · ABC and fake CO₂

Many NDIR sensors have ABC (automatic baseline correction): once a week they assume the lowest reading they saw was fresh air (about 420 ppm) and reset their zero to it. That works in an office that is empty at night. In a space that is occupied 24 hours (a ward, a control room, a data hall with people), the sensor "learns" a wrong zero and reads low. Turn ABC off in those spaces and calibrate against outside air instead. Cheap sensors that report "eCO₂" estimate CO₂ from a VOC (volatile organic compounds) chip. They do not measure CO₂ at all: they respond to breath, deodorant and cleaning products. They are not suitable for ventilation control.

In Australia

Mechanical ventilation of buildings is covered by AS 1668.2 and the National Construction Code. CO₂-based demand-controlled ventilation lets the BMS bring in less outside air when a room is quiet, saving energy. The CO₂ trigger values come from the designer's specification, not from this chapter. Confirm the current standard and the project specification.

For that reason fire services and regulators in Australia have been moving household alarms to photoelectric. Queensland has the strictest rules, which have been phased in since 2017: photoelectric and interconnected alarms are required in homes. Other states and territories have different rules that are being tightened over time. Commercial buildings use addressable detectors on a fire panel (C04): optical, heat, or combined (multi-criteria) heads under AS 1670.1.

In Australia

Household smoke alarms are covered by AS 3786 and the National Construction Code, and the state and territory regulations decide where they must go and which type is required. Confirm the current state rule with your state's fire service or building regulator before telling a customer anything: dates and details differ by state and have changed in recent years. Fire detection systems in commercial buildings (AS 1670.1) are specialist work with their own licensing and training (C04).

Safety

Many household smoke alarms are hard-wired to 230 V mains. Replacing or wiring them is electrical work that only a licensed electrician may do in Australia. The BMS does not replace the fire panel: for life safety, the fire system works on its own, and the BMS only receives a volt-free contact from it. Never disable or bypass a detector to stop nuisance alarms.

Worked scaling examples

  • CO₂, 0–10 V for 0–2000 ppm. The controller reads 3.4 V. Fraction of span = 3.4 / 10 = 34 %, so the reading is 0.34 × 2000 = 680 ppm.
  • DP, 4–20 mA for 0–500 Pa. The loop current is 8 mA. Fraction of span = (8 − 4) / 16 = 25 %, so the pressure is 125 Pa (T16).
  • Duct sensor, NTC in a divider. The input measures 1.32 V from the 5 V divider. Rearranging V = 5R/(R + 10k) gives R = 10k × V/(5 − V) = 10k × 1.32 / 3.68 = 3.59 kΩ, which is 50 °C on the table above.

Universal inputs and configuration

Most modern BMS controllers have universal inputs (UI) that can be configured in software and by jumpers to read a voltage, a current, a resistance or a contact. This is flexible and also a common source of mistakes. A 4–20 mA sensor on an input set to 0–10 V reads nonsense; an input set to 0–10 V can read 0 when the loop is open. Always match the input type to the sensor, then confirm the reading with a meter at the terminals.

Digital sensors skip the analogue steps and send numbers over a serial bus: RS-485 with Modbus RTU or BACnet MS/TP (P14, C02). The advantage is that one pair of wires carries many values, and there is no scaling error or lead resistance. The price is that each device needs an address and the right settings.

Check yourself

A 10 kΩ Beta 3950 NTC sits at 50 °C. What is its resistance, and what will a 10 kΩ / 5 V divider (NTC to ground) read?

T = 323.15 K, T0 = 298.15 K. 1/T − 1/T0 = 0.0030945 − 0.0033540 = −0.0002595. Multiply by B: −1.025. e−1.025 = 0.359, so R = 3.59 kΩ. Divider voltage = 5 × 3.59 / (3.59 + 10) = 1.32 V. The resistance fell to about a third while the temperature rose only 25 °C.

A PT100 is connected with two wires over 60 m of 0.5 mm² cable (60 m each way). What error will the controller show, and how would you fix it?

Each conductor: 0.0344 × 60 = 2.06 Ω. Both: 4.13 Ω. Error = 4.13 / 0.385 ≈ 10.7 °C too high. Fix by changing to a 3-wire or 4-wire connection, or by using a PT1000 (error 4.13 / 3.85 ≈ 1.1 °C), or by measuring the cable resistance and entering an offset. A 3-wire connection only works if the three leads have equal resistance.

A type K thermocouple input reads 6.15 mV. The controller's terminals are at 25 °C. What is the probe temperature (linear rule)?

The cold junction accounts for 41 µV × 25 = 1.03 mV. Total from 0 °C = 6.15 + 1.03 = 7.18 mV. T = 7.18 / 0.041 ≈ 175 °C. (A proper table gives about 176 °C.) Without cold-junction compensation the controller would show 6.15 / 0.041 = 150 °C, which is 25 °C wrong.

A PIR light sensor switches the lights off while a person is working at a desk. Why, and what are two fixes?

A PIR responds to a change in the infrared pattern across its zones, not to presence. A person sitting still gives a steady signal, so the sensor sees nothing happening and the timer runs out. Fixes: move or re-aim the sensor so the desk is in the zones that see small hand and body movements; lengthen the off-delay; or use a dual-technology sensor (PIR plus ultrasonic or microwave) that detects small movements.

You need to know whether a door is shut at a secure site, and also count wheel rotations on a flow meter. Which of reed switch and Hall switch would you choose for each, and why?

For a basic alarm door contact, a reed switch: it needs no power, gives a clean contact for the alarm zone with an EOL resistor, and is cheap. For high-security sites use a balanced (biased) magnetic switch, because a plain reed can be defeated with a second magnet. For counting wheel rotations at speed and for years, a Hall switch: it has no moving parts or contact wear and no bounce, whereas a reed's contacts wear out and bounce.

A 4–20 mA differential pressure transmitter has a range of 0–500 Pa. The loop current is 10.4 mA. What is the pressure? What would 3.2 mA tell you?

Fraction = (10.4 − 4) / 16 = 0.40, so the pressure is 0.40 × 500 = 200 Pa. 3.2 mA is below 3.8 mA, outside the valid range, so it is a fault (broken wire, transmitter fault or low supply), not a pressure. DP can never be less than 0 Pa on a transmitter ranged 0–500 Pa.

A CO₂ sensor in a 24-hour control room always reads about 420 ppm at midday. The room is full. What is probably wrong?

ABC (automatic baseline correction) is probably on. It assumes the lowest reading in the last few days was fresh air at about 420 ppm and resets the zero to match. In a room that is never empty, the lowest reading is not fresh air, so the sensor shifts its zero and reads low. Turn ABC off and calibrate against outdoor air. Another possibility is a cheap "eCO₂" sensor that does not measure CO₂ at all.

Why do fire services favour photoelectric over ionisation alarms in homes, and what must you do before quoting the state rule?

Photoelectric alarms see the larger particles from smouldering fires earlier, and the smouldering fire at night is the dangerous one. Ionisation alarms also trigger often on cooking, so people disable them. Rules differ by state and territory and have been changing (Queensland is the strictest). Confirm the current rule with the state fire service or building regulator before telling anyone it is required or sufficient.

Go deeper (optional): why the Beta equation is only approximate

The Beta model assumes the activation energy of carrier release is constant. In real ceramics it varies a little with temperature, so B itself drifts: the maker may quote B25/50 (between 25 and 50 °C) and B25/85 (between 25 and 85 °C) with slightly different values. Over a wide range, such as −40 to 150 °C, the Beta equation can be off by several degrees at the extremes. The Steinhart–Hart equation, 1/T = a + b·ln R + c·(ln R)³, adds terms to follow the curve to within a few hundredths of a degree. The comparator widget above uses the simple Beta form, so its NTC values at −40 and 150 °C will differ from a maker's table.
