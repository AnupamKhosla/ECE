BMS protocols and commissioning: BACnet, Modbus, Niagara

Controllers from different makers have to share data, and every new system has to be proven before handover. This chapter teaches the protocols you will meet (BACnet, Modbus, Niagara, plus LonWorks, KNX and DALI in brief), how to map a raw register to a real value, and the commissioning steps a technician signs off. It ends with a free home lab you can build to practise.

Reading 60 minLevel Beginner–IntermediateLab cost ~A$0–150Builds on C01, P14, P19

Why protocols matter

A protocol is an agreed language: what the bytes on the wire mean. In a building, the chiller comes from one maker, the energy meters from another, the VSDs from a third and the BMS from a fourth. Without a shared protocol, each needs a custom interface. With an open protocol, the BMS can read the chiller's leaving water temperature as easily as its own sensor.

Two open protocols dominate building work: BACnet, designed for buildings, and Modbus, a simple industrial protocol found in meters, drives and plant. On top of these, many integrators use a software framework (often Niagara) to bring everything into one database. As a technician, most of your protocol work is integration: finding devices, mapping points, checking values are scaled right, and fixing communication faults.

BACnet: objects and properties

BACnet is the "Building Automation and Control networks" protocol, published as ASHRAE Standard 135 and also as ISO 16484-5. Its key idea is simple: every device describes itself as a collection of standard objects, and every object has standard properties. You do not need the maker's manual to know that an Analog Input object has a Present_Value. That is what makes BACnet "interoperable".

Properties that matter on site

  • Present_Value – the current value. This is what graphics display.
  • Status_Flags – four true/false flags: IN_ALARM, FAULT, OVERRIDDEN, OUT_OF_SERVICE. Look here first when something looks wrong.
  • Out_Of_Service – when TRUE, the Present_Value is disconnected from the physical input and can be written by hand. Useful for testing, dangerous if forgotten.
  • Reliability – tells you why a FAULT is set, for example open-loop or shorted-loop on a sensor.
  • Units – engineering units. A wrong unit (°F instead of °C) is a common integration error.
  • Priority_Array and Relinquish_Default – on commandable objects (AO, BO, AV, BV…). See below.

The priority array: why your command "doesn't work"

A commandable object has 16 priority slots. Several sources can write to it at different priorities, and the lowest-numbered (highest-priority) non-empty slot wins. If every slot is empty (NULL), the Present_Value takes the Relinquish_Default. Common conventions: priority 1 and 2 are life-safety, 5 is critical equipment control, 8 is manual operator, and control programs often write at a low priority such as 16.

Worked example: a supply fan BO has the program writing inactive at priority 16 (after-hours), and an operator wrote active at priority 8 last week to test it and forgot. Result: the fan runs all night, every night, until someone writes NULL at priority 8 ("relinquishes" the override). Finding forgotten overrides is one of the most valuable things a service tech does for energy use.

Common misconception

Wrong: "I wrote the value, so it changed."
Right: in BACnet you write a request at a priority. If something is holding a higher priority, your write is stored but ignored. Always read back the Priority_Array, and always release your overrides before leaving site.

BACnet networks: IP, MS/TP, routers and BBMDs

BACnet can run over several "data links". In Australian buildings two matter:

  • BACnet/IP: BACnet messages inside UDP packets on Ethernet. The standard port is UDP 47808 (hex BAC0, an easy way to remember it). Used by network controllers, front ends and newer IP field controllers.
  • BACnet MS/TP (master-slave/token-passing): BACnet over an RS-485 twisted pair. Cheap and still very common for VAV and FCU controllers. Typical speeds are 38,400 or 76,800 bit/s (9,600 and 19,200 also exist). Each device has a MAC address from 0 to 127 for masters. Masters pass a token: only the device holding the token may talk.

Addresses: device instance vs MAC

There are two different numbers, and mixing them up is a classic beginner mistake:

  • The device instance (0 to 4,194,302) is the device's name on the whole BACnet internetwork. It must be unique across the whole site, including every building on a campus that shares a network. Sites use a numbering plan, for example building + level + device number.
  • The MAC address is the local address on one link: the IP address and port for BACnet/IP, or 0–127 on an MS/TP trunk. Two trunks can both have a MAC 5.

Each separate link also has a network number (1 to 65,534) so routers can deliver messages between them. Duplicate network numbers or duplicate device instances cause strange, intermittent faults that are hard to trace.

Discovery: Who-Is and I-Am

To find devices, a tool broadcasts Who-Is (optionally limited to a range of instances). Each device replies with I-Am, giving its instance, max message size and vendor ID. The tool then uses ReadProperty or ReadPropertyMultiple to read the Object_List and each object's properties. Values can update by polling, or by COV (change-of-value) subscriptions, where the device sends a message only when a value changes by more than its COV increment.

BBMD: crossing subnets

Who-Is is a broadcast, and IP routers do not forward broadcasts. A BBMD (BACnet Broadcast Management Device) on each subnet holds a table of the other BBMDs and forwards broadcasts to them as normal unicast packets. A laptop on a different subnet can register with a BBMD as a foreign device to join in. Rule: one BBMD per subnet, and all BBMD tables must match. Two BBMDs on the same subnet cause broadcast storms.

Safety and security

Classic BACnet/IP has no passwords or encryption: anyone who can send a UDP packet to port 47808 can write to a BO and start a fan. Never connect a BMS network to the internet or to a guest network. Keep it on its own VLAN behind the owner's firewall, with remote access by VPN. A newer option, BACnet Secure Connect (BACnet/SC), adds encrypted TLS connections through a hub; you will see it on newer projects. Never scan or write to a building network you are not authorised to work on.

MS/TP wiring and faults

MS/TP is RS-485, so all the rules from Practice P14 apply: twisted pair, daisy-chained (no star or long stubs), 120 Ω termination at the two physical ends only, bias resistors at one point, shield earthed at one end. A rule of thumb from the RS-485 standard is about 32 standard unit loads and about 1,200 m per segment; check the controller maker's limits.

Two 120 Ω terminators in parallel = 120 × 120 / (120 + 120) = 60 Ω Worked check: with the trunk powered down, measure resistance between the A(+) and B(−) conductors. About 60 Ω means two terminators (correct). About 120 Ω means one is missing. About 40 Ω means three are fitted, one too many.

Modbus: registers, scaling and word order

Modbus is older and much simpler than BACnet. There are no objects, names or units: just numbered 16-bit registers and single-bit coils. A master (client) asks a slave (server) for a block of registers, and gets back numbers. What those numbers mean is written only in the device's register map (in the manual). That is why Modbus integration is mostly careful reading and arithmetic.

  • Modbus RTU: binary frames over RS-485, slave addresses 1–247, settings like 9600 8N1 or 19200 8E1, a CRC at the end of each frame.
  • Modbus TCP: the same requests inside TCP on port 502, with a "unit ID" to reach devices behind a gateway.
Function codeWhat it doesOld-style numbering
01 / 05Read coils / write single coil (1-bit outputs)0xxxx

The off-by-one trap

Manuals often say "register 40001". On the wire, that is holding register address 0. Some tools want the 40001 form, some want 0, some want 1. If every value you read looks like it belongs to the next point in the map, you are one register out.

Scaling

A register holds a whole number from 0 to 65,535 (or −32,768 to 32,767 if signed). Decimals are carried by a scale factor. Example: a meter's map says "Voltage L1-N, register 40101, unsigned 16-bit, × 0.1 V". You read 2315, so the voltage is 231.5 V. A temperature of −5.0 °C stored as signed × 0.1 is −50, which as a raw unsigned register is 65,536 − 50 = 65,486. Read it as unsigned by mistake and you see 6,548.6 °C.

32-bit values and word order

Bigger numbers (energy totals) and floating-point numbers need two registers. Modbus never standardised which register holds the "high" half, so makers differ. This is called word order (or "word swap").

Analogue scaling works the same way

Hard-wired analogue inputs use straight-line scaling too. A duct pressure transmitter sends 4–20 mA for 0–500 Pa. At 12 mA:

value = (12 − 4) / (20 − 4) × (500 − 0) + 0 = 0.5 × 500 = 250 Pa General form: value = (signal − signal_low) / (signal_high − signal_low) × (eng_high − eng_low) + eng_low. If the controller is set for 0–1,000 Pa but the transmitter is ranged 0–500 Pa, every reading is exactly double: a common commissioning find.

Niagara: the integration framework

Niagara (by Tridium) is a software framework rather than a protocol. Many integrators and manufacturers build systems on it, so you will meet it under different badges. The concepts, in neutral terms:

  • Station: one running Niagara application with its own database of components. It runs on an embedded controller or on a server.
  • JACE: the embedded network controller that runs a station in a panel on site. A Supervisor is a station on a server that gathers data from many JACEs and serves graphics to users.
  • Workbench: the engineering tool on your laptop used to connect to stations and configure them.
  • Drivers: each protocol (BACnet, Modbus, LonWorks, others) is a driver. Under a driver network you discover devices, and under each device you create proxy points that mirror the remote values.
  • Wire sheets: graphical logic. You drag function blocks (math, logic, PID loops, timers) onto a sheet and wire them together.
  • Histories and alarms: extensions added to points to log trends and generate alarms, routed to consoles, email or other stations.
  • Graphics (often called Px pages or HTML5 views) and tagging, which attaches semantic tags so points can be searched and analysed.

Niagara software is licensed, and certification courses are paid; in practice your employer arranges them. You can still learn every concept above for free with BACnet and Modbus tools, because Niagara only organises those same devices and points.

In Australia

"Niagara 4 experience" and "BACnet integration" appear often in Australian BMS job ads, along with platform names from C01. If you have not used Niagara yet, say honestly that you understand drivers, devices, proxy points and histories conceptually, and show your home-lab BACnet/Modbus project. Employers hire for the concepts and train the tool.

Other protocols in brief

LonWorks (legacy)

A protocol from the 1990s built around the Neuron chip, using a free-topology twisted pair and "network variables" with standard types. Still found in older buildings. Today you mostly meet it when migrating a site to BACnet.

KNX

A European bus for lighting, blinds and room control, common in high-end homes and some commercial fit-outs. A low-voltage twisted-pair bus, configured with the ETS software. Devices have an individual address like 1.1.12 and talk via group addresses like 1/0/5. Integrated to the BMS through a gateway.

DALI

Digital Addressable Lighting Interface (IEC 62386). A two-wire bus to LED drivers: up to 64 addresses per line, 16 groups and 16 scenes. DALI-2 adds sensors and push-buttons. Used for dimming, scheduling and often emergency lighting monitoring.

Safety

Do not treat a DALI bus as harmless low voltage. In most installations the DALI pair runs in the same cable as the 230 V lighting supply, and the standard does not class the bus as SELV. Treat DALI terminals as mains-adjacent and leave the work to the electrician.

Commissioning: proving the system works

Commissioning is the process of proving that every point, every sequence and every interface works as designed, and recording the proof. A building cannot be handed over until it is done. For a technician, commissioning is where you learn fastest: you touch every device in the building.

Point-to-point (I/O) checks

A point-to-point check proves the whole chain for one point: the right device, in the right place, wired to the right terminal, configured with the right type and range, named correctly, and shown correctly on the graphic.

Results go on ITC sheets (inspection and test certificates) or commissioning sheets: one row per point with the value at the field, the value on the BMS, pass/fail, your initials and the date. Often a consultant or the builder's representative witnesses a sample, and their signature goes on the sheet too.

Functional performance tests

A functional performance test (FPT) proves a sequence of operations, the written description of how plant should behave. Example sequence: "When the AHU is in cooling and outside air is at least 2 K cooler than return air, open the outside air damper to hold supply air setpoint before opening the chilled water valve." To test it, you often override a value (for example put the outside air temperature AI out of service and write a test value), watch the response, record it, and then release the override. Other common FPTs: fire mode (with the fire contractor, see C04), power-fail restart, duty/standby pump changeover, and after-hours requests.

Loop tuning and trend review

Each PID loop is tuned so it settles without hunting (oscillating). C03 lets you practise in a simulator. After handover, trends of key points are kept for one or two weeks and reviewed for hunting loops, plant short-cycling, heating and cooling fighting each other, and wrong schedules.

As-built documentation and handover

  • Final points list, schematics and wiring diagrams marked up to match what was actually built ("as-built").
  • A network riser diagram with every device instance, MAC and IP address.
  • Backups of every controller program and the front-end database, in a known location.
  • O&M (operation and maintenance) manuals, operator training, and passwords handed over securely, not in an email.
  • A defects period follows handover (often 12 months by contract), during which the installer fixes problems.
On the job

New techs often start on commissioning crews doing point-to-point checks with a laptop, a multimeter, a reference thermometer and a radio. One person in the ceiling or plant room, one at the front end, working down the ITC sheet. It is repetitive, but it teaches you every device type and every common fault, and a tech who keeps neat, honest sheets is trusted quickly.

Bench tip

Keep an override log: every point you put out of service or write at priority 8, with the time. Before leaving site, go down the list and release each one, then check the Priority_Array is clear. Many front ends also have a report of all overridden points; run it.

Free tools and a home lab

You can practise almost everything in this chapter at home for little money. Everything here is ELV: a laptop, a Raspberry Pi on its USB power supply, and a 5 V Arduino.

  • YABEFree "Yet Another BACnet Explorer" for Windows: Who-Is, browse objects, read and write properties.
  • WiresharkFree packet capture. Filters: bvlc, bacnet, bacapp for BACnet; mbtcp, modbus for Modbus TCP.
  • QModMasterFree Modbus master (poller) for RTU and TCP. Similar free tools exist.
  • Python librariespymodbus for a Modbus server or client; BAC0 or bacpypes3 for a simulated BACnet device. Check their current docs; APIs change.
  • Raspberry Pi 4 or 5~A$90–150 with power supply. Or use any spare PC.
  • USB to RS-485 adapter~A$10–30 (Core Electronics, Jaycar, element14, AliExpress).
  • RS-485 modules for Arduino~A$3–8 each; MAX485-type TTL modules.
  • Twisted pair + 120 Ω resistorsAn offcut of Cat6 works for a bench trunk.

  1. Modbus TCP server on the Pi. Write a small pymodbus server with a register map you design: register 0 = room temperature × 10 (int16), registers 10–11 = an energy total (uint32), registers 20–21 = a float voltage. Use port 5020, because ports below 1024 need root on Linux. Write the map in a table, exactly like a maker's manual.

  2. Poll it from the PC. In QModMaster, read holding registers from the Pi's IP. Decode them with the widget above. Deliberately set the wrong word order and the wrong start address, and write down what the errors look like.

  3. Modbus RTU on a real bus. Make an Arduino with an RS-485 module into a slave (ID 1, 9600 8N1) that reports a thermistor reading. Poll it with the USB adapter. Fit 120 Ω at both ends, then remove one and watch the error count. Look at the frames with a logic analyser (P15).

  4. A simulated BACnet device. On the Pi, run a Python BACnet device with an Analog Input "Room temp", a Binary Output "Fan" and an Analog Value "Setpoint", with a device instance from your own numbering plan.

  5. Discover and command it. In YABE on a PC on the same subnet, send Who-Is, find your device, read Present_Value, write the BO at priority 8, read the Priority_Array, then write NULL to relinquish. Capture it all in Wireshark and find the Who-Is, I-Am, ReadProperty and WriteProperty packets.

  6. Document it. Make a points list, a riser diagram and an ITC sheet for your lab, and tick each point off. This is a portfolio piece (see C12).

Safety and ethics

Run these tools only on your own home network. Do not scan, discover or write to building networks at work or anywhere else unless you are authorised and following the site's procedures. A careless Who-Is flood or a stray write can disrupt real plant.

Interview questions

"What port does BACnet/IP use, and what transport?"

UDP port 47808 (0xBAC0) by default. Modbus TCP uses TCP port 502.

"What is a BBMD and when do you need one?"

A BACnet Broadcast Management Device forwards BACnet broadcasts such as Who-Is between IP subnets, because IP routers do not pass broadcasts. You need one per subnet when BACnet devices are spread over more than one subnet.

"What is the difference between a device instance and a MAC address?"

The device instance is unique across the whole BACnet internetwork and identifies the device. The MAC is its local address on one link (an IP address, or 0–127 on MS/TP) and only needs to be unique on that link.

"Half the VAVs on a trunk keep dropping offline. Where do you start?"

Check the trunk physically: termination (measure about 60 Ω across A and B with power off), daisy-chain topology, shield earthed at one point, cable not beside VSD cables. Then check for duplicate MACs, baud mismatches and the Max_Master setting. Watch the signal on a scope if available.

"A meter reads 3.8 billion kWh on the BMS. What's wrong?"

Almost certainly the word order of a 32-bit value is swapped (or the register offset is one out). Compare with the meter's display, check the register map, and swap word order in the driver.

"You command a fan off and it keeps running. Why?"

Something holds a higher priority in the Priority_Array (an old operator override at 8, or a life-safety command), or the MSSB switch is in Hand, or a fire mode is active. Read the Priority_Array, then check the switchboard and interlocks.

"What is a point-to-point check?"

Proving each I/O point end to end: the correct device, wiring, terminal, configuration, name and graphic, by comparing a real field condition with the BMS reading or by commanding an output and watching the device. Recorded on an ITC sheet.

Check yourself

An AV setpoint has priority 8 = 24.0 and priority 16 = 22.5, and Relinquish_Default = 21.0. What is its Present_Value? What happens if someone writes NULL at priority 8?

Present_Value is 24.0, because 8 is the highest-priority (lowest-numbered) slot in use. After NULL at 8, the value becomes 22.5 from priority 16. Only if 16 is also cleared does it fall to 21.0.

Your laptop on subnet 192.168.20.0/24 sends Who-Is but only sees devices on its own subnet. Why, and what are two fixes?

The broadcast does not cross the IP router. Fixes: register your tool as a foreign device with the site's BBMD, or plug into the subnet where the devices are (with permission). If no BBMD exists, the site needs one per subnet.

You measure 120 Ω between A and B on a powered-down MS/TP trunk. What does that tell you?

Only one 120 Ω terminator is in circuit; the far end is missing its terminator (or there is a break between the ends). A correct trunk reads about 60 Ω.

A meter's map says "Current, register 40015, uint16, ×0.01 A". Your tool reads 1,234 at address 14. What is the current?

40015 corresponds to address 14 (zero-based), so you are reading the right register. 1,234 × 0.01 = 12.34 A.

An outside air temperature arrives over Modbus as 65,476 with a scale of ×0.1. What is the temperature?

As a signed 16-bit value: 65,476 − 65,536 = −60, × 0.1 = −6.0 °C. If you see 6,547.6 °C, the point is set as unsigned.

A 4–20 mA transmitter is ranged 0–1,000 Pa but the controller is configured for 0–500 Pa. The real pressure is 400 Pa. What does the BMS show?

400 Pa on a 0–1,000 Pa range gives 4 + 0.4 × 16 = 10.4 mA. The controller maps 10.4 mA onto 0–500 Pa: (10.4 − 4)/16 × 500 = 200 Pa. It reads half the real value.

Why should you never leave an AI "out of service" after a functional test?

While out of service, its Present_Value is frozen at whatever you wrote, so the control loop keeps acting on a fake value. Plant may overcool, overheat or run all night. Always release overrides and check Status_Flags.
