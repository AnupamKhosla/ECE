Seeing signals: oscilloscope and logic analyser

A multimeter gives you one number. An oscilloscope shows you the whole shape of a voltage over time, and a logic analyser turns digital signals into decoded bytes. By the end you will set volts/div, time/div and the trigger with confidence, measure PWM and power-supply ripple from earlier labs, and decode UART and I²C traffic with free software.

Time 3–4 hLevel IntermediateKit cost ~A$15 (analyser) to A$900 (bench scope)Theory T07, T16

What a scope shows that a meter cannot

A multimeter on DC volts measures for a fraction of a second and shows the average. On AC volts it shows an RMS value (a kind of effective average). Either way you get one number, updated a few times per second. Anything that happens faster, or any shape, is hidden.

An oscilloscope ("scope") draws a graph: voltage up the screen, time across. It captures millions of samples per second, so it shows things a meter never can:

  • the real shape of a PWM signal: 0 V and 5 V, not the "average" the meter shows;
  • the ripple riding on a DC supply;
  • glitches, spikes and ringing lasting microseconds;
  • button bounce (P11) and coil kickback spikes (P08, P13);
  • serial data: the bits of UART, I²C and RS-485 (P14);
  • noise on sensor and 0–10 V signals.

Prices are indicative and change often. Well-known entry-level makers include Rigol, Siglent, Owon and Hantek, sold through element14, RS Components, Jaycar, Altronics and online. Three numbers matter:

  • Bandwidth (MHz): the frequency at which a sine wave's displayed amplitude has fallen by about 30%. Choose at least 3–5 times the highest frequency you care about; square waves need more, because their sharp edges contain higher harmonics.
  • Sample rate (Sa/s): how many points per second it captures. Aim for at least 5–10 samples across the fastest detail you want to see.
  • Memory depth: how many samples it stores. Deep memory lets you keep a high sample rate over a long time window, for example a whole Modbus frame.
In Australia

Buy a scope whose power supply carries the RCM mark, and keep it on a tested and tagged lead if your workplace requires it (common on construction and commercial sites under work health and safety rules). Second-hand scopes on marketplaces are good value; ask the seller to show the self-calibration passing.

Probes: ×1, ×10 and compensation

A scope input looks like 1 MΩ in parallel with about 15–20 pF. A probe cable adds more capacitance. That capacitance is the problem: at high frequencies it draws current from your circuit and rounds off fast edges.

  • ×1 setting: the tip connects straight through. Full sensitivity (good for tiny signals), but the cable's ~100 pF loads the circuit and bandwidth is often only a few MHz.
  • ×10 setting: a 9 MΩ resistor in the probe tip forms a 10:1 divider with the scope's 1 MΩ. The circuit now sees about 10 MΩ and only around 10–15 pF, so it is disturbed far less, and the full scope bandwidth is available. The signal arrives 10 times smaller, so you must set the channel's probe factor to ×10 and the scope multiplies back. Use ×10 for nearly everything.

For the divider to be correct at all frequencies, the capacitor across the 9 MΩ must match the scope's input capacitance in the right ratio. A small trimmer in the probe sets this: compensation. Do it once per probe, per channel.

  1. Connect the probe tip to the scope's calibration terminal (often marked with a square-wave symbol, giving roughly 1 kHz) and the ground clip to its ground tab. Set the probe switch and the channel to ×10.

  2. Press Auto (or Autoset), then adjust to show two or three cycles filling most of the screen.

  3. Adjust the trimmer with the plastic tool until the tops and bottoms are flat with square corners, as in Figure 2.

The ground clip is earthed: the one rule that saves equipment

On almost every mains-powered bench scope, the probe's ground clip is connected to the scope's metal case and to mains protective earth through the power cord. The clip is not a floating "negative lead" like a multimeter's black lead. Wherever you clip it, you connect that point to earth.

Safety: scope ground and mains

1. Only clip the ground lead to a point that is at ground (the circuit's 0 V) unless you are certain the whole circuit floats (battery, or an isolated plug-pack with nothing else connected).
2. Never remove or defeat the scope's earth pin ("floating the scope"). Its case could then rise to a dangerous voltage.
3. Never probe mains. Measuring 230 V needs differential probes, equipment rated for the right measurement category (CAT II/III), and in Australia the work is for licensed electricians. Everything in this course stays at extra-low voltage.
4. With two probes, both ground clips are joined inside the scope. Clip both to the same point.
5. Rectifier circuits: clip only to the DC negative output. A ground clip on the AC side of a bridge rectifier, with another clip on DC negative, shorts one of the diodes.

Common misconception

"The ground clip is just the negative lead, like on my multimeter." A multimeter is battery-powered and fully floating, so its black lead can go anywhere. A mains-powered scope's ground is tied to earth. Battery handheld scopes are different again: they float, but on many models the channels share one common, so two clips at different voltages still short. Read the manual for your exact scope.

The controls: volts/div, time/div and the trigger

The screen is a grid, usually 10 divisions across and 8 divisions high. Every control answers one question about that grid.

Worked example. A 0–5 V square wave at 1 kHz. At 1 V/div it is 5 divisions tall. Its period is 1/1000 = 1 ms. At 200 µs/div one period spans 5 divisions, so you see two full cycles across the 10-division screen. Reading back: count 5 divisions × 200 µs = 1 ms → f = 1 kHz.

Why the trigger matters

The scope captures a new picture many times per second. Without a trigger, each picture starts at a random point in the waveform, so the trace slides about and blurs. The trigger tells the scope: "start every picture at the moment the signal crosses this level, going in this direction". Every picture then lines up exactly, and a repeating waveform stands still. If the trigger level is outside the signal's range (above its top or below its bottom), nothing ever triggers: in Auto mode the scope draws anyway, untriggered and rolling; in Normal mode the screen freezes on the last good picture.

Measurement 1: PWM from your 555 (P09) and Arduino

  1. Connect CH1 (×10) tip to the 555 output (pin 3), ground clip to the circuit's 0 V. Your circuit runs from a battery or plug-pack, so the clip is safe on 0 V.

  2. Set DC coupling, 2 V/div, 0 V marker two divisions below centre, trigger CH1 rising at about half the supply voltage. Press Auto if you are lost, then fine-tune.

  3. Measure with the cursors or the automatic measurements: frequency, period, positive width, duty cycle, peak-to-peak. Compare frequency with your P09 calculation f ≈ 1.44 / ((R1 + 2R2) × C).

  4. Compare with the meter. Put the meter on DC volts across the same points. It should read about duty × Vhigh.

  5. Arduino PWM. Probe an Uno pin running analogWrite(9, 64). Expect about 490 Hz and 25% duty. Pins 5 and 6 give about 980 Hz.

Measurement 2: ripple on your rectifier supply (P07)

Rebuild the full-wave bridge with a smoothing capacitor from P07, fed from an AC plug-pack (extra-low voltage). Add a load so ripple appears.

Vripple(p-p) ≈ Iload / (fripple × C) A 12 V AC plug-pack gives a peak of about 12 × √2 ≈ 17 V (often more when lightly loaded), minus two diode drops (~1.4 V) ≈ 15.5 V DC. Into 150 Ω that is about 0.10 A. Ripple ≈ 0.10 / (100 × 0.001) ≈ 1.0 V peak-to-peak. The resistor dissipates about 15.5² / 150 ≈ 1.6 W, so use a 5 W part and keep fingers off it: it gets hot.

  1. DC coupling first. 5 V/div: you see a nearly flat line at about 15 V. The ripple is too small to judge.

  2. Switch to AC coupling, 200 mV/div, 5 ms/div, trigger level near 0 V. The sawtooth fills the screen. Measure peak-to-peak and the 10 ms spacing.

  3. Experiment. Double the capacitor (add another 1000 µF in parallel): ripple should halve. Remove the load: ripple almost disappears. With the plug-pack unplugged, remove one diode so the bridge becomes a half-wave rectifier, then power up again: the ripple roughly doubles and its frequency drops to 50 Hz.

  4. Compare with the meter on AC volts. A 1 V p-p sawtooth reads only about 0.3 V RMS (Vpp / (2√3)), and many cheap meters read it inaccurately. The scope's peak-to-peak number is what matters for regulator headroom.

Why it works

Between peaks the capacitor alone feeds the load, so it loses charge at a steady rate: dV/dt = I/C. Over one ripple period (1/f) the voltage falls by I/(f × C). A bigger capacitor or a lighter load means a smaller fall. AC coupling simply puts a capacitor in series with the scope input to block the steady DC part, so you can use a sensitive volts/div setting on the small changing part.

The logic analyser: many digital channels, decoded

A scope shows a few channels with full analogue detail. A logic analyser shows many channels as only HIGH or LOW, over a long time, and can decode them into bytes. The small USB analysers sold for about A$15 have 8 channels and sample up to about 24 MHz. With the free sigrok software and its graphical program PulseView (it uses a driver called fx2lafw for these devices), you can decode UART, I²C, SPI, 1-Wire, Wiegand and many more protocols.

Safety: what the analyser can take

These cheap analysers are for logic signals: most accept roughly 0–5 V, and their inputs have little protection. Never connect them to RS-232 (±12 V), to the A/B lines of RS-485 directly (differential, and can go negative), to 12/24 V field wiring, or to anything mains-related. To look at RS-485 traffic, probe the TTL side of the transceiver (its RO and DI pins) instead. Check your unit's listing for its input range.

Lab: decode UART

  1. Install PulseView from the sigrok project's website. On Windows, its installer includes a tool (Zadig) to set up the USB driver for the analyser.

  2. Wire analyser GND to Arduino GND and CH0 to the TX pin of the P14 sender sketch (D11), or to D1 for normal Serial output.

  3. Capture. In PulseView choose the fx2lafw device, sample rate 1 MHz (about 100 samples per bit at 9600 baud), 1 M samples (one second). Click Run.

  4. Decode. Add a protocol decoder: UART. Set RX = D0, baud rate 9600, 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit. Set the display format to ASCII. You will read "Hello from board A" across the trace.

  5. Break it on purpose. Set the decoder to 19 200 baud: you get framing errors and garbage, exactly what a mismatched device sees.

Lab: decode I²C

  1. Wire CH1 to SCL (A5), CH2 to SDA (A4), GND to GND. Run the P14 I²C scanner (press the Uno's reset button to repeat it).

  2. Capture at 4 MHz or more (at least 10 samples per bit at 400 kHz; 100 kHz needs less). Use a trigger on CH2 falling edge so the capture starts with the first START condition.

  3. Decode: add the I²C decoder with SCL = D1 and SDA = D2. You will see the scanner trying "Address write 0x01, NACK", "0x02, NACK"… and then "0x3C, ACK" for an OLED (or whatever your module's address is).

Bench tip

Sample at least 4 times, and ideally 10 times, faster than the fastest bit rate you are decoding. Too slow and edges land between samples, so decoders report errors that are not really on the wire. Too fast simply uses more memory: lower the sample count or rate for long captures.

On the job

A handheld scope or scope-meter earns its place in a BMS or security technician's kit. Typical uses: checking a 24 V DC or 12 V DC supply for ripple and dropouts (noisy supplies cause rolling lines on analogue CCTV images and random reboots of controllers and cameras); confirming a 0–10 V control signal is clean and steady rather than full of VSD noise; seeing whether an RS-485 trunk has ringing or reflections from bad termination (use two channels, A and B, both clips on the signal common, and view CH1 − CH2); and checking a 4–20 mA loop by viewing the voltage across a 250 Ω resistor (1–5 V). Access control technicians use a logic analyser to decode Wiegand reader output (C06) when a card reads "wrong". Always clip to the equipment's 0 V and keep to the ELV side of the equipment.

Check yourself

A waveform is 3.4 divisions tall at 2 V/div. What is its peak-to-peak voltage?

3.4 × 2 = 6.8 V peak-to-peak.

One cycle spans 4 divisions at 500 µs/div. What is the frequency?

Period = 4 × 500 µs = 2 ms, so f = 1 / 0.002 = 500 Hz.

Why is ×10 the normal probe setting?

It presents about 10 MΩ and only around 10–15 pF to the circuit, so it disturbs it far less and gives the scope's full bandwidth. The 10× smaller signal is corrected by setting the channel to ×10. ×1 is only for very small, slow signals.

You set the trigger level to 8 V on a 0–5 V square wave. What happens?

The signal never crosses 8 V, so the trigger never fires. In Auto mode the scope draws untriggered pictures that start at random points, so the trace rolls; in Normal mode it waits and shows nothing new.

Why must you not clip a mains-powered scope's ground lead to the 5 V side of a shunt resistor on a PC-powered Arduino?

The ground clip is connected to protective earth, and the PC's 0 V is also earthed. Clipping to 5 V connects 5 V to 0 V through the earth path, a short circuit. Use two probes with both grounds on 0 V and display the difference.

A bridge rectifier on 50 Hz with 2200 µF supplies 0.5 A. Estimate the ripple.

Full-wave ripple frequency is 100 Hz. Vpp ≈ I / (f × C) = 0.5 / (100 × 0.0022) ≈ 2.3 V.

What sample rate should you use to decode 115 200 baud UART with a logic analyser?

At least 4× and ideally 10× the bit rate: about 0.5–1.2 MHz. A 2 MHz or 4 MHz setting is a comfortable choice.

Why can you not connect a cheap logic analyser straight to RS-485 A and B?

RS-485 is differential: the information is the difference between A and B, and each wire can sit at voltages the analyser's single-ended, roughly 0–5 V inputs cannot read correctly, including negative ones. Probe the transceiver's TTL-side pins (RO, DI) instead.
