Access control and intruder alarms

How an electronic door works, part by part: readers, cards, the Wiegand and OSDP wires between them, locks, exit buttons and door switches. Then intruder alarms: detectors, zones, end-of-line resistors and monitoring. By the end you can wire a door on paper, decode a card read, and explain why egress doors must always let people out.

The big picture

Access control decides who may open which door at what time, and keeps a log of every attempt. An intruder alarm (also called a burglar alarm or security alarm) detects people where they should not be, and raises an alarm locally and at a monitoring centre. On commercial sites the two systems are often one product, or are tightly linked. For example, badging in at the front door can also disarm the alarm for that area.

Why it works

A lock coil is an inductor. When the relay opens, the coil's magnetic field collapses and drives a voltage spike that can reach hundreds of volts for a moment. That spike arcs the relay contacts and can reset or damage the controller. A diode across the coil, cathode (band) to the + side, gives the current a safe loop to die away in. Many locks have one built in; if not, you fit one (or the varistor the maker supplies).

Credentials: cards, fobs and phones

125 kHz proximity cards

The old "prox" card has a coil antenna and a tiny chip. The reader makes a 125 kHz magnetic field. The card harvests power from it and replies with a fixed number by loading the field on and off. That is all it does. There is no secret and no challenge. Anyone with a matching reader, held within a few centimetres of the card, can read the number. A cheap handheld copier can then write it onto a blank card. This is why security consultants now recommend moving off 125 kHz.

13.56 MHz smart cards

Smart cards work at 13.56 MHz (the same band as NFC). A secure smart card holds secret keys and does mutual authentication: the reader and the card each prove they know the key, using cryptography (usually AES-128 on current cards), without ever sending the key itself. Copying the card's number is no longer enough. Be aware that early 13.56 MHz products, such as MIFARE Classic and legacy iCLASS, have known weaknesses. Current product families, such as MIFARE DESFire EV2/EV3 and newer iCLASS SE / Seos credentials, are what specifications usually ask for now.

Mobile credentials, PINs and biometrics

Mobile credentials put the card in a phone app and talk to the reader over Bluetooth Low Energy or NFC. PIN keypads add "something you know". Card + PIN readers are common on comms rooms. Biometric readers (fingerprint, face) are used on high-security doors and bring privacy obligations for the client.

Common misconception

"An encrypted card means the door is secure." Security is a chain. If the reader sends the card number to the controller on plain Wiegand wires, someone can tap those wires behind the reader and record or replay the number. Secure cards need a secure reader link (OSDP Secure Channel), tamper switches on readers, and controllers in locked enclosures.

Reader protocols: Wiegand and OSDP

Wiegand

Wiegand is the old, very common reader interface. Two data lines, D0 and D1, are both held high (usually 5 V) when idle. To send a 0, the reader pulls D0 low for a short pulse (typically around 50 µs). To send a 1, it pulls D1 low. Bits follow each other about 1–2 ms apart. It is one-way (reader to controller), not encrypted and not supervised. If the cable is cut, the controller only notices when nobody can get in. Typical maximum cable length is about 150 m with suitable cable; check the reader's datasheet.

The most common format is the 26-bit format (often called H10301):

Bit(s)FieldRule
1Even parityMakes the number of 1s in bits 1–13 even
2–9Facility code (8 bits)0–255; identifies the site or batch of cards
10–25Card number (16 bits)0–65 535; printed on many cards
26Odd parityMakes the number of 1s in bits 14–26 odd

Worked example. Facility code 12 and card number 34567. 12 in 8 bits is 00001100. 34567 in 16 bits is 1000011100000111. Bits 2–13 (the first 12 data bits) are 00001100 1000, which has three 1s, so the even parity bit must be 1. Bits 14–25 are 011100000111, which has six 1s, so the odd parity bit must be 1 to make seven. The full read is 1 00001100 1000011100000111 1. The 16-bit card number only allows 65 536 cards per facility code, so numbers repeat across sites. Larger sites use longer formats (34, 35 or 37 bits, or a manufacturer's own format).

OSDP

OSDP (Open Supervised Device Protocol) was developed by the Security Industry Association and is also published as an international standard (IEC 60839-11-5). It runs over RS-485: one twisted pair for data plus a pair for power, so four wires in total.

Wiegand

  • One reader per cable
  • One-way: reader → controller
  • No encryption
  • Not supervised: a cut cable is silent
  • ~150 m typical
  • 6 or more cores per reader

OSDP over RS-485

  • Several readers on one bus, each with an address
  • Two-way: the controller can set LEDs, the buzzer and the display, and update reader firmware
  • Secure Channel encryption (AES-128)
  • Supervised: the controller polls each reader and alarms if one stops answering
  • RS-485 reaches much further (up to about 1200 m at low data rates)

OSDP is the right choice for new work and is often written into specifications. When you upgrade an old site, you can usually reuse the existing reader cable if it has a twisted pair.

Fail-safe

Power on = locked. Power off = unlocked. Safe for people. A power cut, a cut wire, a break-glass or a fire alarm releases the door. Maglocks are always fail-safe.

Fail-secure

Power on = unlocked. Power off = stays locked from the outside. Secure for property. People inside can still leave using the mechanical lever, so egress does not depend on power.

Safety · egress doors must release

In Australia the National Construction Code (NCC) requires doors on exits and paths of travel to an exit to be openable from the inside without a key, with a simple single action. An access control system must never trap people. In practice, electric locks on these doors are either fail-secure with a mechanical lever that always opens from the inside, or fail-safe with a REX, a break-glass and a release from the fire indicator panel (FIP) on fire alarm. Which arrangement a door needs depends on the building's fire safety design, and the certifier and fire services decide it. Never bypass a break-glass or fire release, even "temporarily", and never connect anything to a fire panel yourself unless you are authorised to work on it.

Power supplies and battery backup

Add up the current of everything on the supply, then check the battery. Worked example: one 12 V supply feeds a 2-door controller (200 mA), two readers (100 mA each) and two maglocks (400 mA each). Total = 200 + 2 × 100 + 2 × 400 = 1200 mA = 1.2 A. A 12 V 7.2 Ah sealed lead-acid battery would ideally last 7.2 / 1.2 = 6 hours. Real batteries age, and you must not run them flat, so plan for about 70% of that: around 4 hours. If the specification asks for longer standby, use a bigger battery or a separate supply for the locks. Also check the supply's rating covers the full load plus battery charging current.

Door alarms the controller raises

  • Access granted / denied: logged with card number, door and time.
  • Door forced open: the reed opened without a valid card or REX.
  • Door held open: the reed stayed open longer than the set time (for example 30 seconds).
  • Tamper: a reader or enclosure tamper switch opened.
  • Reader offline (OSDP only) and AC fail / low battery from the supply.

Intruder alarms

Detectors

  • PIR (passive infrared). A pyroelectric sensor behind a segmented (Fresnel) lens. Warm bodies emit long-wave infrared: at body temperature, about 310 K, Wien's law puts the peak near 2898 / 310 ≈ 9.3 µm. When a person crosses from one lens segment to the next, the sensor sees a change, and the detector alarms. So a PIR is most sensitive to walking across its view and least sensitive to walking straight towards it. Mount it so likely intruder paths cross the beams. Avoid aiming at heaters, air-conditioner outlets and sunny windows.
  • Dual-technology detectors add a microwave (Doppler) sensor. Both must trigger, which cuts false alarms in difficult spaces.
  • Reed switches on doors and windows: a magnet holds the reed contacts closed while the door is shut. Open the door and the circuit opens.
  • Glass-break detectors listen for the sound pattern of breaking glass: a low thud as the pane flexes, then high-frequency shattering.
  • Shock / vibration sensors on walls, safes and ATMs; beams and outdoor detectors for perimeters.
  • Sirens and strobes inside and outside, keypads to arm and disarm, and panic buttons.

Zones, areas and arming

Each detector connects to a zone (an input on the panel or on an expander module). Zones have types: instant, delayed (entry/exit delay, for the door near the keypad), 24-hour (tampers and panic buttons, active even when disarmed), and so on. Zones are grouped into areas (also called partitions), so the warehouse can be armed while the office is still occupied. Users arm away (everything) or stay / part (perimeter only, while people are inside at night).

End-of-line resistors and tamper

If a zone were just a switch on two wires, an intruder could short the wires together and the panel would always see "closed = normal". To prevent this, the panel measures the resistance of the loop, and resistors are fitted at the far end, inside the detector. That is why they are called end-of-line (EOL) resistors. With a double-EOL scheme the panel can tell four states apart on one pair of wires.

Bench tip

Fit the EOL resistors in the detector, not at the panel terminals. A resistor at the panel end still "proves" the loop to the panel even if someone shorts the cable halfway along, which defeats the whole point. Auditors and senior techs look for this.

Monitoring

Most commercial alarms are monitored: the panel's communicator sends events (alarm, tamper, arm, disarm, AC fail, low battery) to a monitoring centre, where operators follow the client's response plan: phone the keyholders, send a patrol, call police. Older panels used a phone-line dialler. With the move to the NBN, many of those were replaced by IP and 4G communicators, often with two paths so that one failing is reported. Communicators also send regular "test" signals, so the centre knows if a site goes silent.

In Australia

The AS/NZS 2201 series covers intruder alarm systems. Part 1 covers systems at the client's premises (design, installation, commissioning and maintenance) and Part 2 covers monitoring centres. Specifications and insurers often reference this series. Read the current edition your job names; Standards Australia publishes them and your employer or TAFE library may give you access.

On the job: installing and commissioning a door

  1. Survey the door. Door and frame material, swing, which side is secure, glass or solid, existing lock, and whether it is a fire door (look for the tag on the hinge edge). Fire doors must only be modified in ways that keep their certification, so check before you drill.

  2. Choose the hardware. Lock type, fail-safe or fail-secure (per the egress design), reader technology, REX type, reed type, power supply and battery.

  3. Rough-in. Run the reader cable (twisted pair for OSDP), lock cable (heavy enough for the current), and input cables to the controller location. Leave service loops. Fire-stop any penetrations.

  4. Fit-off. Mount the reader at the height and position the specification gives. Fit the lock, armature or strike, reed and REX. Terminate at the controller, labelling every core.

  5. Program. Add the door, schedules, access groups and test cards in the software.

  6. Test every function. Valid card opens; invalid card is denied and logged; REX opens with no alarm; forcing the door raises "door forced"; holding it raises "door held"; the break-glass releases it; a fire alarm test releases it (with the fire contractor); a mains-off test shows the lock does the right thing and the battery carries the load; reader tamper alarms. Record the results.

  7. Handover. As-built wiring, controller and door schedule, user training, and the test sheet.

The same state-based security licensing applies as for CCTV (see C05): for example SLED in NSW, the Licensing & Regulation Division of Victoria Police, and the Office of Fair Trading in Queensland. Installing and servicing alarms and access control is security work under these schemes. Cabling that connects to a telecommunications network may also need an ACMA cabling registration (C07). Confirm the current rules with your state's regulator, because they differ by state and change over time.

Check yourself

A fail-safe maglock is wired through the relay's NC contact. What happens when a valid card is presented, and what happens in a power cut?

A valid card energises the relay, the NC contact opens, the maglock loses power and the door releases. In a power cut the battery holds the door locked until it runs down; if all power is lost, the maglock releases, because it is fail-safe. People can always get out.

Decode this 26-bit read: 0 00000001 0000000001100100 0. Is it valid?

Facility code = 00000001 = 1. Card number = 0000000001100100 = 100. Even parity covers bits 1–13: 0 + 00000001 + 0000 has one 1, which is odd, so the even parity check fails. For this card the first bit should be 1. Bits 14–26 are 000001100100 + 0, which has three 1s, odd, so the odd parity check passes. The controller rejects the read. Try it in the widget.

Why is OSDP called "supervised", and why does that matter?

The controller polls each reader regularly and expects an answer. If a reader is cut off, removed or swapped, the controller notices immediately and raises an alarm. A Wiegand reader only sends data when a card is presented, so a cut cable or tampered reader stays silent.

An intruder shorts a zone's two wires together at the panel end. What does a double-EOL panel see?

Almost 0 Ω instead of the expected 2.2 kΩ (in the example). The panel reports a tamper or trouble on that zone, 24 hours a day. Without EOL resistors, a short would look the same as a closed contact (normal), and the attack would work.

Why is a PIR better at detecting someone walking across a room than towards it?

The Fresnel lens divides the view into segments. The sensor responds to a change in infrared between segments. Walking across the view moves the warm body from segment to segment quickly. Walking straight towards it keeps the body in the same segment, so the change is small.

Controller 250 mA, four readers at 90 mA, four strikes at 300 mA (only one open at a time). What is the worst-case standby load?

Fail-secure strikes draw current only while unlocked, so worst case is one strike on: 250 + 4 × 90 + 300 = 910 mA. If they were fail-safe maglocks drawing all the time, you would count all four: 250 + 360 + 1200 = 1810 mA. Lock type changes the battery size a lot.

Why must the break-glass and fire relay be in series with the maglock power, not just wired to controller inputs?

Because egress must not depend on software, firmware or a working controller. Cutting the lock's power directly releases a fail-safe lock even if the controller has crashed or lost its programming. A controller input only asks the controller to release the door.
