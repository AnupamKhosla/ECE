Transistors as switches: BJT, MOSFET, relays

You will switch a 12 V load from a 5 V signal three ways: with a bipolar transistor (calculating the base resistor so it switches hard), with a logic-level MOSFET, and with a relay protected by a flyback diode. Then you will see exactly where these circuits live inside BMS controllers and access-control panels.

Safety

Everything in this lab runs from 12 V DC and 5 V. A relay's contacts may be printed "10 A 250 V AC". That rating does not give you permission to switch mains: in Australia, connecting anything to 230 V is work for a licensed electrician. Use relays here only to switch ELV loads. Motors and LED strips can draw amps, so keep the plug-pack's current rating in mind and never short its output.

1 · The idea: a small signal controls a big current

A microcontroller pin can supply about 20 mA at 3.3 V or 5 V. A 12 V LED strip may need 1 A; a relay coil 30–70 mA; a door strike 250–500 mA. The pin cannot drive these directly. A transistor is an electrically controlled switch: a small control current (BJT) or a control voltage (MOSFET) opens or closes a path for a much bigger current from a separate supply.

In this lab we always use a low-side switch: the load connects to +12 V, and the transistor sits between the load and ground (0 V). That way the transistor's control terminal is referenced to ground, the same ground as the logic signal. The one rule you must never forget:

The rule people forget: common ground

Wrong: "The 5 V signal and the 12 V supply are separate, so their grounds should stay separate." Right: a voltage is always measured between two points. The base or gate signal only means something relative to the transistor's emitter or source. Join the 0 V of the logic supply to the 0 V of the 12 V supply. Without it, the transistor sees no signal at all, or a random one.

2 · NPN transistor low-side switch

Calculate the base resistor

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) amplifies current: collector current IC ≈ hFE × IB, where hFE (the current gain) is somewhere between about 100 and 800 for a BC547. That spread is the problem. If you design for "exactly enough" base current, a low-gain transistor will not switch fully. So for a switch we ignore the typical gain and push in far more base current than needed. The datasheet rule for a hard, saturated switch is:

IB ≈ IC / 10 RB = (Vcontrol − VBE) / IB VBE ≈ 0.7 V (it rises toward 0.8–0.9 V at high currents). The "/10" is called a forced beta of 10. It is the condition datasheets use when they quote VCE(sat), the voltage left across a fully-on transistor.

Worked example 1: the LED. Load current: (12 − 2) / 560 ≈ 18 mA. IB = 18 / 10 = 1.8 mA. RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.0018 = 2.4 kΩ. The nearest standard value below is 2.2 kΩ (giving 1.95 mA). Round down, so you get a little more base current, not less.

Worked example 2: a 12 V relay coil of 400 Ω. IC = 12 / 400 = 30 mA. IB = 3 mA. RB = 4.3 / 0.003 = 1.43 kΩ → use 1.2 kΩ (3.6 mA). Well within a BC547's 100 mA rating.

Worked example 3: 30 cm of LED strip at 200 mA. Too much for a BC547 (100 mA maximum). Use a 2N2222: IB = 20 mA, RB = 4.3 / 0.02 = 215 Ω → 220 Ω. But 20 mA is the most a typical microcontroller pin should supply. This is where a MOSFET (section 3) becomes the better tool.

Build it and measure VCE(sat)

  1. Pin-out first. A BC547 seen from the flat face, legs down, is usually C–B–E from left to right. A 2N2222 in TO-92 plastic (PN2222A) is often E–B–C, the other way round. Check the datasheet for your exact part; pin-outs vary between makers.

  2. Build Figure 1. 12 V supply to the LED and 560 Ω, then to the collector. Emitter to 0 V. Base through 2.2 kΩ to the control wire; 10 kΩ from base to 0 V. Join the logic supply's 0 V to the 12 V supply's 0 V.

  3. Control wire to 0 V. LED off. Measure collector to emitter (VCE): about 10–12 V. The transistor is open: almost all the supply appears across it.

  4. Control wire to 5 V. LED on. Measure VCE: expect about 0.05–0.15 V. That is VCE(sat). Measure VBE: about 0.7–0.8 V.

  5. Starve the base. Replace 2.2 kΩ with 100 kΩ (IB ≈ 43 µA), then 1 MΩ (≈ 4 µA). Measure VCE each time. The transistor is now in its active (amplifying) region: partly on, with volts across it.

Why it works

An NPN transistor is two p–n junctions back to back with a very thin p-type base in the middle. Forward-biasing the base–emitter junction floods the base with electrons from the emitter. The base is so thin that almost all of them diffuse across it and are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased collector junction. Only a small fraction recombine in the base, and that fraction is the base current. So a small base current "unlocks" a collector current 100 or more times bigger. In saturation, you have supplied more base current than the load can use, both junctions become forward biased, and VCE falls to about 0.1–0.2 V.

3 · Logic-level N-MOSFET low-side switch

A MOSFET (metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor) is controlled by voltage, not current. The gate is insulated by a thin layer of oxide, so in steady state it draws essentially no current. Put enough voltage between gate and source and a conducting channel forms between drain and source with a very low resistance, RDS(on), often tens of milliohms. That makes MOSFETs ideal for switching amps from a microcontroller pin.

What "logic-level" means

Every MOSFET datasheet lists VGS(th), the threshold voltage. Beginners think the MOSFET is "on" above this. It is not. Threshold is where it starts to conduct, typically defined at only 250 µA. What you need is the RDS(on) figure quoted at your gate voltage. A logic-level MOSFET has RDS(on) specified at VGS = 4.5 V (sometimes 2.5 V), so 5 V from an Arduino turns it fully on. A standard MOSFET such as an IRF540N is specified at 10 V; at 5 V it may be only partly on, and at 3.3 V it may barely conduct.

P = I² × RDS(on) = (1.0 A)² × 0.03 Ω = 0.03 W VDS = I × RDS(on) = 30 mV 30 mW in a TO-220 package is nothing; it stays at room temperature without a heatsink. A BJT carrying 1 A with 0.5–1 V across it would make 0.5–1 W and need a base current of about 100 mA. That is why MOSFETs took over power switching.

  1. Pin-out. Most TO-220 MOSFETs, seen from the front with legs down: Gate, Drain, Source from left to right. The metal tab is the drain. Check your datasheet.

  2. Build Figure 2 with the LED strip as the load (strip + to +12 V, strip − to drain).

  3. Switch it. Control to 5 V: strip on. Control to 0 V: strip off.

  4. Measure VDS while on, and the strip current with the meter in series (10 A range, red lead moved to the 10 A socket). Calculate RDS(on) = VDS / I.

  5. Feel the tab after a minute. Cool. Then try 3.3 V as the control (two AA cells ≈ 3 V). A logic-level part usually still works; note any rise in VDS.

  6. Motor version. Swap the strip for a 12 V fan or motor, with the 1N4007 or 1N5819 across the motor (band to +12 V). Add a 100 nF ceramic across the motor terminals to reduce electrical noise.

Try this: why the pull-down matters

Remove the 10 kΩ pull-down and unplug the control wire. Touch the gate leg with a finger, then touch 0 V with the other hand, then 12 V. The strip may switch on or off and stay that way. The gate is a tiny capacitor (a few nanofarads) with almost no leakage, so it holds whatever charge you gave it. In a real product, that is a motor starting by itself while the microcontroller boots. Put the pull-down back.

Why it works

The gate, oxide and silicon underneath form a capacitor. A positive gate voltage pulls electrons to the surface of the p-type silicon under the gate until, above threshold, the surface "inverts" into a thin n-type channel joining the n-type drain and source. More gate voltage, more electrons, lower resistance. No steady current flows into the gate, only the charge needed to fill that capacitor when it switches.

4 · Relays and the flyback diode

A relay is a switch moved by an electromagnet. Current through the coil pulls a metal armature, which moves the contacts. The coil circuit and the contact circuit are electrically separate (isolated), so a 12 V coil can switch a completely different circuit: 24 V AC, a separate 12 V supply, or a dry contact input on another controller. The contacts are labelled COM (common), NO (normally open: joined to COM only when the coil is energised) and NC (normally closed: joined to COM when the coil is off).

Why the coil kicks back

A coil is an inductor. Its magnetic field stores energy (½LI²), and it resists any sudden change in its current: V = L × ΔI/Δt. When the transistor switches off in a microsecond, the coil "tries" to keep its 30 mA flowing. Put numbers in: a relay coil of, say, 0.5 H, with 30 mA stopping in 1 µs, would need 0.5 × 0.03 / 0.000001 = 15 000 V. In reality the voltage climbs until something conducts: the transistor's collector junction breaks down (a BC547 is rated 45 V), or a tiny arc forms. Each event damages the transistor a little; eventually it fails shorted, and the relay stays on forever.

The flyback diode (also called a freewheeling or snubber diode) sits across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation (band to +12 V). At switch-off, the coil's voltage reverses, the diode conducts, and the coil current circulates through the diode until its energy is spent as heat in the coil's resistance. The collector never goes above about 12.7 V.

Why it works

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field, and the field cannot vanish instantly: collapsing it induces a voltage that tries to keep the current flowing (Faraday's and Lenz's laws). The faster you try to stop the current, the bigger that voltage. The diode gives the current a gentle path, so the stored ½LI² turns into a little heat instead of a spike.

  1. Build Figure 3 with the diode fitted. Check the band goes to +12 V. Backwards, it shorts the supply through the transistor as soon as it turns on.

  2. Click it. Control to 5 V: relay clicks; measure continuity COM–NO (beeps) and COM–NC (open). Control to 0 V: the opposite.

  3. Measure the coil (power off) on the ohms range: about 400 Ω for a 12 V relay, about 70 Ω for a 5 V relay. Coil current = V/R.

  4. Use the contacts. Wire a separate LED + 1 kΩ from the 12 V supply through COM and NO. Then move it to NC and see the logic reverse.

  5. If you have a scope (P15), look at the collector at switch-off with and without the diode. Without it, use a spare transistor, keep switching to a few clicks only, and expect the spike to be clipped by breakdown somewhere above 45 V. Do not touch the circuit while doing this. Refit the diode afterwards.

Bench tip: diode side effects

A plain flyback diode lets the coil current die away slowly, so the relay releases a few milliseconds later than it would otherwise. Usually that does not matter. Where fast release matters, designers put a Zener in series with the diode, which lets the voltage rise to a safe, higher level and dumps the energy faster. For AC coils (24 V AC relays and contactors), a diode cannot be used; use the RC snubber or varistor that the relay maker specifies.

Relay modules

The common "5 V relay module" boards contain everything in Figure 3: a transistor, base resistor, flyback diode and an indicator LED, sometimes an optocoupler (an LED and phototransistor in one package for isolation). Know their quirks:

  • Active-low inputs. Many modules switch ON when the input is pulled to 0 V, not 5 V. Read the board or test it.
  • 3.3 V boards. Modules built for 5 V may not switch reliably from a 3.3 V pin. Check before you depend on it.
  • JD-VCC jumper. On opto-isolated modules, removing this jumper lets the relay coils run from a separate supply for real isolation. Read the module's notes.
  • Contact ratings. The relay may say 10 A; the thin PCB tracks and screw terminals on cheap modules may not carry that. Derate generously and keep to ELV loads.

5 · On the job: relay outputs, dry contacts and door locks

Everything above is exactly what sits behind the terminals you will wire on site.

On the job: BMS controllers

A BMS controller's digital outputs (DOs) are usually relay contacts or solid-state switches. A dry contact (also "volt-free" or "potential-free") is a relay contact with no voltage of its own: just a switch between two terminals. The device on the other side provides the voltage, for example a chiller's "remote start" input. A wet output supplies voltage itself. When a controller output is too small or the wrong voltage for the job, it drives an interposing relay: a separate relay in the panel whose coil is driven by the DO and whose contacts switch the bigger or different circuit, such as a pump starter's control circuit. Check the contact rating printed on the controller (voltage, current, AC or DC) before you connect anything. Where an interposing relay's contacts are in a mains control circuit, that side is electrician's work; many BMS roles prefer or require an electrical licence for that reason (see C11).

On the job: access control

Door strikes and maglocks are coils. Switching them off creates the same inductive kick as your relay, and the kick appears on long cables right next to the reader and data wiring. That causes reader resets, damaged relay contacts and controller faults. Manufacturers typically require a diode across each DC lock, at the lock end, band to +. Some locks have suppression built in and some are polarity sensitive: read the lock's instructions. Fail-safe versus fail-secure choices, fire-alarm release of maglocks, and free egress (being able to leave without a key) are governed by the National Construction Code and the relevant Australian standards plus the site's fire design. Your employer and the fire engineer set what is required on a given door; confirm the current rules with your state's building regulator.

In Australia

Security installation work (including access control and alarms) needs a security licence in every state and territory, issued by the state regulator (for example the police licensing body in several states). The classes and names differ by state. Cabling that connects to the telecommunications network needs ACMA cabling registration. Check C11 and confirm with your state regulator before taking paid work.

Check yourself

A 12 V relay coil measures 360 Ω. You drive it from a 5 V pin through a BC547. Choose the base resistor.

IC = 12 / 360 ≈ 33 mA. For hard saturation, IB = IC / 10 ≈ 3.3 mA. RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.0033 ≈ 1.3 kΩ. Round down to 1.2 kΩ (≈ 3.6 mA). Fit a 1N4007 across the coil, band to +12 V.

Why do we design for IB = IC/10 when the BC547 has a gain of 110 to 800?

Because the gain varies hugely from part to part and with temperature and current. Designing for exactly IC/hFE would leave a low-gain part partly on, with volts across it and heat in it. Overdriving the base guarantees saturation (VCE ≈ 0.1–0.2 V) with any part from the batch.

A MOSFET's datasheet says VGS(th) = 2 V. Is it fully on with a 3.3 V gate?

Not necessarily. Threshold is where the MOSFET just starts to conduct (often defined at 250 µA). Look for RDS(on) specified at VGS = 2.5 V or 4.5 V, or read the output characteristic graph at 3.3 V. Only then do you know whether it is fully on at your current.

A MOSFET with RDS(on) = 25 mΩ switches 4 A. How much heat, and does it need a heatsink in TO-220?

P = I² × R = 16 × 0.025 = 0.4 W. At about 62 °C/W for a bare TO-220, the rise is about 25 °C, so roughly 50 °C at room temperature: fine without a heatsink, though warm. Note RDS(on) rises when the part is hot, so leave margin.

What does the flyback diode do, and which way round does it go?

It gives the coil's current a path when the transistor turns off, so the coil's stored energy circulates and dies away harmlessly instead of forcing the collector voltage to tens or hundreds of volts. It goes across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation: cathode (band) to the positive supply, anode to the transistor side.

What is a "dry contact" output on a BMS controller?

A relay contact that provides no voltage itself: it just opens or closes the connection between two terminals. The equipment on the other side supplies the voltage and current. You must still respect the contact's voltage and current rating.

A fail-safe maglock must release when power fails. Which relay contact do you wire it to, and why?

NC (normally closed) with the lock supply on COM. When the controller is idle, the relay is off, NC is closed, and the maglock is powered and holding. To let someone through, the controller energises the relay, NC opens, and the lock releases. If the controller or supply fails, the lock is unpowered and releases: that is "fail-safe". Fire release and egress requirements for such doors come from the NCC, standards and the site's fire design.

Your relay module turns on when you set the pin LOW and off when you set it HIGH. Is it broken?

No. Many relay modules are "active low": the input drives an optocoupler LED or a PNP stage from the module's own 5 V, so pulling the input to 0 V turns the relay on. Write your code (or wiring logic) to match, and make sure the relay stays off during start-up.

  • I can calculate a BJT base resistor using IB ≈ IC/10 and round it the safe way.
  • I can measure VCE(sat) and tell saturation from the active region by the readings.
  • I can choose a logic-level MOSFET by reading RDS(on) at my gate voltage, not VGS(th).
