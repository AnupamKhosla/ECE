Licences, tickets and pathways in Australia

Australian technical work runs on "tickets": short certificates and registrations that prove you are allowed and trained to do a task. Knowing which ones each role needs, and which to get first, saves you months and money.

Read 25 minLevel EssentialApplies to every role in the job track
Read this first

Licensing in Australia is split between the federal government (telecommunications cabling, migration) and the states and territories (electrical, security, fire, work health and safety). Rules, fees and course names change. Everything here is a map to help you ask the right questions; confirm each requirement with the named regulator before you pay for anything. For visas, talk to a registered migration agent or the Department of Home Affairs, not a forum.

Three kinds of permission

It helps to sort every requirement into one of three boxes. Each box has a different authority behind it.

The pathway at a glance

Most people in this track follow the same shape: sort out the right to work, collect the cheap tickets, pick one role family, get that role's licence or registration (often with an employer's help), and start earning while learning. The electrical licence is a separate, longer road you can join later.

Order of attack

White Card first (cheap, nearly universal, shows you are serious). Then the driver's licence conversion if you need it. Then first aid. Leave heights, EWP and confined spaces until an employer asks; many will pay for them.

Occupational licences, role by role

Telecommunications and data cabling: ACMA cabling registration

In Australia, customer cabling that connects (or will connect) to a telecommunications network must be installed by a registered cabler, or by someone directly supervised by one. This is federal law, administered by the Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) through the Cabling Provider Rules. You do not register with ACMA directly; you register through an ACMA-accredited registrar after doing an approved course.

  • Registration types: open (general cabling work; the one installers want), restricted (very limited, e.g. simple plug-in work at home), and lift (lift installations).
  • Endorsements add skills to an open registration. Commonly mentioned ones include structured cabling, optical fibre, coaxial cabling, aerial and underground cabling. Many employers want structured cabling and optical fibre.
  • The standard you work to is the Wiring Rules for customer cabling, AS/CA S009, and after work you give the customer a compliance form (the TCA1 form).
  • Time and cost: an open registration course is often about a week of training, with endorsements as extra short courses. Check current course prices with RTOs.

See C07 · Telecom cabling in Australia for the detail. Confirm current registration types and endorsements on ACMA's website and with a registrar.

Security installing: state security licences

Installing, maintaining or selling security equipment (CCTV, access control, intruder alarms, sometimes locks and safes) is licensed by each state or territory. The licence usually needs an approved course, a criminal history check, identity documents and often proof of the right to work. Examples of the regulators:

The usual training is a Certificate II or III in technical/electronic security (course names change; ask the regulator which qualification its licence class requires). Many employers hire trainees and support the licence application. Some states let you work under supervision while the licence is processed, others do not; check.

Electrical licence: the big one

Any work on low voltage (above 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC) installations must be done by a licensed electrician. This is state law, and penalties are serious. The normal path:

  1. A four-year apprenticeship with an employer, studying the Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician at the same time.
  2. A final practical assessment (the "capstone") and then a state-issued licence.

If you have overseas electrical qualifications and experience, there is a skills recognition route: Trades Recognition Australia (TRA) runs assessments for trades, and for electricians the route usually involves an Offshore Technical Skills Record (OTSR) followed by a provisional licence and gap training in your state. A diploma in ECE is not the same as an electrician qualification, so this route depends on your actual trade experience. Check TRA and your state's electrical regulator.

Common misconception

"I can't work in electronics without an electrical licence." Wrong for this job track. Cabling, CCTV, access control, intruder alarms, much of BMS and much of NBN work is extra-low voltage. You need the right other licences (ACMA, security), not an electrical licence. An electrical licence does open more doors later, especially in BMS (wiring controllers and mechanical switchboards), solar/battery installation and industrial instrumentation, which is why some people do both.

Fire systems

Fire detection work is licensed in some states (for example, New South Wales has a Fire Protection Accreditation Scheme, and Queensland licenses fire protection work through the Queensland Building and Construction Commission). Entry roles are often "fire alarm technician trainee", with a Certificate II/III in fire protection completed on the job. See C04 and check your state.

Refrigerant handling (ARC licence)

Anyone who handles refrigerant (for example, connecting gauges to an air conditioner) needs a national refrigerant handling licence, administered by the Australian Refrigeration Council. BMS technicians generally do not need it because they work on controls, not the refrigerant circuit. Know it exists so you never "just help" with gas work.

Solar and batteries

Designing and installing grid-connected solar and battery systems generally needs an electrical licence plus industry accreditation. Without an electrical licence, people still work as installer assistants, panel mounters, monitoring and support technicians. Check current accreditation rules with the industry scheme and your state.

Training: certificates and where your diploma fits

Australian vocational qualifications follow the Australian Qualifications Framework. Certificates II–IV and Diplomas are delivered by TAFE institutes and private RTOs.

LevelTypical use in this job track
Certificate IIEntry courses, e.g. telecommunications cabling (often bundled with the ACMA open registration).
Certificate IIIThe trade level: electrician, technical security, fire protection, instrumentation trades. Usually done during employment as an apprentice or trainee.
Certificate IVAdvanced technician and supervisor level, e.g. instrumentation and control, security systems design.
Diploma / Advanced DiplomaPara-professional engineering technician level, e.g. electronics and communications engineering.

Your old ECE diploma. It may be recognised for some purposes, and it helps you learn fast, but on its own it is rarely what an installer job ad asks for. Two ways it can help:

  • Recognition of prior learning (RPL). RTOs can assess what you already know and give credit toward a certificate, which saves time and money. Ask about RPL when you enrol.
  • Skills assessment for migration. If you need a skilled visa, the occupation you nominate decides which assessing authority looks at your qualification and experience (for example, Engineers Australia assesses engineering occupations including "engineering associate" roles that fit diploma holders; Trades Recognition Australia assesses many trades). Which occupations are eligible for which visas changes over time.

If you are not yet in Australia

This course cannot give migration advice, and you should not rely on forums either. The honest outline:

  1. Work out your right to work. Check the Department of Home Affairs website for the visa types that fit your age, skills and situation. If you are serious, pay for one consultation with a registered migration agent; they are regulated, and the advice is specific to you.
  2. Pick your occupation carefully. The occupation you are assessed in should match both your real experience and the job you will do. An assessment as an electronics technician and a job as a CCTV installer can fit; a mismatch causes problems later.
  3. Build the evidence now. Payslips, reference letters on company letterhead with duties and dates, and your portfolio projects (C12) all help, both for assessment and for employers.
  4. Do what you can from overseas. Learn the systems in this track, build the projects, and study Australian standards and job ads. The White Card and state licences are done once you are in the state.

Pay: how to find real numbers

This course does not quote pay figures because they change with state, experience, overtime, awards and the boom-and-bust of construction. Use these instead:

  • Fair Work Ombudsman pay guides and the Pay Calculator show the legal minimum rates under the relevant modern award (electrical/electronic/communications contracting work has its own award).
  • Job ads on major job boards often show salary bands. Collect 20 ads for your target role in your target city and note the range, tickets and tools; this is your personal market research.
  • Ask in interviews what the award or enterprise agreement is, whether there is a van, tool allowance and overtime. These change take-home pay a lot.
On the job

Keep scans of every ticket on your phone and the cards in your wallet. Site inductions start with "show me your White Card", and a missing card can mean being sent home unpaid for the day.

Check yourself

Who regulates telecommunications cabling registration, and is it state or federal?

The Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA), a federal authority. You register through an ACMA-accredited registrar after completing approved training, and you work to AS/CA S009.

You hold an ACMA open registration. Can you install CCTV for a client in NSW?

Not on that alone. The cabling registration covers telecommunications cabling. Installing security equipment is separately licensed by the state; in NSW that is the Security Licensing & Enforcement Directorate. Many CCTV installers hold both.

Which is the most useful first ticket for almost every role in this track, and why?

The White Card. It is legally required for construction work in every state, it is cheap and quick, and without it you cannot go onto most job sites.

Does a BMS technician need an ARC refrigerant licence?

Usually not, because BMS work is on controls, sensors and networks, not the refrigerant circuit. Anyone who handles refrigerant does need one, so a BMS tech should never connect gauges or "just help" with gas work.

Why is "I'll get an electrical licence first, then look for work" often the slow path for this track?

Because the electrical licence normally takes a four-year apprenticeship (or a skills-recognition route that needs real electrician experience), while cabling, security and much BMS work only needs ELV tickets that take days to weeks. You can earn and learn in ELV work first, and add an electrical licence later if your career needs it.

Where do you find legal minimum pay rates for a role?

The Fair Work Ombudsman's pay guides and Pay Calculator, under the relevant modern award. Real offers are often above the award, so compare with job ads too.
