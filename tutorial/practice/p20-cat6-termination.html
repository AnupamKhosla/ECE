Cat6 termination and testing

You will understand what is inside a Cat6 cable and why it is twisted, terminate RJ45 plugs, keystone jacks and a patch panel to T568A or T568B, test your work, and recognise every common fault — including the split pair that fools a cheap tester.

Safety

Data cable is ELV. The hazards are physical: sharp conductor offcuts in your eyes (wear safety glasses when trimming and punching), cuts from strippers, and — on real sites — ceilings, ladders, asbestos in older buildings, and nearby mains cables you must never touch or move. Keep your bench practice away from mains wiring entirely.

Inside the cable

A Cat6 cable has 8 copper conductors arranged as 4 twisted pairs. Each pair has a solid-colour wire and a white wire with a stripe of the same colour: blue, orange, green and brown. Cat6 usually adds a plastic spline (cross-shaped separator) that keeps the pairs apart, and a rip cord to help open the jacket.

Why twisting cancels noise

Ethernet sends each signal as a differential pair: one wire carries +V while its partner carries −V, and the receiver measures only the difference between them. Interference from a nearby cable or motor induces a voltage — call it n — in both wires. If both wires pick up the same n, the difference is unchanged and the noise vanishes. Twisting is what makes the pick-up equal: each half-twist swaps which wire is closer to the noise source, so over a length of cable both wires see the same average field. Different pairs use different twist rates so they do not line up with each other and cross-couple.

(V+ + n) − (V− + n) = V+ − V− The receiver subtracts. Noise that is equal on both wires (common-mode) cancels. Untwisting, or putting the two halves of a signal on wires from different pairs, makes n unequal — and it no longer cancels.
Why it works

Induced noise comes from changing magnetic flux through the loop formed by the two wires (Faraday's law). A twist reverses the loop's orientation, so the voltage induced in one twist-loop is opposite to that in the next and they cancel along the cable. Tight, even twists right up to the termination keep the loops small and balanced; that is why the standard limits untwisting at the jack.

Solid vs stranded, and the categories

Solid conductors (one thick copper wire each) are for installed cable in walls and ceilings — lower loss, and they terminate properly in punch-down jacks. Stranded conductors (many fine wires) are for patch cords — flexible, but higher loss, so they are limited in length. Use plugs made for the conductor type: solid-wire plugs have contacts that straddle the wire; stranded plugs pierce it.

T568A and T568B

The RJ45 connector has 8 pins. Two standard colour orders exist: T568A and T568B. They differ only in that the orange and green pairs swap places. Electrically both are equally good. The golden rule is: use the same scheme at both ends of every link, and the scheme the site specification calls for. In Australia you will see both; some clients insist on one, so always check the spec.

Memory trick: in both schemes the white wire always comes first in a pair (odd pins 1, 3, 5, 7), except that the blue pair sits in the middle "backwards" (blue on 4, white/blue on 5). For T568B think "Orange first"; for T568A, "Green first". The two ends of a T568A cable and the two ends of a T568B cable connect identically pin for pin (1 to 1 … 8 to 8): a straight-through cable. A cable with A at one end and B at the other is a crossover, an old way of joining two PCs directly; it fails a wire-map test in structured cabling.

Which pins carry data? 10 and 100 Mb/s Ethernet use only pins 1, 2, 3 and 6 (two pairs). Gigabit and faster use all four pairs. PoE uses two or four pairs. So a cable with one broken pair may "work" at 100 Mb/s and then fail when someone plugs in a gigabit device or a PoE+ camera — always terminate and test all eight.

Terminate an RJ45 plug

Two kinds of Cat6 plug are common. A standard plug often comes with a load bar (a small insert that holds the wires in order) and you trim the wires before inserting. A pass-through plug lets the wires come out the front so you can check the order, and the matching crimper trims them flush as it crimps. Pass-through is easier for beginners; standard plugs are preferred by some installers because no trimmed copper can be left proud at the tip.

  1. Strip about 30–40 mm of jacket with a rotary stripper. Do not nick the conductor insulation: bend the cable to check — any copper showing means cut it off and start again.

  2. Remove the spline and rip cord flush with the jacket using flush cutters.

  3. Untwist each pair only as much as needed and straighten the wires by running them between your fingers. Arrange them in order: T568B = W/O, O, W/G, Bl, W/Bl, G, W/Br, Br. Note how green crosses over the blue pair.

  4. Load bar / trim. Feed the wires into the load bar in order, slide it right down to the jacket, then trim straight across leaving about 12–14 mm of conductor past the jacket (for pass-through plugs, skip the trim).

  5. Insert into the plug, clip facing away, contacts towards you. Push until every conductor touches the end of the plug (look through the clear tip: eight copper dots) and the jacket sits under the strain-relief wedge. Check the colour order again through the plastic.

  6. Crimp firmly in one full squeeze. For pass-through, check the trimmed ends are flush. Tug the cable gently: the plug must not move.

  7. Do the other end in the same scheme, then test (below) before you call it done.

Bench tip

Keep the twists right up to the plug or jack: no more than about 13 mm untwisted at a Cat5e/Cat6 termination. The most common rejected terminations are too much untwist, jacket not inside the plug (the wires then take all the strain and break), and conductors not fully home (intermittent faults that come and go with temperature). Make ten patch leads and test every one.

Keystone jacks and patch panels (110 punch-down)

Installed cabling does not end in plugs. It ends in jacks: a keystone jack in a wall plate at the user's end, and a port on a patch panel in the comms rack at the other. Both use IDC (insulation displacement contact) slots. You push each conductor into a forked metal slot with a 110 punch-down tool; the fork slices through the insulation and grips the copper, and the tool's blade trims the waste in the same stroke.

  1. Strip 25–50 mm of jacket and remove the spline. Keep pairs twisted.

  2. Read the jack's colour label. Each jack is printed with both A and B colour codes; follow the row for your scheme. The pin order on the jack is not left-to-right 1–8 — the label tells you where each colour goes, so trust the label.

  3. Lay each pair into its slots with the twist kept to within ~13 mm of the IDC, and the jacket close to the back of the jack.

  4. Punch with the tool square to the jack and the cutting side facing the waste end (outwards). A firm click seats and trims the wire. Check each one is level and fully down.

  5. Fit the cap or strain relief, then label the jack and the matching panel port before you forget which cable is which.

A patch panel is the same job repeated 24 or 48 times along the back of the panel, with cables dressed neatly in from the side or rear and supported by a cable management bar. At the panel front, short patch cords connect each port to the switch.

Permanent link and channel

Installation practice

  • Bend radius: no tighter than about 4 × the cable's outside diameter once installed (Cat6 at ~6 mm → at least ~25 mm radius), and gentler while pulling. Kinks change the pair geometry permanently.
  • Pull tension: no more than about 110 N (25 lbf) on a 4-pair cable. Pull by hand, not with a vehicle or a power tool, and pull from the box/reel straight without twisting it.
  • No crushing: do not staple, over-tighten cable ties or let cables be walked on. Use hook-and-loop (Velcro-type) ties in bundles and catenary/cable tray supports.
  • Keep away from power: do not share a conduit, duct compartment or cable tie with low-voltage (230 V) power cables, cross them at right angles, and keep the separation and barriers required by the Australian wiring rules for customer cabling (AS/CA S009). Learn the exact distances in your cabling course.
  • Leave a service loop (a little spare) at the rack and outlet so the cable can be re-terminated once.
  • Label both ends with the same ID, e.g. outlet L2-024 ↔ panel port PP2-24, and record it in the cable schedule. The Australian standard for cabling administration and labelling is AS/NZS 3085.1; sites often have their own scheme on top.
In Australia

Cabling connected to the telecommunications network (and in practice almost every building LAN is, because it connects to the internet service) must be installed by an ACMA-registered cabler — registered through an ACMA-accredited registrar, with the right type of registration and endorsements (structured cabling for this work), and following AS/CA S009. Your bench patch leads are fine for learning; fixed cabling in a building, even your own home, is not DIY. Read C07 Telecom cabling in Australia and confirm the current requirements with the ACMA, as rules change.

Testers: continuity vs certification

Wire-map / continuity tester (~A$20–150)

A main unit and a remote. It sends a signal down each conductor in turn and lights the matching LED on the remote, 1 to 8. It finds opens, shorts, reversed pairs, crossed pairs and miswires. Better ones show length to a fault and detect split pairs by measuring crosstalk; basic ones cannot see a split pair.

Certification tester (thousands to tens of thousands of A$, indicative)

Measures performance across the full frequency range against the Cat6 limits of the standards: wire map, length, insertion loss, NEXT (near-end crosstalk), return loss, delay and more. Gives a PASS/FAIL report per link that the cable manufacturer needs before it will issue a long system warranty. Usually owned by the company and used by trained techs.

Between them sit qualification testers, which answer "will this link run 1 Gb/s (or PoE)?" without full certification — common in service work.

Why a split pair fools a simple tester

A continuity tester only asks "is pin 3 at this end connected to pin 3 at the other end?" With a split pair, it is. The tester cannot know which wires are twisted together inside the jacket. But Ethernet sends one signal on pins 3 and 6 as a differential pair. If pin 3 is white/green and pin 6 is blue (from the blue pair), the signal's two halves travel on wires that are not twisted with each other. The noise-cancelling of Figure 2 is lost, and worse, each half sits twisted tightly against a wire of another signal, so they couple strongly: huge near-end crosstalk. At 10 Mb/s on a short cable you may get away with it; at 1 Gb/s over 50 m you get errors, retries, and a link that "works but is slow" or drops. A certification tester fails it on NEXT straight away.

The classic way to make a split pair is to wire the plug in "neat" colour order — W/O, O, W/G, G, W/Bl, Bl, W/Br, Br — identically at both ends. Every LED lights in order, yet pins 3 and 6 now carry white/green and blue.

Common misconception

"The tester lit 1 to 8, so the cable is good." It only proves continuity and order. It does not prove the pairs are twisted correctly, the untwist is short, the cable is not kinked, or that it meets Cat6 performance. That is why commercial installs are certified, and why you should terminate to the standard every single time instead of trusting the green lights.

On the job

Terminating and testing Cat6 is a daily task for data cablers, CCTV and access-control installers, AV techs and many BMS techs. Employers test this at interview or on the first day: "make me a T568B patch lead" or "punch down this keystone". Speed comes with practice, but neatness and correct colour order are non-negotiable. Labelling and a cable schedule at handover are what separate a tradesperson from a hobbyist. See C07 and C05.

Check yourself

Write the T568B order from pin 1 to pin 8.

White/orange, orange, white/green, blue, white/blue, green, white/brown, brown. T568A swaps the orange and green pairs: white/green, green, white/orange, blue, white/blue, orange, white/brown, brown.

Which pins form the pair that is "split around" the blue pair, and why does that matter when terminating?

Pins 3 and 6. In T568B that is white/green (3) and green (6). If you lay wires in "neat" pairs (3–4 green, 5–6 blue) you create a split pair. Always lay green (T568B) or orange (T568A) across the blue pair.

Why does a split pair pass a continuity test but fail at gigabit speed?

Continuity only checks pin-to-pin connections, which are correct. But a differential signal must travel on the two wires of one twisted pair to cancel noise and limit crosstalk. With a split pair it travels on wires from two different pairs, so it couples into neighbouring signals (high NEXT) and picks up noise unequally. At high frequencies this causes errors; a certifier fails it on NEXT.

A run from patch panel to outlet is 87 m. The equipment cord is 3 m and the work-area cord is 5 m. Is the channel within limits?

Yes: permanent link 87 m ≤ 90 m, cords 3 + 5 = 8 m ≤ 10 m, channel 95 m ≤ 100 m. A 12 m work-area cord would break the 10 m cord allowance even though the total is 102 m — fails both ways.

You use stranded patch cable with plugs designed for solid conductors. What can go wrong?

Solid-conductor contacts straddle a single wire; on stranded wire they may not pierce reliably, giving intermittent opens. Use plugs rated for the conductor type (many Cat6 plugs are rated for both — check).

The tester shows 2, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 on the remote. Name the fault and the fix.

Reversed pair on pins 1/2: white/orange and orange are swapped at one end. Re-terminate that end in the correct order.

Why must installed Cat6 not be bent tighter than about 4 × its diameter?

Tight bends deform the pair geometry — twists open up, the spline shifts and pair spacing changes — which raises crosstalk and return loss. The damage stays after you straighten it. For ~6 mm Cat6 keep the radius above ~25 mm.

Can you install Cat6 in a client's office in Australia if you are not registered?

Not for fixed cabling connected to the telecommunications network — that needs an ACMA-registered cabler with the appropriate registration, working to AS/CA S009. You can work under the supervision of a registered cabler while you train; check the ACMA's current rules for what supervision allows.
