Fault-finding: a technician's troubleshooting method

Learn the step-by-step method that good technicians use to find faults fast: understand normal, gather symptoms, check the simple things, measure instead of guessing, half-split, change one thing at a time, verify and document. Then practise it on seven real faults from CCTV, Arduino, Modbus, 4–20 mA, networking and access control, with the readings you would actually see.

Safety

Everything in this chapter is extra-low voltage (ELV): 5 V, 12 V, 24 V DC and AC plug-packs. When a fault leads you to the mains side of a power supply (no output, PSU dead, a hard-wired 230 V supply), stop. Swapping a plug-pack is fine; opening, wiring or measuring anything at 230 V is licensed electrician work in Australia. Always switch off before you measure resistance or continuity: an ohmmeter on a live circuit gives wrong readings and can blow the meter's fuse.

Why a method beats guessing

A beginner sees a dead camera and swaps the camera. Then the power supply. Then the cable. Two hours and A$400 later, the camera works, and nobody knows why. Next month it fails again.

An experienced technician walks up to the same camera, measures the voltage at the camera while it is running, sees 9.6 V instead of 12 V, and knows within five minutes that the cable is too long and too thin. That is not talent. It is a method: a fixed order of questions that narrows the fault down quickly, using measurements instead of hope.

Common misconception

"Fault-finding is about knowing every fault." Nobody knows every fault. Good technicians fix equipment they have never seen before, because the method works on anything that has an input, a path and an output. Knowing common faults makes you faster, but the method is what makes you right.

The method, step by step

  1. Understand normal. You cannot spot "wrong" until you know "right". What should the system do? What voltage should be at each point? What should the LEDs show? Read the drawing (see P22), the manual and the commissioning sheet. On a working site, measure a working camera or door next to the faulty one: that is your "normal".

  2. Gather symptoms. Ask the person who reported it: what exactly happens, when did it start, does it happen all the time or only sometimes, did anything change (new equipment, storm, painters, a software update)? Look for burnt smells, water, damaged cable, lights that are off. Read the event log on the NVR, controller or BMS. "Fails every night at dusk" is worth more than any measurement.

  3. Check the simple things. Power (is it on, is the fuse good, is the breaker on — the electrician checks the mains side), connections (is it plugged in, are the screw terminals tight, is the right cable in the right port), and settings (address, baud rate, IP address, input type). Most faults are here. Check them first, even when you are sure, because they are fast.

  4. Measure, don't guess. Use the meter to compare real readings with the normal ones from step 1. Measure voltage under load (with the device connected and running), because a bad joint can show full voltage with no current and collapse when current flows.

  5. Half-split the signal path. Instead of starting at one end, test in the middle of the chain. If it is good there, the fault is after that point; if bad, before it. Each test removes half of the suspects.

  6. Change one thing at a time. Then test again. If you change three things at once and it starts working, you do not know which one fixed it, and you may have added a new fault.

  7. Verify the fix. Check the readings are normal, the whole function works (not just "the light came on"), and the conditions that caused the fault are tested (if it failed at night, test with the infrared forced on). Ask yourself: do I know why it failed? A fuse you replaced without finding why it blew will blow again.

  8. Document. Write what you found, what you measured, what you did and what is left. The section at the end of this chapter shows how.

Measuring like a technician

Three meter habits do most of the work in fault-finding.

1. Voltage across a part tells you about current

By Ohm's law, the voltage across a part is V = I × R. That gives you four rules you will use every day:

You measure across…ReadingMeaning
A closed switch, good fuse, or good joint (current flowing)≈ 0 V (a few mV)Normal. Very low resistance, so almost no voltage.
A fuse, switch or joint in a working circuitFull supply voltageOpen circuit there. Blown fuse, open switch, broken joint. No current flows, so no other part drops any voltage, and the whole supply appears across the gap.
A contact or jointSome volts (e.g. 0.5–2 V)A high-resistance joint: corroded, loose or burnt. It gets hot and steals voltage from the load.
The load (lamp, camera, coil)Much less than supplyVoltage is being lost somewhere in the path. Find where by measuring along it.
Why it works

Kirchhoff's voltage law says the voltages around a loop add up to the supply voltage. If one part is open (infinite resistance), no current flows, every other part drops I × R = 0, so the whole supply must appear across the open part. That is why a blown fuse in a connected circuit reads the full supply voltage across it.

2. Measure under load

A cable with one strand left in a screw terminal still shows 12.3 V with nothing connected, because the meter draws almost no current (its input resistance is about 10 MΩ). Connect the camera and that one strand cannot carry 0.5 A without dropping volts. Always measure with the device connected and working, and at the device's own terminals.

3. Know your reference point

"There's 12 V at the camera" is meaningless unless you know where the black probe was. Measure across the device (red on +, black on − at the device). Measuring from the device + back to the PSU − includes the volt drop in the return wire too, and hides half the problem.

Bench tip

Write readings down as you go, with where the probes were: "C7 +/− at camera, running: 11.1 V". After four measurements you will not remember which was which, and the note becomes your job record for free.

Half-splitting the signal path

Most systems are a chain: power or signal goes in at one end, passes through several blocks, and comes out at the other. If the output is wrong, one block (or one link between blocks) is faulty. You could test each block from the start. Half-splitting is faster: test in the middle.

Why it is fast. With 8 suspect blocks, testing one by one can take up to 7 tests. Half-splitting takes 3: 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. With 16 blocks it takes 4. Each test doubles how many blocks you can search (2 tests → 4 blocks, 3 → 8, 4 → 16). The more complex the system, the bigger the saving.

Choose the split point sensibly. The best split point is not always the exact middle. Pick a point that is easy to reach (a plant room, a patch panel, a junction box) and that splits the likely causes in half. Field cables and connectors fail far more often than controllers, so weight your choice towards them.

On the job

Half-splitting is how network technicians work too. A PC cannot reach the internet: can it reach its own gateway? (ping the router.) Yes → the problem is beyond the router. No → is the link light on at the switch? Each question cuts the suspects in half. See P19.

Fault 1: a dead (or rebooting) 12 V camera

Symptom. Camera C7 at the far end of a car park shows "video loss" on the NVR. Sometimes it is dead all the time; sometimes it works by day and reboots every night.

The fix for volt drop. Make the cable resistance smaller or the current smaller. Options: run thicker cable (1.0 mm² halves the resistance), move the PSU closer to the camera, feed the camera from PoE over the data cable (PoE runs at around 48 V, so the same power needs about a quarter of the current; see C05), or use a 24 V AC camera model if the job allows. Turning the PSU up to 13.5 V is a weak fix: the daytime voltage then rises and the problem returns when the cable warms or the joints age.

Try this

Build Figure 3 on the bench. 12 V plug-pack → 20 m of thin figure-8 cable (go out and back on the reel) → a 12 V 5 W lamp or a 27 Ω 10 W resistor. Measure at the plug-pack and at the load, both under load. Thin 7/0.2 cable (about 0.22 mm²) has about 0.08 Ω per metre per core, so 40 m of conductor is about 3 Ω and you should lose roughly 1.2–1.5 V. Now unplug the load and measure at the far end again: the "missing" volts come back. That is why you measure under load.

Fault 3: an Arduino input that reads random values

Symptom. A push button is wired between pin 2 and 5 V. With the button not pressed, the serial monitor prints a random mix of 0s and 1s. It changes when you move your hand near the wire. Sometimes the button "works", sometimes not.

Measurement. Check the code: pinMode(2, INPUT);. Check the wiring: there is no resistor from pin 2 to anything. With the button released, pin 2 is connected to nothing. A microcontroller input has a very high resistance (megohms), so the tiny charge picked up from mains wiring and your body is enough to swing it between HIGH and LOW. This is a floating input.

Watch for a strange meter effect. When you put the meter on pin 2, it may read a steady 0 V, and the random readings stop. The meter's own 10 MΩ input acts as a weak pull-down resistor. A fault that disappears when you measure it is a strong clue that something is floating.

Fix. Give the input a defined level when the button is open. Either wire the button from pin 2 to GND and use pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP); (released reads 1, pressed reads 0: invert your logic), or keep the button to 5 V and add a 10 kΩ resistor from pin 2 to GND (a pull-down). For a door contact on a long cable, use a stronger external pull-up (1–4.7 kΩ) and a 100 nF capacitor to GND to reject noise, and debounce in software.

Fault 5: a 4–20 mA loop reading 0 or stuck at 20+ mA

A 4–20 mA loop sends a measurement as a current: 4 mA means the bottom of the range, 20 mA the top. Current is the same everywhere in a series loop, so cable resistance does not change the reading, and 0 mA can only mean a fault (a "live zero"). The controller usually reads it as the voltage across a 250 Ω resistor: 4 mA → 1 V, 20 mA → 5 V.

Reads 0 mA / under-range alarmAcross 250 Ω0 VNo current: an open circuit somewhere in the loop.

At the transmitter terminals0 VBreak before the transmitter: loop fuse, loose terminal, broken core. Half-split: measure at the junction box halfway.
Normal checkAcross 250 Ω3.0 V3.0 / 250 = 12 mA = (12 − 4) / 16 = 50% of range. For a 0–100% level transmitter, 50%.
Stuck at 21–23 mAAcross 250 Ωabout 5.3–5.8 VMany transmitters drive the loop above 20.5 mA (NAMUR NE43 uses ≥ 21 mA) to signal "sensor fault". Check the sensor: an open RTD or a disconnected probe. Or the process really is over range.
Very high, e.g. 60–96 mAAt the transmitter terminals≈ 0 VA short across the transmitter (or across the cable). Current is limited only by the 250 Ω: 24 / 250 = 96 mA, which can overheat the input. Find the short (water in a junction box, crushed cable).
Bench tip

To read loop current without opening the loop, measure the voltage across the 250 Ω and divide by 250. Many field transmitters also have TEST terminals for a meter on mA, and a milliamp clamp meter can read it around one core. Opening a live loop to insert a meter makes the controller see 0 mA and may trip alarms or move valves: tell the operator first.

Fault 6: a network camera you can't reach

Symptom. A new IP camera is plugged into a PoE switch. Its link light is on, and it powers up. But typing its address into the browser gives nothing.

CheckFindingMeaning
Switch port lights and PoE powerLink on, switch shows 5 W drawnCable and power are fine. Not a physical fault.
Laptop address (ipconfig on Windows, ip addr on Linux)192.168.0.25, mask 255.255.255.0 (/24)The laptop can talk directly only to 192.168.0.1 – 192.168.0.254.
Camera's default address (manual or label)192.168.1.64 /24Different subnet. With a /24 mask the first three numbers must match. 192.168.0 ≠ 192.168.1.
ping 192.168.1.64"Request timed out" or "Destination host unreachable"Confirms the laptop has no route to it.

Fix. Give the laptop a temporary address in the camera's subnet (for example 192.168.1.200 /24, added as a second address or set manually), ping the camera, log in, and set the camera to its planned address from the IP schedule (for example 192.168.0.51 /24 with the correct gateway). Then put the laptop back. The manufacturer's discovery tool can also find cameras on any subnet, because it uses broadcast messages.

Other causes with the same symptom. A duplicate IP (unplug the camera and ping again: if something still answers, another device owns that address). The switch port in the wrong VLAN. A camera set to DHCP on a network with no DHCP server, which gives itself a 169.254.x.x address.

Fault 7: a door that won't unlock

Symptom. A staff member badges their card. The reader beeps and shows green. The controller relay clicks. The door stays locked. The lock is a 12 V DC fail-secure electric strike (it needs power to unlock), powered from a 13.8 V battery-backed supply.

Measure (during an unlock)ReadingMeaning and fix
Across the strike terminals13.4 V, strike hums, door still lockedElectrically fine. The fault is mechanical: the door is pressing on the strike keeper ("preload": a warped door, a seal, wind pressure). Push the door closed while badging: if it then opens, adjust the keeper or door.
Across the strike9.0 VVolts lost on the way. Half-split the path: across the fuse (should be ≈ 0 V), across the relay COM–NO (should be ≈ 0 V; 2 V means burnt contacts), along the cable (thin or long cable, loose joint). Also check the PSU itself under load: a failing battery can pull it down.
Across the strike0 V, relay clicksOpen circuit after the relay: broken core, or wired to NC instead of NO (then it would be powered when it should not be).
Across the strike≈ 0.7–1 V, fuse blows or PSU shuts downFlyback diode backwards. It conducts forward and shorts the supply. Turn it round: band (cathode) to +.
Controller resets or relay contacts pitted after a few months—No flyback diode. Each release makes a voltage spike. Fit a 1N4007 across the coil, band to + (many strikes have one built in: check before adding).

The REX (request-to-exit) button. If the door unlocks by itself, stays unlocked, or will not unlock from the inside, check the exit button. Switch off and measure ohms across its terminals: "OL" released and ≈ 0 Ω pressed means it is normally open (NO); the reverse means normally closed (NC). The controller's REX input must be configured to match. If the input is set to NC but an NO button is wired, the controller sees "exit requested" all the time. On many sites the exit device for a maglock must also cut the lock's power directly, not only signal the controller: follow the design drawings and the site's egress requirements.

    Verify the fix, then write the job note

    Verify means three things. The readings are back to normal (write them down). The whole function works, end to end (the door unlocks and the event shows on the access control software; the camera records and the NVR shows it at night). And the condition that caused the fault has been tested (force the IR on; run the pump to fill the tank; badge ten times in a row).

    A job note (also called a service report or job card) is the written record of your visit. It goes to your supervisor, to the client, and to the next technician who opens that panel. A good note answers four questions:

    1. What I found: the fault and its root cause, in plain words.
    2. What I measured: the key readings, with units and where you measured.
    3. What I did: the repair, parts used, settings changed, and how you verified it.
    4. What's left: anything not fixed, risks, follow-up work, quotes needed.

    Bad job note

    C7 not working. Checked camera and power. Fixed. All OK now.

    Nobody can tell what was wrong, whether it will come back, what was changed, or whether the client should pay for more work. If C7 fails again, the next technician starts from zero.

    Good job note

    Job 4471 · Car park L2 · Camera C7 FOUND: C7 rebooting at dusk when IR turns on. Cause: volt drop on long thin feed. MEASURED: PSU 12.3 V on load.     On the job

    In Australia, job notes usually go into a phone app (the service management system) before you leave site, and the client often sees them. Write them as if the client, your boss and a lawyer will all read them: facts, readings and actions, no blame, no guesses presented as facts. A technician whose notes are clear gets trusted with bigger jobs.

    Common mistakes

    • Replacing parts before measuring. "Swap until it works" wastes money and hides the cause. Measure, then replace only what the readings point to.
    • Measuring with no load. A broken joint can show full voltage until current flows. Measure with the device connected and running.
    • Replacing a fuse without finding why it blew. A fuse is a symptom, not a cause. Look for the short or the overload first. Never fit a bigger fuse.
    • Changing several things at once. You lose track of what fixed it, and you may create a second fault.
    • Trusting labels. A, B, +, −, NO, NC and cable labels can all be wrong. Verify with the meter.
    • Measuring resistance on a live circuit. The reading is meaningless and the meter can be damaged. Switch off first.
    • Stopping at "it works now". Without the root cause, it will fail again. Test the condition that caused it.
    • Forgetting the operator. Opening a 4–20 mA loop or disabling a door can move valves, trip alarms or lock people in or out. Tell the site contact before you start, and follow the site's permits.

    Check yourself

    In a working 12 V circuit (load connected), you measure 12 V across a fuse. Is the fuse good?

    No, it is blown. A good fuse has almost no resistance, so it drops almost no voltage (a few mV). When it is open, no current flows, the load drops nothing, and the whole 12 V appears across the gap. Find the cause before you replace it.

    A chain has 8 blocks and one is faulty. How many measurements does half-splitting need, at most?

    Three. 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. Each measurement halves the suspects, so 2³ = 8 blocks need 3 tests. Testing one by one from the start can need 7.

    The PSU reads 12.4 V with the camera unplugged. Does that prove the power is fine?

    No. With no load, almost no current flows, so cable resistance and bad joints drop almost nothing. You must measure across the camera while it is running, ideally in its worst case (IR on).

    With the bus powered off, you read 40 Ω between A and B on an RS-485 bus. What is wrong?

    Three 120 Ω terminators are fitted (120 / 3 = 40 Ω). There should be exactly two, one at each end of the bus, giving 60 Ω. Turn off the termination on the device that is not at an end.

    A Modbus device replies "exception 02: illegal data address". Should you check the wiring?

    No. An exception reply proves the wiring, ID, baud rate and parity are all working: the device heard and answered. The register address is wrong. Check the register map (often an off-by-one: register 40001 is address 0).

    You read 2.0 V across the 250 Ω input of a 0–10 bar pressure transmitter. What is the pressure?

    I = 2.0 / 250 = 8 mA. Fraction of range = (8 − 4) / 16 = 0.25. Pressure = 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 bar.

    Your laptop is 10.1.1.20/24 and the camera is 10.1.2.30/24. Why can't you reach it?

    With a /24 mask the first three numbers must match. 10.1.1 and 10.1.2 are different subnets, so the laptop sends the traffic to its gateway instead of directly to the camera. Give the laptop a temporary 10.1.2.x address, or change the camera's address.

    Since a new strike was fitted, the lock supply fuse blows every time the door is unlocked. What is the first thing you check?

    The flyback diode across the strike. If it was fitted backwards, it is forward-biased when the relay closes and shorts the supply. The band (cathode) must go to +. Also check the strike's own wiring and its built-in diode before fitting another.

    Skills checklist

    • I can list the eight steps of the fault-finding method in order, and explain why "understand normal" comes first.
    • I measure voltage under load, across the device, and write down where my probes were.
    • I can tell an open circuit, a good joint and a high-resistance joint from the voltage across them.
    • I can half-split a signal chain and choose a sensible split point.
    • I can calculate cable volt drop and recognise it as the cause of a night-time camera reboot.
    • I can find a floating input and fix it with a pull-up or pull-down.
    • I can check a Modbus RS-485 bus: settings, unique IDs, 60 Ω termination, A/B, and timeout vs exception.
    • I can diagnose a 4–20 mA loop from the voltage across the 250 Ω input and at the transmitter.
    • I can find a wrong-subnet problem with ipconfig and ping and fix it.
    • I can check a door strike circuit under load, test a REX button, and fit a flyback diode the right way round.
    • I write job notes that say what I found, what I measured, what I did and what is left.
