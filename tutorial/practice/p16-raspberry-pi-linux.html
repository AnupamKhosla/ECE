Raspberry Pi and the Linux you need

By the end you will flash a Raspberry Pi with no screen or keyboard, log in to it over the network with SSH, move around a Linux system with confidence, read logs and services, check the network, and know which header pins are safe to touch.

Time 2–3 hLevel Beginner → IntermediateKit cost ~A$110–180Theory T15, T10

Computer or microcontroller?

In P11–P15 you used an Arduino. An Arduino board carries a microcontroller: one chip that holds a small processor, its program memory (flash), a little working memory (RAM) and the input/output hardware. It runs exactly one program, your sketch, from the moment power arrives. There is no operating system. When the sketch says "turn pin 13 on", the pin turns on within a microsecond, every time.

A Raspberry Pi is a single-board computer. Its main chip is a system on a chip (SoC): several fast processor cores, a graphics unit, USB, Ethernet and more. RAM is a separate chip. The program storage is a microSD card (or an SSD), and on it sits a full operating system: Linux. Linux runs many programs at once, called processes, and shares the processor between them thousands of times a second. That gives you networking, a file system, a web server, Python, databases and remote login — but it also means your program can be paused for a few milliseconds while Linux does something else.

Common misconception

"The Pi is just a faster Arduino." No. It is a different kind of machine. Use a Pi when the job is mostly software and networking (log, serve, talk to the cloud, run a broker). Use a microcontroller when the job is mostly pins and timing (count pulses, drive a stepper, wake every 10 minutes on a battery). Real building systems use both: small controllers at the plant, and a Linux box (a supervisor or gateway) that collects their data.

Why it works

A microcontroller executes instructions straight from flash in a fixed loop, so the time from "instruction" to "pin changes" is a known number of clock cycles. On the Pi, your Python program asks the Linux kernel to change the pin, and the kernel's scheduler decides when your process runs. That is why the Pi is excellent at "every minute, read the sensor and log it" but poor at "generate an exact 38 kHz signal".

Which Pi, and which power supply

Raspberry Pi 4 Model B

Quad-core 64-bit ARM, 1–8 GB RAM, Gigabit Ethernet, Wi-Fi, 2 × USB 3, 2 × micro-HDMI. Power: USB-C, 5.1 V 3 A (15.3 W) official supply. Still a great, cheaper bench machine.

Raspberry Pi 5

About two to three times faster than a Pi 4, with a power button, real-time clock connector and a PCIe connector for an SSD. Power: the official 27 W USB-C supply (5.1 V 5 A). On a 3 A supply it runs but limits USB current.

Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W

Tiny (65 × 30 mm), quad-core, 512 MB RAM, 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only, no Ethernet. Power: micro-USB, 5.1 V 2.5 A supply. Ideal for one sensor in a cupboard.

Safety

Use the official supply or another supply that carries the Australian RCM (Regulatory Compliance Mark). The plug-pack is the only part of this lab that touches 230 V mains, and it is sealed. Never open a plug-pack, never cut and rewire a mains lead, and never power the Pi from anything you built that connects to mains. Everything on the Pi side is 5 V and 3.3 V — extra-low voltage.

Why the power supply matters so much

The Pi needs 5 V at its input, but a cheap phone charger and a thin USB cable often deliver less when the Pi draws a burst of current. Every cable has resistance. A worked example: a thin 1 m cable might have 0.25 Ω total (out and back). At 2.5 A that drops V = I × R = 2.5 × 0.25 = 0.63 V, so a 5.0 V charger gives the Pi only about 4.4 V. Below about 4.63 V the Pi flags under-voltage and slows itself down. That is why the official supply is 5.1 V with a thick, short cable.

VPi = Vsupply − I × Rcable = 5.0 − 2.5 × 0.25 ≈ 4.4 V (too low) Rcable is the round-trip resistance of both conductors. The official 5.1 V supply with a heavier cable keeps the Pi above the 4.63 V warning threshold.

You can ask the Pi whether it has ever been short of power with vcgencmd get_throttled. throttled=0x0 means all good. Anything else is a bit-field: for example 0x50005 means "under-voltage right now, throttled right now, and both have happened since boot". Fix the supply before you trust any test result.

Flash the card with Raspberry Pi Imager

A headless Pi is one with no screen, keyboard or mouse. Most Pis in the field are headless: they sit in a cabinet and you reach them over the network. Raspberry Pi Imager lets you pre-set everything needed for that before the first boot.

  1. Install Imager on your laptop from raspberrypi.com (free, Windows/macOS/Linux).

  2. Choose device (Pi 4, Pi 5 or Zero 2 W), then choose OS. Pick Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit). For a headless logger, Raspberry Pi OS Lite (64-bit) is even better: no desktop, less to update, faster boot. Zero 2 W: Lite only.

  3. Choose storage: your microSD card. Check the size shown — you do not want to wipe your laptop's backup drive.

  4. OS customisation (Imager asks "Would you like to apply OS customisation settings?" → Edit settings). Set: hostname plantpi; username tech and a strong password; wireless LAN SSID and password; wireless LAN country: AU; locale/time zone (e.g. Australia/Sydney) and keyboard us; on the Services tab Enable SSH → "Use password authentication" for now.

  5. Write, wait for verification, then put the card in the Pi. Plug in Ethernet (if you have it), then power. The first boot takes 1–2 minutes because the Pi expands the file system and applies your settings. The green activity LED flickers while it works.

Bench tip

There is no default pi user with password raspberry any more (that changed in 2022). If you skip the customisation step on a headless Pi, you cannot log in. Re-flash with settings — it is quicker than troubleshooting.

In Australia

Set the Wi-Fi country to AU. It selects the Australian radio rules (allowed channels and power), which are set by the ACMA. A wrong or missing country code can stop 5 GHz Wi-Fi from working at all.

Log in headless with SSH

SSH (Secure Shell) gives you an encrypted text terminal on another machine over the network, on TCP port 22. Windows 10/11, macOS and Linux all have an ssh client built in: open PowerShell (Windows) or Terminal (macOS) and type:

ssh tech@plantpi.local

tech is the user, plantpi.local is the host. The .local ending uses mDNS (multicast DNS): the Pi announces its own name on the local network, so you do not need to know its IP address. The first time, SSH shows a "fingerprint" and asks if you trust it. Type yes, then the password (nothing appears while you type — that is normal).

The authenticity of host 'plantpi.local (192.168.1.42)' can't be established.
ED25519 key fingerprint is SHA256:Xq3v0m9...kQ.
Are you sure you want to continue connecting (yes/no/[fingerprint])? yes
tech@plantpi.local's password:
Linux plantpi 6.12.x-v8+ #1 SMP PREEMPT aarch64
...
tech@plantpi:~ $

The prompt tech@plantpi:~ $ means: user tech, on host plantpi, in folder ~ (your home folder, /home/tech), as a normal user ($; root shows #).

If plantpi.local is not found

  • Wait two minutes after first power-on, then try again.
  • Log in to your home router's web page and look at the DHCP client list for plantpi. Then ssh tech@192.168.1.42 (your number will differ).
  • From the laptop, ping plantpi.local. "Could not find host" means mDNS is blocked (common on guest Wi-Fi and some corporate networks).
  • Wi-Fi details wrong? Plug in Ethernet — it needs no settings.

Use a key instead of a password

A password can be guessed. An SSH key pair cannot be, in practice. You keep the private key on your laptop and copy only the public key to the Pi:

# on the laptop (PowerShell or Terminal) — press Enter to accept the defaults
ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "tech-laptop"

# macOS / Linux: copy the public key to the Pi
ssh-copy-id tech@plantpi.local

# Windows PowerShell (no ssh-copy-id): append the key by hand
type $env:USERPROFILE\.ssh\id_ed25519.pub | ssh tech@plantpi.local "mkdir -p ~/.ssh && cat >> ~/.ssh/authorized_keys"

Now ssh tech@plantpi.local logs in without asking for the Pi's password. Never share the private key file (id_ed25519, without .pub).

The Linux commands you will actually use

Linux keeps everything in one tree of folders starting at / (called "root"). Your files live in /home/tech. Settings live in /etc. Logs are managed by the journal. Programs live in /usr/bin. On current Raspberry Pi OS the boot settings file is /boot/firmware/config.txt.

Moving around and reading files

tech@plantpi:~ $ pwd                 # print working directory: where am I?
/home/tech
tech@plantpi:~ $ ls -l               # list, long format
total 8
drwxr-xr-x 2 tech tech 4096 Nov  2 09:20 logger
-rw-r--r-- 1 tech tech  220 Nov  2 09:10 notes.txt
tech@plantpi:~ $ cd logger           # change directory
tech@plantpi:~/logger $ cd ..        # up one level
tech@plantpi:~ $ cd /etc             # absolute path (starts with /)
tech@plantpi:/etc $ cd               # cd alone = back home
tech@plantpi:~ $ cat notes.txt       # print a whole (short) file
Plant room Pi. Sensor on GPIO4.
tech@plantpi:~ $ less /var/log/dpkg.log   # page through a long file: Space = next, / = search, q = quit
tech@plantpi:~ $ nano notes.txt      # edit: Ctrl+O then Enter = save, Ctrl+X = exit

ls -a also shows hidden files (names starting with a dot, such as .ssh). Press Tab to auto-complete file names and ↑ to recall earlier commands — this saves more time than any other habit. Ctrl+C stops a running command.

Admin rights: sudo, and updating

Normal users cannot change system files. sudo ("superuser do") runs one command as root, the all-powerful admin user. Use it only when needed.

tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo apt update          # refresh the list of available packages
Hit:1 http://deb.debian.org/debian ... InRelease
Get:2 http://archive.raspberrypi.com/debian ... InRelease
...
Reading package lists... Done
14 packages can be upgraded. Run 'apt list --upgradable' to see them.
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo apt full-upgrade -y  # install the upgrades
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo apt install -y htop  # install one program
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo reboot               # restart cleanly
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo shutdown -h now      # stop cleanly, wait for the green LED to stop, then unplug

apt update only downloads the list; apt full-upgrade actually installs. Run both, in that order, every time you start work on a Pi that has been sitting for a while.

Services and logs: systemctl and journalctl

A service (also called a daemon) is a program that runs in the background from boot, such as the SSH server. On modern Linux, systemd starts and watches services. You talk to it with systemctl:

tech@plantpi:~ $ systemctl status ssh
● ssh.service - OpenBSD Secure Shell server
     Loaded: loaded (/lib/systemd/system/ssh.service; enabled; preset: enabled)
     Active: active (running) since Mon 2026-11-02 09:14:03 AEDT; 2h 3min ago
       Docs: man:sshd(8)
   Main PID: 655 (sshd)
      Tasks: 1 (limit: 3910)
     CGroup: /system.slice/ssh.service
             └─655 "sshd: /usr/sbin/sshd -D [listener] 0 of 10-100 startups"
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo systemctl restart ssh     # stop + start
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo systemctl enable ssh      # start automatically at boot
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo systemctl disable ssh     # do not start at boot
tech@plantpi:~ $ systemctl --failed             # list anything that crashed

Read it like a tech: Loaded … enabled = it will start at boot; Active: active (running) = it is running now; anything in red such as failed = look at its log. The logs are in the journal:

tech@plantpi:~ $ journalctl -u ssh -n 20        # last 20 lines for one unit
tech@plantpi:~ $ journalctl -u ssh -f           # follow live (Ctrl+C to stop)
tech@plantpi:~ $ journalctl -b -p err           # only errors since this boot
tech@plantpi:~ $ journalctl --since "10 min ago"

How to read ip a: state UP / LOWER_UP means the link is connected; NO-CARRIER on eth0 means no cable (or a dead port at the other end). inet 192.168.1.42/24 is the IPv4 address and subnet size (P19 explains /24). link/ether is the MAC address. In ss -tulpn: t = TCP, u = UDP, l = listening, p = show process (needs sudo), n = numbers not names. Port 5353 is mDNS — that is what makes plantpi.local work.

Network settings on current Raspberry Pi OS are handled by NetworkManager. nmcli dev status lists interfaces, nmcli con show lists saved connections, and sudo nmtui gives a simple menu for Wi-Fi and static addresses. On a site, prefer a DHCP reservation on the router (the router always gives this MAC the same IP) over a static address typed into the Pi.

File permissions, in plain words

    
Every file and folder has an owner (a user), a group, and three sets of permissions: for the owner, for members of the group, and for everyone else ("others"). Each set says whether you can read, write, or execute (run the file; for a folder, enter it).

    

tech@plantpi:~ $ nano backup.sh                 # write a small script
tech@plantpi:~ $ ./backup.sh
-bash: ./backup.sh: Permission denied             # no x bit yet
tech@plantpi:~ $ chmod +x backup.sh             # add execute for everyone (or: chmod 755)
tech@plantpi:~ $ chmod 600 ~/.ssh/authorized_keys   # only I can read/write: SSH insists on this
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo chown tech:tech /home/tech/logger/data   # change owner:group
tech@plantpi:~ $ groups                          # which groups am I in?
tech adm dialout sudo ... gpio i2c spi
tech@plantpi:~ $ sudo usermod -aG dialout tech   # add me to a group (log out and in to take effect)

    
The groups matter for hardware. Members of gpio can use the header pins, i2c and spi the buses, and dialout the serial ports (USB-to-RS-485 adapters for Modbus, P14). "Permission denied" on /dev/ttyUSB0 nearly always means the user is not in dialout.

    
Safety (for your data)
Never "fix" a permission error with chmod 777 or by running everything with sudo. It hides the real problem and lets any program change any file. Find the right owner or group instead. And be very careful with sudo rm -r: Linux has no recycle bin.


    
The 40-pin header

    
All current full-size Pis (and the Zero 2 W, once you solder a header on) share the same 40-pin GPIO header. GPIO means general-purpose input/output: pins your program can switch on and off or read. Some pins also have special jobs (I²C, SPI, UART, PWM). Pins are numbered two ways, and mixing them up is the number one beginner mistake:

    
    
      
  • Physical (board) numbers 1–40: odd numbers on the row nearer the board's centre, pin 1 at the end nearest the SD card slot. Pin 1 has a square solder pad underneath.
    • 
      
  • GPIO (BCM) numbers such as GPIO17: the chip's own names. Python libraries such as gpiozero use these. GPIO17 is on physical pin 11.
    • 
    


    
Safety · 3.3 V only
The Pi's GPIO pins are 3.3 V logic and are NOT 5 V tolerant. Putting 5 V on any GPIO pin — from an Arduino output, a 5 V sensor, or a slip of a probe onto pin 2 or 4 — can permanently kill that pin or the whole SoC, and there is no fuse to save it. Use a level shifter or a resistor divider (e.g. 10 kΩ over 20 kΩ turns 5 V into 3.3 V) for 5 V signals. Each GPIO pin can only source or sink a few milliamps (keep it under ~8 mA; ~16 mA is the absolute limit) — drive relays and motors through a transistor (P08). And never connect the 5 V pins to the 3.3 V pins or to a GPIO.

A first GPIO test from the shell

    
Before Python (P17), prove a pin works with the pinctrl tool that ships with current Raspberry Pi OS. Wire an LED and a 330 Ω resistor in series from physical pin 11 (GPIO17) to pin 9 (GND), long LED leg towards the pin.


tech@plantpi:~ $ pinctrl set 17 op dh    # GPIO17: output, drive high → LED on
tech@plantpi:~ $ pinctrl set 17 dl       # drive low → LED off
tech@plantpi:~ $ pinctrl get 17
17: op dl pd | lo // GPIO17 = output

    
Current check: I = (3.3 − 2.0) / 330 ≈ 3.9 mA for a red LED — comfortably inside the pin's limit. Measure the pin with your meter: about 3.3 V when high, 0 V when low.

On the job
Field technicians log in to Linux boxes far more often than they expect. Many network video recorders, BMS supervisory controllers and IoT gateways, 4G routers and Modbus-to-BACnet gateways run embedded Linux under the hood, and some give you SSH or a similar console for diagnostics. The questions you ask are always the same: is it powered properly, is the disk full, is the service running, what does its log say, what is its IP, can it reach the gateway, and which port is it listening on? That is vcgencmd/df -h/systemctl/journalctl/ip a/ping/ss — this chapter. See C01 BMS fundamentals and C05 CCTV and IP cameras.

    
Bench tip
On a customer's device, read before you change. Run systemctl status, journalctl, df -h and ip a, write down what you see, and only then restart things. A restart can wipe the evidence of what went wrong. And never update or reboot a live building controller without the site's permission.

    
Try this
Unplug the Ethernet cable while running ping 192.168.1.1 (no -c) over a Wi-Fi SSH session in another window, then run ip a — watch eth0 switch to NO-CARRIER. Then deliberately use a cheap thin USB cable and a phone charger, run a CPU load with yes > /dev/null & four times, and check vcgencmd get_throttled. Stop the load with killall yes.


    
Check yourself

    
You need to count pulses from a water meter at up to 5 kHz and also show a web dashboard. Pi, Arduino, or both?
Both, or a microcontroller feeding a Pi. Counting fast pulses needs exact timing that a microcontroller's hardware counter does well; Linux may miss pulses while it schedules other processes. The Pi is ideal for the dashboard and logging. This "small controller + Linux supervisor" split is exactly how BMS systems are built.

    
ssh tech@plantpi.local says "could not resolve hostname", but ssh tech@192.168.1.42 works. What is broken?
Only the name lookup (mDNS). The Pi, its network connection and SSH are all fine. Typical causes: a guest or corporate network that blocks multicast, or a laptop on a different subnet/VLAN. Use the IP or set up a DHCP reservation.

    
What do Loaded: … enabled and Active: inactive (dead) together mean?
The service is set to start at boot (enabled), but it is not running right now — it was stopped, or it exited. Check journalctl -u NAME to see why, then sudo systemctl start NAME.

    
What does -rw-r----- mean, and what is it in octal?
A file (first -). Owner can read and write (rw- = 4+2 = 6). Group can read (r-- = 4). Others have nothing (--- = 0). Octal 640. Typical for a password file that one service group needs to read.

    
Your 5 V Arduino's TX pin must talk to the Pi's RXD (GPIO15). Can you connect them directly?
No. The Arduino drives its TX to 5 V for a logic high, and the Pi's GPIO is not 5 V tolerant. Use a level shifter or a divider such as 10 kΩ (top) and 20 kΩ (bottom): 5 × 20/(10+20) = 3.33 V. The Pi's TX → Arduino RX direction is usually fine because 3.3 V reads as a high on a 5 V Uno.

    
vcgencmd get_throttled returns 0x50000. Is there a problem right now?
Not right now (the low bits are 0), but bits 16 and 18 are set: under-voltage and throttling have occurred since boot. The supply or cable is marginal under load. Fix it before trusting the system.

    
Which command shows whether a web server on the Pi is actually listening on port 8080?
sudo ss -tulpn (or sudo ss -tlnp | grep 8080). If nothing listens on 8080, the problem is the program, not the network. If it listens on 127.0.0.1:8080 only, it is reachable from the Pi itself but not from your laptop.

    
GPIO17 in your Python code is which physical pin, and why does it matter?
Physical pin 11. Libraries like gpiozero use GPIO (BCM) numbers; the header is labelled physically 1–40. Mixing them puts your wire on the wrong pin — at best nothing happens, at worst you short a power pin.


    
Go deeper (optional)
Read man ssh_config and create ~/.ssh/config on your laptop with a Host plantpi entry (HostName, User, IdentityFile) so ssh plantpi is enough. Then turn off password logins on the Pi by setting PasswordAuthentication no in a file under /etc/ssh/sshd_config.d/ and restarting ssh — only after you have proven your key works, or you will lock yourself out. Finally, look at /boot/firmware/config.txt: this is where overlays like dtoverlay=w1-gpio (P17) turn header pins into buses.
