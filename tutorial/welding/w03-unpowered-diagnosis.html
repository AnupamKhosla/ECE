Welding Repair Track · W03

Unpowered diagnosis: inspect, isolate, measure

Build a repeatable cold-test routine for a donor welder. Record evidence, separate parallel paths and establish what must be investigated before replacement parts are energised.

Study + exercises 2–3 hours with a mentorLevel W01, W02 and verified safe accessContext India · family repair shop
Entry condition

Inside-machine work in this chapter starts only after your repair mentor has isolated the machine, followed the exact stored-energy procedure and verified de-energisation. “Cold test” describes the test state; it does not prove that a machine is safe to touch. Re-establish safe conditions after any power-up.

Intake: make the complaint testable

Ask when the fault occurs: on switching on, when striking an arc, after warming up, or only on one supply or extension lead. Record mode, current setting, electrode, lead arrangement, fan/display/indicator behaviour, recent impact or moisture and any previous repair. Ask whether a fuse, MCB or RCD operated; those are different observations. Do not repeatedly reconnect a machine that trips.

Attach a job number and list all supplied leads. Use R04. Photograph the original wiring, connectors, insulating pads and mounting hardware before disconnecting anything. Label both ends of a connector.

The cold-test sequence

  1. Identify the actual circuit. Match board revision to the diagram. Mark bridge +/−/~ terminals, each switch’s G/D/S or G/C/E and the output rectifier configuration from datasheets.

  2. Check the input protection path. Test the disconnected fuse for continuity; inspect the specified fuse type, surge protection, series inrush element and relay. Do not bypass an open limiter. A disconnected cold NTC has a model-specific resistance, not a universal “good” value.

  3. Screen for a bus short. With voltage verified absent, observe resistance across the designated bus nodes in both polarities. Capacitor charging by the meter can make the reading rise. A persistent low value is a lead for investigation, not a universal resistance threshold.

  4. Test the bridge. Perform W02’s eight junction readings. Record whether each measurement is in circuit or isolated. If an abnormal result follows the bridge after isolation, the bridge itself is implicated.

  5. Screen all power switches. Compare corresponding devices with their gates safely held off according to their datasheets. Check D–S or C–E both ways and gate-to-power terminals. The ordinary MOSFET’s body diode and an IGBT’s optional co-pack diode are different; use W04.

  6. Test output diodes and inspect the load path. Account for transformer windings and parallel rectifiers. Check the socket, cable and work-clamp connections. Do not use the meter’s current socket across welding terminals.

  7. Examine nearby causes. Gate resistors, driver transistors/ICs, pull-downs, clamps, snubbers, current sensing, cooling and capacitor condition all matter. Note the next test needed for each unresolved item.

Write useful measurements

Poor noteUseful note
“Bridge bad”BR1 removed from circuit; red A, black +: 0.006 V; reverse: 0.007 V. Other three junctions each forward 0.56–0.60 V and reverse OL. Meter diode mode.
“IGBT okay”Q1 gate held off, isolated: no hard C–E short; G–E and G–C show no meter-detectable low-resistance path. Dynamic switching and blocking not verified.
“Capacitor okay”C7 disconnected and discharged: capacitance recorded at meter’s test frequency; ESR compared with the correct series/specification. Rated-voltage leakage not tested.
“Earth fine”Visual earth-bond inspection performed only. Protective-conductor test pending with specified instrument and procedure; continuity beep alone is insufficient.

Worked example: an input bridge failure

Training example, invented readings. A machine trips its branch MCB immediately at turn-on. After safe isolation, BR1’s A-to-+ pair reads near zero both ways in circuit. Removing BR1 gives the same reading. The downstream capacitor bank no longer shows a persistent hard short on the mentor’s designated cold test.

Confirmed: one junction of BR1 is shorted. Not confirmed: why it failed, whether the IGBTs behave correctly at operating voltage, and whether the inrush relay has been bypassed. The repair plan includes the matching bridge specification, inrush circuit, bank condition, switch screening and thermal mounting.

Suppose inspection finds an earlier repair has soldered across the inrush resistor. That is evidence of a plausible stress mechanism. Restore the original specified circuit, investigate the reason for the bypass and have the mentor follow the service manual’s controlled recommissioning. Only model-specific electrical and load tests close the job; clearing one ohmmeter short does not.

Why cold tests have limits

Resistance and diode mode use small voltages and currents. They find many shorts and opens, but they cannot reproduce operating voltage, heat, gate timing or switching transients. A normal-looking capacitor may have high ESR; a normal diode may leak when hot; a transistor may fail to block hundreds of volts. When a reading depends on test voltage or on another circuit branch, write that limitation.

The fuse is open and a power switch is shorted. Which do you replace first?

Neither is ready for immediate re-energisation. Document both failures, test the rest of that switch group and its gate-driver/protection neighbourhood, check downstream faults, then plan the complete repair and controlled retest. Fit only the specified fuse after the fault is addressed.

Skills checklist
