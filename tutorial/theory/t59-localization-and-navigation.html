Wheel odometry, localization and mobile navigation

A wheel measures rotation. An odometry estimate integrates motion. A localizer compares current evidence with a map. Each result has its own frame and failure modes.

Time 40 minutesBefore this T31 · T33

Calculate centre travel and heading change

For a differential-drive model, let L and R be signed wheel travel in metres over the same interval and b be the effective track width in metres. Forward centre travel is their average. The distance difference is the angle turned multiplied by the track width. Right travel greater than left travel turns the body anticlockwise in the convention x forward, y left, z up.

s = (R + L) / 2   ·   Δθ = (R − L) / b

If the robot is at heading θ, for straight travel add s cos θ to x and s sin θ to y. Under constant curvature for a nonzero turn, the centre follows an arc of radius s/Δθ. Therefore Δx = (s/Δθ)[sin(θ+Δθ) − sin θ] and Δy = (s/Δθ)[cos θ − cos(θ+Δθ)]. Turn in place gives s = 0. For short samples a midpoint heading θ + Δθ/2 approximates the arc. Use signed cumulative count differences with rollover handled in the counter’s numeric range.

Use continuous local motion and global corrections

In the common ROS frame tree, odom → base_link provides smooth short-term motion and may drift. map → odom corrects that drift relative to the map and may change abruptly. An observation from a lidar or camera needs a transform from its frame at the observation time. The base-to-sensor mount is typically static. A transform from the wrong instant gives especially visible offsets during turns.

Wheel scale error accumulates with distance. Unequal rolling radii produce drift in yaw during straight travel. An IMU can help estimate yaw rate, but gyro bias integrates into heading drift. Two estimates from the same encoders share errors. An estimator that treats them as independent can become overconfident. With a known map, a localizer such as AMCL compares laser observations with candidate poses. SLAM estimates both a map and robot poses when the map is being built. Revisiting a known place can correct drift accumulated earlier.

Distinguish an unreachable goal from poor tracking

A global planner looks for a route through traversable space. A controller follows it while accounting for current motion and nearby obstacles. A costmap describes occupied areas and clearance costs. Use the actual footprint including payload. Compare goal acceptance, global path, velocity commands, odometry and stopping evidence in that order. A valid global path can still fail if the local path is blocked, the wheel scale is wrong or the drive stops receiving fresh commands.
